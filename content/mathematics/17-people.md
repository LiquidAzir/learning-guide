## How to use this chapter

Mathematics carries names the way few subjects do: Pythagoras's theorem, Euclid's algorithm, Newton's method, Gaussian elimination, Bayes' theorem. This roster is organized by era; each entry gives the central contribution and the chapter where it appears. Where credit is shared or contested, the entry says so. Two prizes are noted: the Fields Medal, awarded every four years since 1936 to mathematicians under forty, and the Abel Prize, awarded annually since 2003 for lifetime achievement; there is no Nobel Prize in mathematics.

## Antiquity (to 500 CE)

**Pythagoras** (c. 570–495 BCE). The theorem bears his name though the Babylonians knew the fact a millennium earlier; his school discovered irrational numbers and made number the center of a philosophy. Chapters 2, 3, 5.

**Euclid** (fl. 300 BCE). *Elements*: geometry and number theory derived from five axioms, the model of proof for two thousand years; the algorithm for the greatest common divisor; the proof that primes are infinite. Chapters 3, 5, 12, 13.

**Archimedes** (c. 287–212 BCE). Areas and volumes by exhaustion, anticipating the integral; $\pi$ to two decimal places; levers, buoyancy, and enormous numbers. The greatest mathematician of antiquity. Chapters 3, 5, 8.

**Eratosthenes** (c. 276–194 BCE). Measured the Earth's circumference from shadows at two cities to within a few percent; the sieve for finding primes. Chapters 5, 13.

**Diophantus** (fl. c. 250 CE). Equations in whole numbers, now called Diophantine; the book in whose margin Fermat wrote his note. Chapter 13.

## The middle period (500–1500)

**Brahmagupta** (598–668). Rules for zero and negative numbers; the general solution of the quadratic; a formula for cyclic quadrilaterals. The first to treat zero as a number. Chapters 2, 3.

**Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi** (c. 780–850). *Al-jabr*: the systematic solution of linear and quadratic equations, in words, giving algebra its name; his own name gave us "algorithm." Chapters 3, 4, 12.

**Omar Khayyam** (1048–1131). Classified and geometrically solved cubic equations; also the poet of the *Rubaiyat*. Chapter 3.

**Fibonacci** (Leonardo of Pisa, c. 1170–1250). *Liber Abaci* (1202) brought Indian numerals and Arabic algebra to Europe; the rabbit sequence was a minor exercise. Chapter 3.

**Madhava of Sangamagrama** (c. 1340–1425). Infinite series for sine, cosine, and $\pi$, three centuries before Newton and Gregory. Chapters 3, 8.

## The Renaissance and the seventeenth century

**Gerolamo Cardano** (1501–1576). Published the solution of the cubic and quartic in *Ars Magna* (1545), after a contested oath to Tartaglia; the first to work with square roots of negative numbers; an early writer on probability. Chapters 3, 4, 9.

**François Viète** (1540–1603). Introduced letters for unknowns and constants: the beginning of symbolic algebra. Chapter 3.

**John Napier** (1550–1617). Logarithms (1614), which turned multiplication into addition for 350 years. Chapters 3, 4.

**René Descartes** (1596–1650). Coordinates (1637): curves as equations. The notation $x, y, z$ and superscript powers. Chapters 3, 5, 6.

**Pierre de Fermat** (1607–1665). Coordinates independently of Descartes; number theory, including the little theorem and the marginal note that became the Last Theorem; with Pascal, the founding of probability. Chapters 9, 13.

**Blaise Pascal** (1623–1662). The 1654 correspondence that founded probability; the triangle of binomial coefficients (known earlier in China and Persia); a calculating machine. Chapter 9.

**Isaac Newton** (1642–1727). Calculus (1665–66), which he used to derive planetary motion from gravity; the binomial series; the method for solving equations that bears his name. Chapters 3, 7, 8, 15.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646–1716). Calculus independently (1675), published first (1684), with the notation $dy/dx$ and $\int$ still in use; binary arithmetic; a mechanical calculator. Chapters 3, 7, 8.

**Jacob Bernoulli** (1655–1705). The law of large numbers; the number $e$ from compound interest; the calculus of the family that produced eight mathematicians. Chapters 6, 9.

## The eighteenth century

**Leonhard Euler** (1707–1783). The most prolific mathematician in history: the notation $f(x)$, $e$, $i$, $\pi$, $\Sigma$; the formula $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$; graph theory from the Königsberg bridges; the generalization of Fermat's little theorem behind RSA; and a large fraction of everything else in this guide. Blind for his last seventeen years and more productive than ever. Chapters 6, 8, 12, 13.

**Thomas Bayes** (1701–1761). The theorem for reversing conditional probabilities, published posthumously in 1763. Chapter 9.

**Pierre-Simon Laplace** (1749–1827). The central limit theorem; probability as "common sense reduced to calculation"; celestial mechanics; the rediscovery and generalization of Bayes. Chapters 9, 10.

**Joseph Fourier** (1768–1830). Every periodic signal as a sum of sines (1807), the basis of signal processing; also the first to describe the greenhouse effect. Chapter 6.

## The nineteenth century

**Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777–1855). Number theory (*Disquisitiones*, 1801), the fundamental theorem of algebra, the normal distribution and least squares, the prime number conjecture, non-Euclidean geometry (unpublished), and the elimination method named for him. Called the prince of mathematicians. Chapters 4, 5, 10, 11, 13.

**Sophie Germain** (1776–1831). Number theory and the theory of elasticity, working under a male pseudonym to correspond with Gauss; a key step toward Fermat's Last Theorem for a class of exponents. Chapter 13.

**Augustin-Louis Cauchy** (1789–1857). The rigorous definition of limit and the foundations of analysis, answering Berkeley's objection after a century. Chapter 7.

**Nikolai Lobachevsky** (1792–1856) and **János Bolyai** (1802–1860). Independently, hyperbolic geometry (1829, 1832): Euclid's parallel postulate can be denied without contradiction. Chapter 5.

**Évariste Galois** (1811–1832). Group theory and the proof that the quintic has no formula, summarized in a letter written the night before the duel that killed him at twenty. Chapter 16.

**George Boole** (1815–1864). Logic as algebra (1854), the mathematics inside every computer. Chapter 12.

**Bernhard Riemann** (1826–1866). Curved geometry of any dimension (1854), which Einstein used; the zeta function and the Riemann Hypothesis (1859); the rigorous integral. Chapters 5, 8, 13.

**Georg Cantor** (1845–1918). Set theory and the discovery that infinities differ in size (1874); ridiculed in his lifetime, foundational after it. Chapter 12.

**Francis Galton** (1822–1911) and **Karl Pearson** (1857–1936). Regression and correlation from studies of heredity; Pearson formalized them and founded mathematical statistics. Galton's enthusiasm for eugenics is part of the record. Chapter 10.

## The twentieth century

**David Hilbert** (1862–1943). The 23 problems of 1900; the program to found mathematics on secure axioms, which Gödel showed impossible; infinite-dimensional spaces named for him. Chapters 12, 16.

**Emmy Noether** (1882–1935). Abstract algebra rebuilt around structures; the theorem linking symmetries to conservation laws in physics. Denied a paid post for most of her career because she was a woman; dismissed by the Nazis in 1933. Chapter 16.

**Srinivasa Ramanujan** (1887–1920). Self-taught in India, produced thousands of formulas in number theory and infinite series, many still being proved; brought to Cambridge by Hardy in 1914. Chapter 13.

**Ronald Fisher** (1890–1962). The randomized experiment, analysis of variance, maximum likelihood, and the p-value convention of 0.05; also a major figure in genetics and, less creditably, eugenics. Chapter 10.

**John von Neumann** (1903–1957). Axiomatized quantum mechanics and set theory, founded game theory, designed the architecture of the modern computer, and implemented the Monte Carlo method. Chapters 12, 15.

**Kurt Gödel** (1906–1978). Incompleteness (1931): any consistent system containing arithmetic has true statements it cannot prove. Chapter 12.

**Alan Turing** (1912–1954). Defined computation (1936), proved the halting problem undecidable, broke Enigma, and proposed the test for machine intelligence. Prosecuted for homosexuality in 1952; pardoned posthumously in 2013. Chapters 12, 15.

**Claude Shannon** (1916–2001). Boolean algebra realized in circuits (1938); information theory (1948), which defined the bit. Chapter 12.

**Andrey Kolmogorov** (1903–1987). The axioms of probability (1933); turbulence; complexity theory. Chapter 9.

**Alexander Grothendieck** (1928–2014). Rebuilt algebraic geometry from its foundations in the 1960s; Fields 1966; withdrew from mathematics and the world in later life. Chapter 16.

**Paul Erdős** (1913–1996). Some 1,500 papers with over 500 collaborators, mostly in combinatorics and number theory; the open problems he left, now catalogued online, are where AI systems made their first contributions. Chapters 12, 16.

**John Nash** (1928–2015). Nash equilibrium in game theory; embedding theorems in geometry. [Chapter 7 of the economics guide](#/economics/game-theory).

**Stephen Cook** (b. 1939). Formulated P versus NP (1971). Chapters 12, 16.

## The present

**Andrew Wiles** (b. 1953). Fermat's Last Theorem (1995), after seven years of secret work and one repaired gap. Abel Prize 2016. Chapter 13.

**Grigori Perelman** (b. 1966). The Poincaré conjecture (2002–03), posted online without journal submission; declined the Fields Medal and the Clay prize. Chapter 16.

**Terence Tao** (b. 1975). Contributions across analysis, combinatorics, and number theory; Fields 2006; co-author of the Marton conjecture proof and leader of its Lean formalization and of the Equational Theories Project; the profession's most careful public voice on AI and mathematics. Chapter 16.

**Yitang Zhang** (b. 1955). Bounded gaps between primes (2013), proved while an unknown lecturer. Chapter 13.

**Maryna Viazovska** (b. 1984). Sphere packing in dimensions 8 and 24 (2016); Fields 2022, the second woman to receive it. Chapter 16.

**James Maynard** (b. 1987). Small gaps between primes and the structure of primes; Fields 2022. Chapter 13.

**Michel Talagrand** (b. 1952). Concentration of measure: why quantities depending on many random inputs are predictable. Abel Prize 2024. Chapter 9.

**Hong Wang** (b. 1991). With Joshua Zahl, the Kakeya conjecture in three dimensions (2025); Fields 2026, the third woman to receive it. Chapter 16.

**Yu Deng**. Rigorous derivation of the Boltzmann equation from particle dynamics; with Hani and Ma, a resolution of Hilbert's sixth problem for fluids (2025, preliminary); Fields 2026. Chapters 8, 16.

**Gerd Faltings** (b. 1954). The Mordell conjecture (1983): curves of high genus have finitely many rational points. Abel Prize 2026. Chapter 13.

**David Smith**. A retired print technician and hobbyist who in 2022 found the "hat," the first aperiodic monotile, proved with Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss in 2023. Chapter 16.

**Peter Scholze** (b. 1987). Condensed mathematics and perfectoid spaces; Fields 2018; the Liquid Tensor Experiment that made formal verification a research tool. Chapter 16.

**Dennis Gaitsgory** (b. 1973) and **Sam Raskin**. Led the nine-author proof of the geometric Langlands conjecture (2024). Chapter 16.

## The pattern

Three things stand out in this list. The first is how often the decisive idea came from someone young or outside the establishment: Galois at twenty, Gödel at twenty-four, Ramanujan self-taught, Zhang an unknown lecturer, Smith a hobbyist. The second is how the subject's center moved: Babylon and Egypt, then Greece, then India and the Islamic world, then Italy, France, Germany, and now everywhere, with the recent Fields medalists born in Ukraine, China, Iran, India, and Britain, or raised in Korea. The third is who was kept out: Germain needed a man's name to be read, Noether was refused a salary, Turing was prosecuted, and only three women have won the Fields Medal in ninety years. Mathematics is the most universal of subjects and has been practiced by the least universal set of people, and the gap has been closing only recently.
