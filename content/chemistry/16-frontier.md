## Recap

You have the map: atoms, the table, bonds, states, reactions, energy, rates, equilibrium, and the three great territories of carbon, life, and materials, plus the instruments that make it all knowable. This chapter walks the edge. Chemistry's frontier looks different from physics': fewer deep mysteries about the laws, more urgent problems about what to build with them. Each section states a problem, why it is hard, and where things stand.

## Making chemistry sustainable

The chemical industry was built on cheap fossil carbon as both fuel and raw material, and it is responsible for a large share of industrial emissions. Redesigning it is the field's central practical challenge. Paul Anastas and John Warner set out twelve principles of **green chemistry** in 1998: prevent waste rather than clean it up, use catalysts rather than stoichiometric reagents, use renewable feedstocks, design products to degrade.[^1] Three problems dominate.

**Nitrogen.** Haber–Bosch (chapter 10) uses one to two percent of world energy and its hydrogen comes from natural gas. The goal is ammonia from air, water, and electricity at ordinary pressure. Electrochemical routes using lithium as an intermediary now send about 61 percent of their electrons into ammonia rather than into side reactions, though only about 13 percent of the electrical energy ends up stored in the product.[^2] The bacterial enzyme nitrogenase, which does the whole job at room temperature, is still not understood well enough to copy.

**Carbon.** Two tasks: stop emitting it, and pull some back. Cement's chemistry emits carbon dioxide by definition (chapter 14), and low-carbon cements are a materials problem. Capturing carbon dioxide from the air is a chemistry problem: the gas is well under a twentieth of a percent of the air, so a capture material must bind it selectively out of a vast excess of nitrogen and oxygen, then release it cheaply. Amine-lined frameworks that work in humid outdoor air and regenerate at 60 °C appeared in 2024, but cost projections suggest direct air capture will remain expensive enough that it complements rather than replaces emission cuts.[^3]

**Hydrogen.** Green hydrogen means splitting water with renewable electricity, and the best catalysts for the hard half of that reaction use iridium, one of the rarest elements on Earth. Replacing it with manganese, cobalt, or nickel oxides that survive in acid is a live race, with stable cheap-metal catalysts reported in 2023 and 2024 (see Latest Research).

## Plastics and forever chemicals

Chemistry's most visible legacy problem is the 400 million tonnes of plastic made each year, designed to last and lasting. Mechanical recycling degrades polymers; the frontier is **chemical recycling**, breaking chains back to monomers that can be re-polymerized as new. Enzymes engineered by machine learning depolymerize PET bottles in days, and chemical routes are being scaled. The harder problem is design: making polymers that are durable in use and degradable on command.

**PFAS**, the per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances in nonstick coatings and firefighting foam, resist everything because the carbon–fluorine bond is the strongest single bond carbon makes. They are now detectable in the blood of almost everyone tested.[^16] In 2022 chemists found that a common class of them falls apart in warm sodium hydroxide, ordinary household lye, and a mild solvent; in 2025 a ball-milling method broke them down with no solvent at all and recovered the fluorine for reuse.[^4] Destruction at scale, and safer replacements, remain open.

## Computation and machines

For most of its history chemistry was too complicated to compute. The Schrödinger equation for a molecule of any size cannot be solved exactly. **Density functional theory**, for which Walter Kohn shared the 1998 Nobel Prize, made useful approximations tractable, and it is now run millions of times a day to predict structures, energies, and spectra before anyone enters a laboratory.[^5] Machine learning has since learned to imitate those calculations a thousand times faster.

The results of the last five years are genuine and mixed. AlphaFold solved protein structure prediction (chapter 13). Language-model agents have planned and run real experiments on robotic equipment.[^6] But the flagship claim of AI materials discovery, millions of new stable crystals and dozens synthesized by a robot, did not survive independent scrutiny: re-analysis found the "new" compounds were mostly known materials or disordered variants, and the synthesis paper was corrected (chapter 14).[^7] The lesson chemists have drawn is not that the tools fail but that verification cannot be automated away. **Self-driving laboratories**, robots that design, run, and analyze experiments in a loop, are real and spreading; the question is what they should be pointed at.

## Synthesis

Organic synthesis can make almost any stable molecule given enough time. The frontier is doing it efficiently and doing it late. C–H activation and skeletal editing (chapter 12) are where synthesis is heading: editing a specific bond, or deleting and swapping a single atom in a ring, so a drug candidate can be adjusted without rebuilding it from scratch.[^8] **Photoredox** and **electrochemical** methods use light and electricity in place of harsh reagents. **Biocatalysis** uses engineered enzymes for steps that ordinary chemistry does badly, whether bred by directed evolution (chapter 10) or designed from scratch, which the 2024 Nobel Prize recognized. **Molecular machines**, rotors and shuttles and elevators built from single molecules and driven by light or chemical fuel, won the 2016 Nobel Prize; making them do useful work is the next step.[^9] The number of drug-like molecules that could exist is around $10^{60}$; deciding which to make is now the bottleneck, not making them.

## Fundamentals still open

Chemistry has fewer unsolved laws than physics, but it has real gaps.

- **The end of the periodic table.** Whether elements beyond 118 can be made, whether an island of stability exists, and whether relativistic effects break the table's logic (chapter 4).
- **What a bond is.** Chemists use the concept daily and quantum mechanics offers several inequivalent definitions (chapter 6). Imaging bonds one molecule at a time has sharpened the pictures, not settled the question.
- **Water.** The most studied liquid is not fully understood; whether supercooled water has a second critical point is unresolved (chapter 7).
- **Nitrogenase, Rubisco, and other enzymes** do at room temperature what industry does at hundreds of degrees, by mechanisms still being worked out.
- **Quantum effects in biology.** Tunneling is established in some enzymes; whether photosynthesis exploits quantum coherence, as was claimed around 2007, has largely been answered no at biological temperatures, a useful case of a striking claim receding under scrutiny.[^10]
- **Watching electrons move.** Attosecond pulses (Nobel Prize in Physics 2023) now catch electrons during a bond's first femtoseconds; a full movie of a chemical reaction at the electron level is close but not done.

## Where life came from

Chapter 13 left the question open: the building blocks form easily, but no one has made a self-sustaining, self-copying chemical system from scratch. Recent progress is real. Plausible prebiotic routes now exist for nucleotides, for RNA strands that load amino acids and link them into peptides, and for the sulfur chemistry that may have powered it.[^11] Samples returned from asteroids Ryugu (2020) and Bennu (2023) contained amino acids, all five nucleobases, and evidence of ancient salty water, so the ingredients were widespread in the early solar system, and the James Webb Space Telescope finds the same ices in clouds where stars have not yet formed.[^12] What is missing is the step from chemistry to biology, and it may be the single most important open question chemistry has.

## Health

Chemistry's medical frontier is molecular precision. **mRNA** medicines, enabled by a nucleoside modification, moved from vaccines toward cancer treatments. **Gene editing** moved from cutting DNA to rewriting single letters: the first CRISPR therapy was approved in 2023, and in 2025 a baby with a fatal metabolic disorder received a base-editing treatment designed for him alone in six months.[^13] **Designed proteins** are in clinical trials. Against all this stands antibiotic resistance, a chemical arms race in which bacteria evolve enzymes to destroy each new drug. Genuinely new classes of antibiotic are rare, though not absent: teixobactin was reported in 2015 and darobactin in 2019, and a new class for drug-resistant *Acinetobacter* entered trials in 2024.[^15]

## How to read a chemistry headline

Chemistry's failures of the last fifteen years have a pattern, and they suggest a checklist.

1. **Has anyone else made it?** The claim of arsenic-based life (2010) collapsed when others could not reproduce it and was formally retracted in 2025.[^14] The AI materials claims of 2023 shrank when others re-analyzed the data. One laboratory is a claim; replication is a result.
2. **Was the characterization independent?** A new material is only new if its identity was established by methods that do not assume the answer. Automated fitting of X-ray patterns to expected structures is where the 2023 claims went wrong.
3. **Detected is not dangerous, and promising is not deployed.** Parts-per-trillion detection of a pollutant is not a dose; a catalyst that works for a hundred hours in a lab is not a plant.
4. **Follow the efficiency, not the record.** Solar cells, batteries, and catalysts are reported by their best number. The numbers that matter are cost, stability, and scale.
5. **Check the Latest Research list**, where each entry carries a status of confirmed, preliminary, disputed, or retracted.

:::key
Chemistry's frontier is less about discovering new laws than about learning to build, at scale and without harm, with the laws already known. That makes it the science most directly responsible for whether the next century goes well. The tools have never been better. The judgment about what to build with them is still, and should be, human.
:::

## Summary

- Sustainability: green ammonia, carbon capture, cheap-metal water splitting, low-carbon cement, and the green chemistry principles.
- Plastics need chemical recycling and designed degradability; PFAS destruction is now possible in the laboratory.
- Computation is transformative (density functional theory, AlphaFold, self-driving labs) but the AI materials episode showed verification cannot be skipped.
- Synthesis is moving to late-stage editing, light and electricity, enzymes, and molecular machines.
- Open fundamentals: the table's end, the definition of a bond, water, enzyme mechanisms, the origin of life.
- Read headlines by replication, independent characterization, and cost and scale.
