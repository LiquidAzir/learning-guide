## Recap

Bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule. But a glass of water is not one molecule; it is $10^{25}$ of them, and what makes it a liquid rather than a gas is a different, weaker set of attractions *between* molecules. This chapter is about those forces and the states of matter they produce.

## The forces between molecules

**Intermolecular forces** are ten to a hundred times weaker than chemical bonds, which is exactly why they matter: they are weak enough to break with a little heat, so they govern melting, boiling, dissolving, and the folding of proteins. There are three kinds that matter.

**Dispersion forces**, worth anywhere from a fraction of a kilojoule per mole to about 40, act between all molecules, even ones with no charge separation at all. The electrons in any molecule are in constant motion, so at any instant one side is slightly more negative than the other. That fleeting dipole induces an opposite dipole in a neighbor, and the two attract. Fritz London explained the effect with quantum mechanics in 1930, and it is often called the **London force**.[^1] It grows with the size of the molecule (more electrons, more sloshing), which is why methane (one carbon) is a gas, octane (eight carbons) is a liquid, and candle wax (twenty-plus) is a solid. It is also why the noble gases, which have no bonds at all, can be liquefied if cooled enough: helium at 4 K, xenon at 165 K.

**Dipole–dipole forces**, typically 5 to 25 kJ/mol, act between polar molecules (chapter 6), whose permanent positive and negative ends line up.

**Hydrogen bonds** are the strongest of the three: a special case in which a hydrogen atom bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine) is left so electron-poor that it is attracted to a lone pair on a neighboring molecule.[^2] A hydrogen bond is worth about 20 kJ/mol, a tenth of a covalent bond, and it is the reason for almost everything unusual about water, and for the double helix.

## Water is strange

Compare water with its neighbors in the periodic table. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, the same bent two-hydrogen molecule with sulfur in place of oxygen, boils at −60 °C. Water boils at +100 °C. Every one of water's peculiarities traces to its hydrogen bonds, each molecule making up to four with its neighbors in a constantly shifting network.[^12]

- **It has an extraordinarily high boiling point** for so small a molecule, because boiling has to break all those hydrogen bonds.
- **Ice floats.** In almost every substance the solid is denser than the liquid. Ice is less dense because hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open hexagonal lattice with more empty space than the jostling liquid. Water is densest at 4 °C. This is why lakes freeze from the top down and fish survive winter.
- **It holds heat.** Water's heat capacity is among the highest of any liquid, because warming it means breaking hydrogen bonds as well as speeding up molecules. Oceans buffer the planet's temperature for this reason.
- **It has high surface tension**, enough for insects to walk on, and it climbs narrow tubes, which is part of how trees lift water.
- **It dissolves salts and sugars** because its dipoles wrap around ions and hydrogen-bond to anything with an O–H or N–H group. It will not dissolve oil, which has neither.

Life, which is water-based chemistry, depends on every item on this list.

## Gases: the simplest state

In a gas the molecules are far apart, moving fast, and the intermolecular forces barely matter. That makes gases the one state with a simple, universal law. Robert Boyle found in 1662 that at fixed temperature, pressure times volume is constant.[^3] Jacques Charles and Joseph Gay-Lussac found around 1800 that volume grows in proportion to absolute temperature. Avogadro added that volume is proportional to the number of molecules. Combine them:

$$PV = nRT$$

:::math The ideal gas law, read aloud
$P$ is pressure, $V$ volume, $n$ the number of moles, $T$ the temperature in kelvin, and $R$ the gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K), the same $R$ that appears throughout thermodynamics. Read it as: the pressure a gas exerts, times the room it fills, is set by how many molecules there are and how hot they are, and by nothing else. Not by what gas it is. A mole of helium and a mole of carbon dioxide at the same temperature and pressure fill the same volume, about 24 liters at room conditions. The equation follows from picturing molecules as tiny billiard balls whose collisions with the walls are the pressure, the kinetic theory described in the [physics guide](#/physics/heat).
:::

Real gases deviate when they are squeezed close together or cooled toward liquefying, because molecules do have size and do attract one another. Johannes van der Waals corrected the law in 1873 with two adjustable constants, one for molecular volume and one for attraction, and his equation predicts that every gas has a **critical temperature** above which no pressure can liquefy it.[^4] That prediction is what let Kamerlingh Onnes liquefy helium in 1908 and discover superconductivity three years later.

## Liquids

Liquids are the awkward middle: molecules close enough to feel one another but free to slide. Their properties are the intermolecular forces made visible. **Viscosity**, resistance to flow, rises with stronger attractions and longer molecules (honey, motor oil). **Surface tension** is the energy cost of the surface, where molecules lack neighbors on one side. **Vapor pressure** is the pressure of molecules that have escaped into the space above the liquid; it rises with temperature, and a liquid **boils** when its vapor pressure reaches the surrounding pressure. That is why water boils at 70 °C on Everest and pasta takes forever, and why a pressure cooker, by raising the pressure, cooks faster.

## Solids

In a solid the molecules or ions are locked in place. **Crystalline** solids have a repeating three-dimensional pattern, a **lattice**; **amorphous** solids like glass are frozen disorder. The lattice can be probed with X-rays, which have wavelengths comparable to the spacing between atoms and diffract off the regular planes. William Lawrence Bragg worked out in 1912 how the angles of the diffracted beams reveal the spacings, and he and his father William Henry Bragg turned it into a working method the next year. They shared the 1915 Nobel Prize; Lawrence was 25, still the youngest science laureate.[^5] **X-ray crystallography** has since determined the structures of salt, diamond, penicillin, DNA, and hundreds of thousands of proteins (chapter 15).

Solids are classified by what holds them together.

| Type | Held by | Examples | Properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Coulomb attraction of ions | NaCl, CaCO₃ | Hard, brittle, high melting, insulating |
| Molecular | Intermolecular forces | Ice, sugar, dry ice | Soft, low melting |
| Covalent network | Covalent bonds throughout | Diamond, quartz | Very hard, very high melting |
| Metallic | Electron sea | Iron, copper, gold | Conductive, malleable |

The same element can form different solids. Carbon as **diamond** is a three-dimensional network of tetrahedral sp³ carbons, the hardest natural material. Carbon as **graphite** is stacked flat sheets of sp² carbons, soft and slippery because the sheets slide, and conductive because the delocalized electrons roam within each sheet. A single sheet is **graphene**, isolated in 2004 with adhesive tape and a Nobel Prize in 2010; a sheet rolled into a tube is a nanotube; sixty carbons closed into a soccer ball is **buckminsterfullerene**, discovered by accident in 1985.[^6] These are **allotropes**, and they are why "carbon" is not one material but a family.

:::story
Different crystal packings of the same molecule, **polymorphs**, can have different solubilities and melting points, and in pharmaceuticals that is a matter of life and money. In 1998 the HIV drug ritonavir, already on the market, began spontaneously converting to a previously unknown, less soluble crystal form that the body could not absorb. The manufacturer had to pull the drug and reformulate it at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars.[^7] Drug companies now screen exhaustively for polymorphs before launch.
:::

## Phase diagrams

A **phase diagram** maps which state a substance takes at each combination of temperature and pressure.

{{fig:phase-diagram|The phase diagram of water. Lines mark where two phases coexist. At the triple point all three coexist; beyond the critical point liquid and gas become indistinguishable. Water's solid–liquid line leans left because ice is less dense than water.}}

Three features matter. The lines are where two phases coexist: crossing the liquid–gas line is boiling. The **triple point** is the one temperature and pressure where solid, liquid, and gas coexist; for water it is 0.01 °C and 0.006 atmospheres, and it was used to define the kelvin until 2019. The **critical point** is where the liquid–gas line simply ends: above 374 °C and 218 atmospheres,[^12] water is a **supercritical fluid**, neither liquid nor gas, with the density of one and the freedom of the other. Supercritical carbon dioxide, reached at a mild 31 °C, is used to decaffeinate coffee and dry-clean clothes because it dissolves like a solvent and then evaporates without a trace.

Water's solid–liquid line leans the wrong way: raising the pressure on ice *melts* it, a direct consequence of ice being less dense than water. Almost no other substance does this.

## Solutions

A **solution** is a uniform mixture, one substance (the **solute**) dispersed molecule by molecule through another (the **solvent**). Dissolving is a competition. To dissolve salt in water you must pull ions out of their lattice (costing the lattice energy) and surround each with water dipoles (releasing **hydration energy**). Salt dissolves because the two nearly cancel and the resulting disorder is favorable (chapter 9). The rule of thumb **like dissolves like** follows: polar solvents dissolve polar and ionic solutes; nonpolar solvents (oil, hexane) dissolve nonpolar solutes (grease, wax). Soap works by being both, a polar head that likes water on a nonpolar tail that likes grease.

Gases dissolve too, in proportion to their pressure (**Henry's law**, 1803).[^8] That is why soda is bottled under pressure and goes flat when opened, and why divers who surface too fast suffer the bends: nitrogen dissolved in their blood at depth bubbles out as the pressure drops.

Dissolved particles change a solvent's properties in ways that depend only on how many there are, not what they are. These **colligative properties** include lowering the freezing point (salt on icy roads, antifreeze in radiators), raising the boiling point, and **osmosis**, the flow of solvent through a membrane toward the side with more solute. Jacobus van 't Hoff showed in 1887 that the osmotic pressure obeys an equation identical in form to the ideal gas law, work that earned the first Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1901.[^9] Osmosis is how your cells stay inflated, why salt water dehydrates you, and how kidneys and reverse-osmosis plants work.

:::frontier
Water is the most studied liquid on Earth and still not understood. Cooled below freezing without crystallizing (possible in tiny droplets or fast cooling), it behaves increasingly oddly, and a leading hypothesis holds that deeply supercooled water separates into two distinct liquids of different density, with a second critical point hidden below −40 °C.[^10] Simulations support this; direct experiments are extraordinarily hard because the water freezes first. Meanwhile at least twenty crystalline forms of ice are known, several discovered in the last decade, and a form of amorphous ice with the density of liquid water was made by ball-milling in 2023.[^11] The most familiar substance keeps producing surprises.
:::

## Summary

- Intermolecular forces (dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding) are weak, which is why they, not bonds, decide melting, boiling, and dissolving.
- Water's hydrogen-bond network explains its high boiling point, floating ice, heat capacity, surface tension, and solvent power.
- Gases obey $PV = nRT$ regardless of identity; van der Waals' corrections predict critical points.
- Solids are ionic, molecular, covalent-network, or metallic; X-ray diffraction reveals their lattices; one element can have several allotropes and one compound several polymorphs.
- Phase diagrams map states against temperature and pressure, with triple and critical points.
- Dissolving is lattice energy versus hydration energy; like dissolves like; colligative properties depend on particle count alone.
