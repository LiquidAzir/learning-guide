## How does chemistry become the machinery of life?

Every rule so far applies inside a living cell: bonds, intermolecular forces, redox, free energy, catalysis, equilibrium, organic mechanisms. What makes **biochemistry** special is not different chemistry but different organization. A cell runs thousands of reactions at once, in water, at one temperature, each one controlled by a catalyst built to order. This chapter shows how.

## Water first

Life is chemistry in water, and water's peculiarities (chapter 7) are load-bearing. Its dipole dissolves the ions and polar molecules cells run on. Its hydrogen bonds hold DNA's two strands together and shape every protein. Its high heat capacity keeps temperatures steady. And its refusal to mix with oil is what makes cells possible at all: molecules with a water-loving (**hydrophilic**) head and an oil-loving (**hydrophobic**) tail spontaneously arrange themselves into a double layer, tails inward, heads out, forming a closed bag. That bag is a **cell membrane**, and it assembles itself for free. The **hydrophobic effect** that drives it is mostly entropy: water molecules are more disordered when they are not forced to arrange themselves around an oily surface.[^1]

## The four kinds of molecule

Cells are built from four families of organic molecules, three of them polymers.

**Carbohydrates** are sugars and their chains. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is the universal fuel; link glucose units one way and you get starch (energy storage in plants) or glycogen (in animals); link them another way and you get cellulose, the structural fiber of wood and cotton, which humans cannot digest because we lack the enzyme to break that particular bond. Sugars are chiral, and life uses only the right-handed forms.

**Lipids** are the oily molecules: fats, which store energy at twice the density of carbohydrate; the phospholipids of membranes; cholesterol and the steroid hormones made from it. They are defined not by a functional group but by a property: they do not dissolve in water.

**Proteins** are chains of **amino acids**, twenty kinds, each with the same backbone (an amine and a carboxylic acid, chapter 12) and a different side group. Joining the acid of one to the amine of the next, with loss of water, makes a **peptide bond**; a chain of a few hundred is a protein. The sequence of amino acids is the protein's **primary structure**. It folds spontaneously into helices and sheets (**secondary structure**), which pack into a compact three-dimensional shape (**tertiary structure**), and several chains may assemble together (**quaternary**). The folded shape is everything: it decides what the protein binds and what it does. Proteins are the cell's machinery. Enzymes (chapter 10), transporters, motors, receptors, antibodies, and structural fibers like collagen and keratin are all proteins.

**Nucleic acids**, DNA and RNA, are chains of **nucleotides**: a sugar, a phosphate, and one of four flat nitrogen-containing **bases** (in DNA: adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine). The sequence of bases is information.

## The molecule that copies itself

In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the structure of DNA: two chains wound into a **double helix**, running in opposite directions, held together by hydrogen bonds between bases across the middle.[^2] Their key evidence was Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction photograph, taken with Raymond Gosling, which showed the helical pattern, and Erwin Chargaff's finding that DNA always contains equal amounts of adenine and thymine, and of guanine and cytosine.[^3] The reason is that the bases pair: adenine fits thymine with two hydrogen bonds, guanine fits cytosine with three, and no other combination fits the width of the helix.

Complementary pairing is why the molecule can copy itself. Pull the strands apart and each is a template on which the only sequence that can form is the partner it just left. Watson and Crick ended their one-page paper with what may be the most understated sentence in science: "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material."[^2]

Information flows out of DNA in two steps. **Transcription** copies a stretch of DNA into a single strand of RNA. **Translation**, performed by the **ribosome** (itself made largely of RNA), reads the RNA three bases at a time and adds the matching amino acid to a growing protein. The dictionary from three-letter codes to amino acids is the **genetic code**, cracked between 1961 and 1966 by Marshall Nirenberg, Har Gobind Khorana, and others, and shared, with tiny variations, by every organism on Earth.[^4] That universality is the strongest single piece of evidence that all life descends from one ancestor.

## How proteins fold

A protein chain of 100 amino acids, with even three possible twists at each link, could adopt about $10^{47}$ shapes. Trying them all would take longer than the age of the universe, yet real proteins fold in milliseconds. This is **Levinthal's paradox**, and the resolution is that folding is not a search but a funnel: the chain collapses down a free-energy landscape shaped by hydrophobic burial and hydrogen bonding, and the native shape is the bottom.[^5] Christian Anfinsen showed in the 1960s that a denatured protein refolds correctly on its own, so the sequence contains all the information needed (Nobel Prize 1972).[^6]

Predicting the shape from the sequence remained the central unsolved problem of biochemistry for fifty years. In 2020 DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 predicted structures with accuracy matching experiment for most proteins, and by 2022 it had released predictions for essentially every known protein sequence.[^7] David Baker's group, meanwhile, learned to run the problem backward, designing sequences that fold into shapes that do not exist in nature, including new enzymes. Baker, Demis Hassabis, and John Jumper shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.[^8] Misfolding is also disease: Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and prion diseases all involve proteins collapsing into the wrong, sticky shape.

## Metabolism: controlled combustion

You eat glucose and breathe oxygen and produce carbon dioxide and water. So does a fire. The difference is that the cell releases glucose's 2,870 kJ/mol of free energy, which a fire would release as 2,803 kJ/mol of raw heat, in dozens of small steps, capturing about 30 molecules of ATP along the way rather than letting it all go at once.

The bookkeeping runs through three stages. **Glycolysis** splits glucose into two three-carbon fragments, netting two ATP. The **citric acid cycle** (worked out by Hans Krebs in 1937) strips the remaining carbons off as carbon dioxide and, crucially, loads the electrons removed onto carrier molecules.[^9] Then the **electron transport chain** in the mitochondrial membrane passes those electrons down a staircase of redox reactions (chapter 8) to oxygen, using the energy at each step to pump protons across the membrane. The result is a proton gradient, an acid–base imbalance (chapter 11) with a voltage across the membrane of about 0.15 volts, and a total driving force near 0.2 volts once the pH difference is counted. Protons flowing back through a molecular turbine, **ATP synthase**, spin it and drive the making of ATP.

Peter Mitchell proposed this **chemiosmotic** mechanism in 1961; it was so unlike anything in textbook biochemistry that it took fifteen years and a Nobel Prize (1978) to be accepted.[^10] Paul Boyer and John Walker later showed that ATP synthase really is a rotary motor, and shared the 1997 prize; single-molecule experiments then filmed its shaft turning, at rates up to about 130 revolutions a second.[^11] Your cells run on tiny electric turbines, powered by controlled burning.

:::key
Metabolism is redox chemistry (electrons falling from food to oxygen) converted into acid–base chemistry (a proton gradient) converted into mechanical rotation converted into a chemical bond (ATP). Every chapter in Part IV appears in a single mitochondrion. The genius of the design is that the big free-energy drop is divided into steps small enough to capture, which no fire does.
:::

**Photosynthesis** runs the same machinery backward. Chlorophyll absorbs light and uses it to strip electrons from water (releasing oxygen as waste), pumps protons to make ATP, and spends the ATP and the electrons to fix carbon dioxide into sugar in the **Calvin cycle** (Nobel Prize 1961).[^12] The enzyme that grabs the carbon dioxide, **Rubisco**, is slow and error-prone, so plants make enormous amounts of it; it is probably the most abundant protein on Earth. Every calorie you have ever eaten and almost every breath of oxygen came through this route.

## Where did it start?

In 1953 Stanley Miller sealed water, methane, ammonia, and hydrogen in a flask, sparked it for a week to imitate lightning, and found amino acids.[^13] The building blocks of life form easily from simple chemistry. The hard problem is the next step: how a self-copying system got going before there were enzymes to run it. The leading idea, the **RNA world**, rests on the discovery in the 1980s that RNA can act as a catalyst as well as carry information (Nobel Prize 1989), so a single kind of molecule could once have done both jobs.[^14] Recent work has shown plausible prebiotic routes to nucleotides and to RNA strands that attach amino acids, and samples returned from the asteroids Ryugu (2020) and Bennu (2023) have since been found to contain all five nucleobases, so the ingredients were available across the early solar system (see Latest Research). Nobody has yet made a self-sustaining chemical system from scratch. It is one of the field's great open problems (chapter 16).

## Chemistry as medicine

Almost every drug is an organic molecule designed to bind a specific protein. Aspirin (1897) blocks an enzyme that makes inflammation signals. Penicillin, whose structure Dorothy Hodgkin solved by X-ray crystallography in 1945, jams the enzyme bacteria use to build cell walls.[^15] Modern drug design starts from the target protein's structure and shapes a molecule to fit, which is why AlphaFold mattered so much.

Three recent technologies show biochemistry turning into engineering. **mRNA vaccines** deliver a synthetic RNA that instructs cells to make a viral protein; the key chemical trick, swapping one nucleoside for a modified version so the immune system does not destroy the RNA on sight, was worked out by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman in 2005 and won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Medicine.[^16] **CRISPR** gene editing, adapted from a bacterial immune system by Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2020), cuts DNA at a chosen sequence; the first CRISPR-based therapy, for sickle-cell disease, was approved in 2023.[^17] And **designed proteins** from Baker's methods are now in clinical trials.

:::frontier
The frontier of biochemistry is construction. Can we design enzymes for reactions nature never invented? (Yes, increasingly.) Can we build a minimal cell from defined chemicals? (A synthetic genome has run a bacterial cell since 2010; a cell from scratch has not been made.) Can we find life's origin, or a second origin elsewhere? The Latest Research section tracks prebiotic chemistry, protein design, and the asteroid-sample results.
:::

## Summary

- Life is chemistry in water; the hydrophobic effect self-assembles membranes for free.
- Four molecular families: carbohydrates (fuel and structure), lipids (membranes and storage), proteins (machinery, folded from twenty amino acids), nucleic acids (information).
- DNA's complementary base pairing, held by hydrogen bonds, is why it can copy itself; the genetic code is universal.
- Proteins fold down free-energy funnels; predicting the fold was solved computationally in 2020, and designing new folds won the 2024 Nobel Prize.
- Metabolism is combustion in small steps: redox to proton gradient to rotary motor to ATP. Photosynthesis runs it backward.
- Life's building blocks form easily; how they first organized into a self-copying system is unsolved.
