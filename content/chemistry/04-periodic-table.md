## Recap

By 1860 chemists had about sixty elements and, thanks to Cannizzaro, a consistent set of atomic weights. What they did not have was any idea why there were so many, or how they related to one another. The answer turned out to be the most useful diagram in science.

## Hints of a pattern

The first clue was noticed in 1829 by Johann Döbereiner, who found **triads**: groups of three similar elements in which the middle one had an atomic weight about halfway between the others. Lithium, sodium, and potassium; chlorine, bromine, and iodine; calcium, strontium, and barium.[^1] It was suggestive and nobody knew what to do with it.

In 1865 the English chemist John Newlands arranged the elements by atomic weight and noticed that properties repeated every eighth element, like notes in a musical scale. He called it the **law of octaves**. When he presented it to the Chemical Society, a member asked whether he had tried arranging the elements alphabetically. The society declined to publish.[^2] Newlands was right in outline and wrong in detail (the repeat length is not always eight), and he was mocked out of the field.

## Mendeleev

Dmitri Mendeleev was writing a chemistry textbook in St. Petersburg in 1869 and needed a way to organize the elements. He wrote each one on a card with its weight and properties and shuffled them for days, playing what he called "chemical solitaire." (The story that the arrangement came to him in a dream comes from Mendeleev himself, decades later, and historians treat it with caution.)[^3]

The table he published in March 1869 arranged the elements by increasing atomic weight in rows, with the rows broken so that elements with similar properties fell in the same column.[^4] So far this resembled Newlands. Mendeleev's genius was in three decisions.

First, **he left gaps.** Where the pattern demanded an element and none was known, he left a space and predicted the missing element's properties from its neighbors. He named three of them by the Sanskrit prefix *eka* ("one beyond"): eka-aluminium, eka-boron, and eka-silicon.

Second, **he trusted the pattern over the data.** Where an element's measured atomic weight put it in the wrong column, he moved it and declared the weight wrong. Tellurium and iodine were swapped. Several weights were "corrected," and in most cases later measurements proved him right.

Third, **he predicted.** Eka-aluminium, he said, would have atomic weight about 68, density about 6 grams per cubic centimeter, a low melting point, and would form an oxide with formula Ea₂O₃.

In 1875 Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered a new element by its spectrum and named it gallium. Atomic weight 69.7. Melting point 30 °C (it melts in your hand). He first reported the density as 4.7; Mendeleev wrote to say it must be closer to 5.9, and when Lecoq purified his sample the density came out at 5.9.[^5] Mendeleev had corrected a measurement of an element he had never seen. Scandium (eka-boron) followed in 1879 and germanium (eka-silicon) in 1886.

| Atomic weight | 72 | 72.6 |

| Oxide formula and density | EsO₂, 4.7 | GeO₂, 4.7 |

| Color | dark gray | grayish white |

Winkler's own paper laid the two columns side by side.[^6] After that, the periodic table was not a classification; it was a theory that made predictions, and it had passed.

Lothar Meyer in Germany published a nearly identical table in 1870, based largely on a plot of atomic volume against weight, and had drafted one earlier.[^7] The two shared the Royal Society's Davy Medal in 1882. Mendeleev gets the greater credit because he predicted and Meyer did not.

## A whole column nobody had noticed

In 1894 William Ramsay and Lord Rayleigh, puzzled by a discrepancy in the density of nitrogen from air compared with nitrogen from chemicals, isolated a gas from air that reacted with nothing whatsoever. They named it argon, "the lazy one."[^8] It fit nowhere in Mendeleev's table, and for a moment the table seemed threatened. Then Ramsay found helium on Earth (it had been seen in the Sun's spectrum in 1868), and in 1898 neon, krypton, and xenon. An entire new column, the **noble gases**, slotted in between the halogens and the alkali metals. The table had not failed. It had a spare column waiting.

## Moseley finds the real ordering

Mendeleev's table had an awkwardness: atomic weight was the ordering principle, but he had to break the order in a few places (tellurium and iodine, cobalt and nickel) to make the columns work. Something other than weight was doing the ordering.

In 1913 Henry Moseley, a 25-year-old in Rutherford's laboratory, fired electrons at targets of different elements and measured the frequency of the X-rays that came out. The frequencies rose in perfectly regular steps from one element to the next, and the square root of the frequency was proportional to an integer.[^9] That integer is the **atomic number**, the number of protons in the nucleus, and it, not weight, is what defines an element and orders the table. Tellurium (52) really does come before iodine (53); its higher weight is a quirk of its isotopes. Moseley's method also showed exactly where elements were missing: numbers 43, 61, 72, and 75 had no known occupant, and all four were later found. Moseley was killed at Gallipoli in 1915; Rutherford and others argued that the Army should never again send its scientists to the front.

## What the table means

Why do properties repeat? Mendeleev never knew. The answer, which came from quantum mechanics in the 1920s, is that electrons around a nucleus occupy shells of fixed capacity, and an element's chemistry is set almost entirely by how many electrons are in its outermost, partly filled shell. Elements in the same column have the same number of outer electrons, so they do the same things. That is the subject of the next chapter. Here is what you can already read off the table's shape.

{{fig:periodic-table|The periodic table by block. Each block corresponds to a type of electron orbital being filled (chapter 5), which is why the blocks have widths of 2, 6, 10, and 14.}}

**Groups (columns).** The columns are numbered 1 to 18 across the table, the modern international convention; older books and many periodic tables on classroom walls use Roman numerals with A and B labels instead, so the alkali metals may appear as group IA. Group 1, the **alkali metals** (lithium to francium), are soft, silvery, and react violently with water, because each has one loosely held outer electron it is eager to lose. Group 2, the **alkaline earths** (beryllium to radium), have two. Group 17, the **halogens** (fluorine to iodine), are one electron short of a full shell and grab electrons from anything; fluorine is the most reactive element there is. Group 18, the noble gases, have full shells and want nothing. In between, groups 3 to 12 are the **transition metals**: iron, copper, gold, and their relatives, whose partly filled inner shells give them their colors, magnetism, and usefulness as catalysts (chapter 14).

**Periods (rows).** Moving across a row, each element has one more proton and one more electron than the last. The extra proton pulls the electrons in tighter, so atoms actually get *smaller* across a period, and hold their electrons more firmly. Moving down a column adds a whole new shell, so atoms get larger and hold their outer electrons more loosely.

Three trends follow, and they explain most of chemistry.

- **Atomic radius** decreases left to right and increases top to bottom.
- **Ionization energy**, the energy needed to remove an electron, increases left to right (tighter grip) and decreases top to bottom (electron farther away and shielded by inner shells). Alkali metals give up electrons easily; noble gases do not.
- **Electronegativity**, an element's pull on shared electrons in a bond, follows the same pattern: highest at fluorine in the upper right, lowest at cesium in the lower left. Linus Pauling put numbers on it in 1932, and his scale is still used.[^10] The gap in electronegativity between two bonded atoms decides whether the bond is ionic, polar, or covalent (chapter 6).

**Metals and nonmetals.** A stair-step line from boron to astatine divides the table. To its left are the metals (about three quarters of all elements): shiny, conductive, and inclined to lose electrons. To its right are the nonmetals, inclined to gain them. Along the line sit the **metalloids** such as silicon and germanium, in-between elements whose in-between conductivity is what makes them semiconductors.

## The table today

Mendeleev's table had 63 elements. Today it has 118, and every element up to oganesson (118) has been made and confirmed.[^11] Everything past uranium (92) is synthetic, produced by slamming nuclei together in accelerators. Glenn Seaborg's group at Berkeley made plutonium and eight other elements in the 1940s and 1950s, and Seaborg is one of two people to have an element named for them in their lifetime.[^12] Since then laboratories in Dubna (Russia), Darmstadt (Germany), and Wako (Japan) have filled the seventh row, one atom at a time; the heaviest elements exist for milliseconds and have been made only a few dozen times.

The chase continues. Element 120 would open an eighth row, but the reactions that made the current heaviest elements used a calcium-48 beam, and no suitable heavier target exists. In 2024 a Berkeley team showed that a titanium-50 beam can make livermorium (116), demonstrating the harder route that would be needed for 120.[^13] The heaviest elements also probe a prediction of nuclear theory: an **island of stability** around 114 to 126 protons where some isotopes might live for minutes or longer.[^15] The evidence so far is suggestive; the longest-lived superheavy isotopes do live longer than the ones just below them.

:::frontier
The table's shape may be about to change. For the heaviest elements, the innermost electrons move at a substantial fraction of the speed of light, and special relativity (see the [physics guide](#/physics/special-relativity)) alters their orbitals enough to change chemistry. Relativity is why gold is gold-colored rather than silvery and why mercury is a liquid.[^14] For elements beyond about 120, calculations suggest the neat block structure may break down entirely, with elements no longer behaving like the column they sit in. Whether the periodic table has an end, and what it looks like, is an open question.
:::

## Summary

- Döbereiner's triads and Newlands' octaves hinted at periodicity; Mendeleev's 1869 table made it a predictive theory by leaving gaps and forecasting the missing elements' properties. Gallium, scandium, and germanium confirmed it.
- The noble gases added a column nobody had anticipated; the table absorbed them.
- Moseley showed in 1913 that atomic number (protons), not weight, orders the elements, and pinpointed the missing ones.
- The table's columns share outer-electron arrangements; its rows reflect increasing nuclear pull. Radius, ionization energy, and electronegativity follow from that, and they drive bonding.
- The table has grown to 118 elements, the last 26 made in accelerators. Whether it can continue, and whether relativity scrambles its logic at the bottom, is unsettled.
