## The question that started everything

What is stuff made of, and why does it change? Wood burns to ash, ore melts to metal, grape juice turns to wine. For most of history the answers were guesses, some of them brilliant. What eventually settled the matter was not cleverness but a balance: weigh everything, before and after, and refuse to lose track of a single gram.

## Elements before chemistry

The Greek philosopher Empedocles proposed around 450 BCE that everything is made of four elements, earth, water, air, and fire, in varying proportions. Aristotle adopted the scheme and added a logic: each element had two of four qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry), and changing the qualities changed the element.[^1] Around the same time Democritus proposed the opposite picture: matter is made of tiny indivisible particles, **atoms** (from the Greek for "uncuttable"), moving in a void. He had no evidence; it was a philosophical position, and Aristotle's authority buried it for two millennia.

Meanwhile, without any theory, people did chemistry superbly. Smelting copper and tin into bronze, then iron from ore. Making glass, soap, dyes, cement, and beer. These crafts were the real chemistry of antiquity, passed down as recipes.

## Alchemy: wrong goal, right skills

**Alchemy** was the attempt to transform matter, above all to turn base metals into gold and to find an elixir of life. It flourished in Hellenistic Egypt, then in the Islamic world, where Jabir ibn Hayyan and his successors in the eighth and ninth centuries developed distillation, crystallization, and the preparation of strong acids, and then in medieval and Renaissance Europe.[^2]

It is easy to laugh at alchemy. Do not. Its central goal was reasonable given what was known. Ores that look like dirt yield shining metal in a furnace; lead and tin can be alloyed into things that look like silver; nothing then known ruled out finishing the job. Alchemists built the apparatus (the alembic, the retort, the water bath still called a *bain-marie*), isolated substances (alcohol, mineral acids, phosphorus), and established the habit of doing experiments at all. Isaac Newton spent more time on alchemy than on physics.[^3] What alchemy lacked was a way to tell success from failure: no consistent units, no balancing of accounts, and a culture of secrecy.

## Boyle asks what an element is

In 1661 Robert Boyle published *The Sceptical Chymist*, an attack on both the four elements and the alchemists' three "principles" (salt, sulfur, mercury).[^4] His alternative definition was operational: an **element** is a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical means. This shifted the question from philosophy to the laboratory. Whether something is an element is now something you find out by trying to decompose it. Boyle also found, in 1662, that a gas's volume shrinks in exact proportion as its pressure rises, one of the first quantitative laws in chemistry (chapter 7).

## Phlogiston: a good wrong theory

Why do things burn? Around 1700 Johann Becher and Georg Stahl proposed that combustible materials contain a substance, **phlogiston**, that escapes as flame when they burn.[^5] Wood is rich in phlogiston; ash is wood with its phlogiston gone. Metals rust (or "calcine" to a powder) by losing phlogiston slowly. Breathing releases phlogiston from the body, and a candle goes out in a sealed jar because the air becomes saturated with it.

Notice how much this explains, and how it correctly groups burning, rusting, and breathing as the same kind of process. That grouping is right; we now call the process oxidation. The theory's fatal flaw was quantitative. When a metal calcines it *gains* weight, which is hard to square with something leaving it. Defenders proposed that phlogiston had negative weight. That should have been the alarm.

## Lavoisier and the balance

Antoine Lavoisier, a wealthy French tax official and superb experimentalist, settled it in the 1770s and 1780s by weighing with obsessive care.[^6] Heating mercury in a sealed vessel with a measured volume of air, he found the metal gained exactly the weight the air lost, and that about one fifth of the air disappeared, leaving a gas that would not support flame or life. Heating the resulting red powder alone gave back the metal and a gas in which candles burned brilliantly. Joseph Priestley had produced this gas in 1774 and called it "dephlogisticated air"; Carl Wilhelm Scheele had found it independently.[^7] Lavoisier named it **oxygen** and identified it as the active ingredient in burning, rusting, and breathing. Combustion is not something leaving; it is oxygen combining.

Two principles came out of this work, both still foundational.

**Conservation of mass.** In every chemical change, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. Nothing is created or destroyed; it is rearranged. Lavoisier stated it explicitly in his 1789 *Traité élémentaire de chimie*, the first modern chemistry textbook.[^6] (Einstein would later show that mass can convert to energy, but the amounts in chemical reactions are a billionth of a percent, undetectable on any balance.)

**A rational language.** With Guyton de Morveau and others, Lavoisier replaced the alchemical names ("oil of vitriol," "butter of antimony") with a systematic nomenclature in which a compound's name tells you its composition.[^8] Sulfuric acid, sodium chloride, carbon dioxide: the system you learned in school is his. He also published a list of 33 elements, in Boyle's sense, including some (light, heat) that did not survive.

:::story
Lavoisier was guillotined in May 1794 during the Terror, not for his science but for his role as a tax collector. The mathematician Lagrange remarked that it took an instant to cut off his head and a century might not produce another like it. The famous line attributed to the judge, "The Republic has no need of scientists," is almost certainly a later invention.[^9]
:::

## Fixed proportions and the return of the atom

Once mass was conserved and compounds had definite names, patterns appeared in the numbers. In 1799 Joseph Proust showed that a given compound always contains its elements in the same proportions by mass, however it is made: copper carbonate from a mine and from the laboratory are identical.[^10] This **law of definite proportions** was disputed for years by Claude Berthollet, who had examples of variable composition that turned out to be mixtures, not compounds.

John Dalton, a Quaker schoolteacher in Manchester, saw what fixed proportions implied. If matter comes in discrete atoms, each element's atoms having a characteristic weight, then compounds formed by combining whole numbers of atoms *must* have fixed proportions. And when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other should be in small whole-number ratios. They are.

:::math Dalton's law of multiple proportions
Carbon and oxygen form two gases. In one, 12 grams of carbon combine with 16 grams of oxygen; in the other, 12 grams of carbon combine with 32 grams. The ratio 16:32 is exactly 1:2. Dalton's explanation: the first gas has one oxygen atom per carbon atom and the second has two. We now write them CO and CO₂. There is no way to get such clean ratios from a continuous fluid; they are the fingerprint of counting.
:::

Dalton published his atomic theory in *A New System of Chemical Philosophy* (1808).[^11] Atoms of a given element are identical; atoms of different elements have different weights; compounds are fixed combinations of atoms; reactions rearrange atoms without changing them. Every sentence in this guide rests on those four claims.

## The half-century of confusion

Dalton's theory had a hole: it gave relative weights only if you knew the formula, and you could only find the formula if you knew the weights. Dalton assumed the simplest formulas (water was HO), which made oxygen's atomic weight 8 rather than 16, and the error propagated everywhere.

Two clues were available and one was ignored. Joseph Gay-Lussac found in 1808, publishing the following year, that gases combine in simple volume ratios: two volumes of hydrogen with one of oxygen make two volumes of steam.[^12] In 1811 Amedeo Avogadro proposed an explanation: equal volumes of any gas at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles, *and* the particles of elemental gases like hydrogen and oxygen are pairs of atoms, H₂ and O₂.[^13] That accounts for the two volumes of steam and gives water the formula H₂O. Avogadro was an obscure Italian, his argument was abstract, and chemists ignored it for nearly fifty years.

In the meantime Jöns Jacob Berzelius measured atomic weights with unprecedented care and, in 1813, introduced the letter symbols still in use: one or two letters from the Latin name, so that iron is Fe and sodium is Na.[^14] Alessandro Volta announced the first battery in 1800, a stack of zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked cloth that delivered a steady current for the first time. Humphry Davy used it to tear compounds apart, isolating sodium, potassium, calcium, and other metals never before seen (1807–1808).[^15] Michael Faraday put electrochemistry on a quantitative footing in 1834 and coined the word **ion** for the charged particles that carry current through a solution.[^16]

The confusion ended at the first international chemistry conference, at Karlsruhe in 1860, where Stanislao Cannizzaro circulated a pamphlet showing that Avogadro's hypothesis, applied consistently, gave a single self-consistent set of atomic weights.[^17] One attendee, a young Russian named Dmitri Mendeleev, took a copy home. The next chapter is about what he did with it.

## Were atoms real?

Even in 1900, some eminent chemists and physicists treated atoms as a convenient fiction, a bookkeeping device that need not correspond to anything. Wilhelm Ostwald, a Nobel laureate, held this view until 1908. What changed his mind was Jean Perrin's measurement of the random jitter of tiny particles in water, which matched Einstein's 1905 prediction of how molecular collisions should shove them around and yielded a count of the molecules involved.[^18] From then on, atoms were things, not ideas. The [physics guide's chapter on heat](#/physics/heat) tells that story from the other side.

## Atoms have parts

Dalton's atoms were indivisible by definition, the smallest possible piece of an element. That lasted about ninety years.

In 1897 J. J. Thomson, studying the glowing rays that stream from a heated wire in an evacuated tube, showed they were bent by both electric and magnetic fields and measured the ratio of charge to mass of whatever was doing the streaming.[^19] The answer was a shock: the particles were about two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom, the lightest thing then known, and they were the same regardless of what metal the wire was made of. Thomson had found the **electron**, a piece of matter smaller than an atom and common to all of them. Atoms were divisible after all.

That raised a question. Electrons are negative and ordinary matter is neutral, so an atom must also contain positive charge. Thomson proposed the sensible arrangement: a diffuse ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it like currants, a picture known ever since as the **plum pudding model**. It fitted everything known. Nothing suggested that the positive charge was anywhere in particular.

In 1909 Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working for Ernest Rutherford in Manchester, fired **alpha particles**, the heavy positively charged fragments thrown out by radium, at a sheet of gold foil about two thousand atoms thick, and counted the flashes where they landed. Almost all went straight through, as plum pudding predicted. But about one in eight thousand bounced back toward the source. Rutherford later called it the most incredible event of his life, "as if you fired a fifteen-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."[^20]

Nothing diffuse could do that. In 1911 Rutherford drew the conclusion: an atom's positive charge and nearly all of its mass are concentrated in a **nucleus** roughly one hundred-thousandth the width of the atom, with the electrons somewhere in the vast empty space around it.[^21] If the nucleus were a marble on a pitcher's mound, the atom would reach the outfield seats. Matter is almost entirely nothing.

The nucleus itself turned out to have parts. Rutherford identified the positively charged **proton** in 1919, and in 1932 James Chadwick found the **neutron**, a particle of almost the same mass but no charge.[^22] That completed the picture chemistry still uses. The number of protons is the atomic number and fixes which element you have. The number of neutrons can vary, and those variants are the isotopes that had been confusing atomic weights for a century.

One puzzle remained, and it was fatal to classical physics: an electron orbiting a nucleus should radiate away its energy and spiral in within a fraction of a nanosecond. Atoms should not exist. Chapter 5 is about the answer.

:::try Put the idea to work
Two atoms have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Are they different elements, and must their masses be equal?

:::answer Show the reasoning
They are isotopes of the same element: proton number determines the element. Their masses differ because their neutron counts differ. Their electron arrangements, and therefore much of their chemistry, can be similar even though their nuclear properties are not.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Ancient thinkers proposed four elements and, separately, atoms; neither idea had evidence. Craft chemistry (metals, glass, dyes) flourished without theory.
- Alchemy pursued an impossible goal with real skill, building the apparatus and habits of experiment.
- Boyle redefined an element operationally. Phlogiston explained burning, rusting, and breathing as one process, correctly, but failed the test of the balance.
- Lavoisier established conservation of mass, identified oxygen's role, and gave chemistry a rational language.
- Fixed proportions (Proust) and multiple proportions (Dalton) revealed that matter is counted, not poured. Dalton's atomic theory (1808) is the foundation of the subject.
- Avogadro's hypothesis, ignored for fifty years, resolved the confusion over atomic weights in 1860. Atoms were finally shown to be physically real in 1908.
- Then they turned out to be divisible: Thomson's electron (1897), Rutherford's nucleus (1911) after alpha particles bounced off gold foil, and Chadwick's neutron (1932). Atoms are mostly empty space, and classical physics could not explain why they were stable at all.
