## How to use this chapter

Chemistry is made by people, often working with their hands. This roster is organized by era; each entry gives the central contribution and the chapter where it appears. It is selective: these are the figures you need in order to hold the subject together. Where a discovery was shared or contested, the entry says so.

## Before chemistry (to 1700)

**Jabir ibn Hayyan** (c. 721–c. 815) and the Arabic alchemical tradition. Developed distillation, crystallization, and the mineral acids, and left a corpus of recipes that shaped European practice for centuries. Chapter 3.

**Paracelsus** (1493–1541). Turned alchemy toward medicine, insisting that chemical preparations could treat disease and that "the dose makes the poison," a principle toxicology still runs on. Chapter 3.

**Robert Boyle** (1627–1691). Defined an element operationally as what cannot be broken down further, and found that a gas's pressure and volume are inversely related. Chapters 3, 7.

## The chemical revolution (1700–1830)

**Georg Stahl** (1659–1734). Formalized the phlogiston theory, wrong but productive: it grouped burning, rusting, and breathing as one process. Chapter 3.

**Joseph Priestley** (1733–1804) and **Carl Wilhelm Scheele** (1742–1786). Independently prepared oxygen; Scheele also discovered chlorine, and probably more elements than anyone. Neither interpreted the gas correctly. Chapter 3.

**Antoine Lavoisier** (1743–1794). Established conservation of mass, identified oxygen's role in combustion, and gave chemistry its systematic language. Guillotined in the Terror. Chapter 3.

**Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier** (1758–1836). Translated English chemical works for her husband, drew the plates for the *Traité*, and kept his laboratory records; a working collaborator, not an assistant. Chapter 3.

**Alessandro Volta** (1745–1827). The battery (1800), which turned electricity from a spark into a steady chemical tool. Chapters 3, 6, 8.

**Joseph Proust** (1754–1826). The law of definite proportions: a compound always has the same composition. Chapter 3.

**John Dalton** (1766–1844). The atomic theory (1808) and the law of multiple proportions that supports it. Chapter 3.

**Amedeo Avogadro** (1776–1856). Equal volumes of gas hold equal numbers of particles, and elemental gases are diatomic. Ignored for fifty years. Chapters 2, 3.

**Humphry Davy** (1778–1829). Used electricity to isolate sodium, potassium, calcium, and more; also discovered the anesthetic effect of nitrous oxide, on himself. Chapter 3.

**Jöns Jacob Berzelius** (1779–1848). Precise atomic weights, the letter symbols still in use, and the word "catalysis." Chapters 3, 10.

**Michael Faraday** (1791–1867). The quantitative laws of electrolysis and the word "ion"; also benzene's discovery. Chapters 3, 8.

## The classical era (1830–1900)

**Friedrich Wöhler** (1800–1882). Made urea from inorganic salts in 1828, ending vitalism. Chapter 12.

**Justus von Liebig** (1803–1873). Founded the modern chemistry teaching laboratory, invented reliable elemental analysis, and started agricultural chemistry. His students staffed the world's universities. Chapter 15.

**Louis Pasteur** (1822–1895). Separated the two mirror-image forms of tartaric acid by hand in 1848, discovering molecular chirality. Chapter 12.

**Stanislao Cannizzaro** (1826–1910). Resolved the atomic-weight confusion at Karlsruhe in 1860 by applying Avogadro consistently. Chapter 3.

**Robert Bunsen** (1811–1899) and **Gustav Kirchhoff** (1824–1887). Turned spectroscopy into chemical analysis in 1859, and found two elements by their light alone. Chapters 5, 15.

**J. J. Thomson** (1856–1940). Discovered the electron in 1897, the first particle smaller than an atom, and later used its deflection to find the first isotopes of a stable element. Chapters 3, 15.

**Ernest Rutherford** (1871–1937). The nuclear atom (1911), from alpha particles bouncing off gold foil, and the proton. Chapter 3.

**James Chadwick** (1891–1974). The neutron (1932), which completed the nucleus and explained isotopes. Chapter 3.

**Dmitri Mendeleev** (1834–1907). The periodic table (1869), with gaps and predictions that came true. Chapter 4.

**Lothar Meyer** (1830–1895). Reached the periodic table independently and almost simultaneously; did not predict. Chapter 4.

**August Kekulé** (1829–1896). Carbon is tetravalent and chains; the benzene ring (1865). Chapters 6, 12.

**Jacobus van 't Hoff** (1852–1911). The tetrahedral carbon (1874, aged 22), osmotic pressure, and reaction kinetics; the first Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1901). Chapters 7, 12.

**Josiah Willard Gibbs** (1839–1903). Chemical thermodynamics, including free energy and the phase rule, published almost invisibly in Connecticut. Chapters 7, 9.

**Svante Arrhenius** (1859–1927). Ions in solution, the activation energy equation, and, in 1896, the first calculation of how carbon dioxide warms the planet. Chapters 10, 11.

**Wilhelm Ostwald** (1853–1932). Founded physical chemistry as a discipline and defined catalysis; doubted atoms until 1908. Chapters 3, 10.

**William Perkin** (1838–1907). Made mauve by accident at 18 and founded the synthetic dye industry. Chapter 12.

**Alfred Nobel** (1833–1896). Invented dynamite by taming nitroglycerin with diatomaceous earth, and left his fortune to the prizes that appear throughout this guide.

**William Ramsay** (1852–1916) and **Lord Rayleigh** (1842–1919). Found the noble gases, an entire column missing from Mendeleev's table, by weighing nitrogen two ways and getting two different answers. Chapter 4.

## The quantum and structural era (1900–1960)

**Marie Curie** (1867–1934). Named radioactivity, isolated polonium and radium, and won Nobel Prizes in physics and chemistry. Her work opened the study of the atom's interior. Chapter 3.

**Fritz Haber** (1868–1934) and **Carl Bosch** (1874–1940). Ammonia from air, which feeds about half the world; Haber also directed Germany's gas warfare. Chapter 10.

**Gilbert Lewis** (1875–1946). The shared electron pair, Lewis structures, the octet rule, and a broader definition of acids and bases. Never won the Nobel Prize. Chapters 6, 11.

**Alfred Werner** (1866–1919). Coordination complexes: metals with a fixed number of attendants in fixed geometries. Chapter 14.

**Henry Moseley** (1887–1915). Atomic number orders the periodic table. Killed at Gallipoli at 27. Chapter 4.

**Niels Bohr** (1885–1962). Quantized electron orbits, explaining atomic spectra and the table's first hints of structure. Chapter 5.

**Wolfgang Pauli** (1900–1958). The exclusion principle, which sets shell capacities and therefore the table's shape. Chapter 5.

**Erwin Schrödinger** (1887–1961). The wave equation whose solutions are orbitals. Chapter 5.

**Linus Pauling** (1901–1994). Electronegativity, hybridization, resonance, the protein alpha helix, and the nature of the chemical bond; Nobel Prizes in Chemistry (1954) and Peace (1962). Chapters 4, 6.

**Irving Langmuir** (1881–1957). Surface chemistry and adsorption, the physical basis of heterogeneous catalysis; Nobel Prize 1932. Chapter 10.

**Hermann Staudinger** (1881–1965). Polymers are genuinely giant molecules. Ridiculed for a decade. Chapter 12.

**Wallace Carothers** (1896–1937). Nylon and neoprene at DuPont; died by suicide at 41. Chapter 12.

**Lise Meitner** (1878–1968) and **Otto Hahn** (1879–1968). Nuclear fission; the Nobel went to Hahn alone. Chapter 3 (and the physics guide).

**Glenn Seaborg** (1912–1999). Plutonium and nine other elements; rearranged the table's bottom rows to accommodate the actinides. Chapter 4.

**Dorothy Hodgkin** (1910–1994). X-ray structures of penicillin, vitamin B₁₂, and insulin; Nobel Prize 1964. Chapters 13, 15.

**Rosalind Franklin** (1920–1958). The X-ray diffraction data that revealed DNA's helix, and later work on virus structure. Died at 37, before the Nobel. Chapter 13.

**James Watson** (1928–2025) and **Francis Crick** (1916–2004). The double helix and complementary base pairing. Chapter 13.

## The modern era (1960–present)

**Robert Woodward** (1917–1979). Total synthesis of quinine, cholesterol, chlorophyll, and vitamin B₁₂; the standard against which synthesis is still measured. Chapter 12.

**Elias J. Corey** (b. 1928). Retrosynthetic analysis: planning a synthesis backward from the target. Chapter 12.

**Peter Mitchell** (1920–1992). Chemiosmosis: cells make ATP with a proton gradient. Rejected for fifteen years, then a Nobel Prize. Chapter 13.

**Hans Krebs** (1900–1981). The citric acid cycle at the center of metabolism. Chapter 13.

**Christian Anfinsen** (1916–1995). A protein's sequence determines its fold. Chapter 13.

**Ahmed Zewail** (1946–2016). Femtosecond spectroscopy: watching bonds break in real time. Chapter 10.

**George Olah** (1927–2017). Superacids and carbocations, the reactive intermediates of organic mechanisms. Chapters 11, 12.

**Karl Ziegler** (1898–1973) and **Giulio Natta** (1903–1979). Catalysts that made cheap, controlled polymers. Chapters 10, 12.

**John Goodenough** (1922–2023), **M. Stanley Whittingham** (b. 1941), **Akira Yoshino** (b. 1948). The lithium-ion battery; Goodenough received the Nobel Prize at 97, the oldest laureate in any field. Chapter 8.

**Barry Sharpless** (b. 1941), **Morten Meldal** (b. 1954), **Carolyn Bertozzi** (b. 1966). Click chemistry and its version that works inside living cells; Nobel Prize 2022 (Sharpless's second). Chapter 12.

**Benjamin List** (b. 1968) and **David MacMillan** (b. 1968). Organocatalysis: small organic molecules that steer reactions to one mirror image, no metals needed. Nobel Prize 2021. Chapter 10.

**Emmanuelle Charpentier** (b. 1968) and **Jennifer Doudna** (b. 1964). CRISPR as a programmable tool for editing DNA; Nobel Prize 2020. Chapter 13.

**Katalin Karikó** (b. 1955) and **Drew Weissman** (b. 1959). The nucleoside modification that made mRNA medicine possible, after years of rejected grants; Nobel Prize in Medicine 2023. Chapter 13.

**Moungi Bawendi** (b. 1961), **Louis Brus** (b. 1943), **Alexei Ekimov** (b. 1945). Quantum dots: color set by size. Nobel Prize 2023. Chapter 14.

**David Baker** (b. 1962), **Demis Hassabis** (b. 1976), **John Jumper** (b. 1985). Predicting and designing protein structures. Nobel Prize 2024. Chapter 13.

**Susumu Kitagawa** (b. 1951), **Richard Robson** (b. 1937), **Omar Yaghi** (b. 1965). Metal-organic frameworks, the programmable crystalline sponges. Nobel Prize 2025. Chapter 14.

**Frances Arnold** (b. 1956). Directed evolution of enzymes: breeding catalysts rather than designing them. Nobel Prize 2018. Chapter 10.

**Paul Anastas** (b. 1962) and **John Warner** (b. 1962). The twelve principles of green chemistry. Chapter 16.

## The pattern

Two things stand out in this list. The first is how often the decisive idea was rejected: Avogadro ignored for fifty years, Staudinger ridiculed, Mitchell dismissed, Karikó demoted. The second is how often the crucial work was done young or with the hands: Perkin at 18, van 't Hoff at 22, Moseley at 26, Pasteur sorting crystals with tweezers, Lavoisier weighing everything twice. Chemistry rewards patience at the bench and stubbornness in the face of consensus, in roughly equal measure.
