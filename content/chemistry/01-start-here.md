## The whole subject in one sentence

Chemistry is the study of how atoms join, part, and rearrange, and of what those rearrangements make.

That is a modest sentence for an immodest fact. There are only about ninety kinds of atom that occur in nature. Combined, they make every rock, every drug, every smell, every color, every living thing, and the more than 290 million distinct substances that chemists have so far catalogued.[^1] The rules that govern the combining are few enough to fit in this guide. The consequences are not few enough to fit in any library.

:::key
Physics supplies the rules for individual atoms. Biology uses what the atoms build. Chemistry is the layer in between, where atoms become *substances*: things with a taste, a melting point, a tendency to explode or to heal. It has been called the central science for that reason, and the name is deserved.[^2]
:::

## Why chemistry works at all

Here is something that should surprise you. An atom of carbon in a diamond, in a pencil, in a molecule of sugar, and in your own DNA is the same atom, indistinguishable in every way. The wild differences between those four things come entirely from *arrangement*: which atoms are next to which, and how they are held. Chemistry is the science of arrangement.

This means chemistry is far more predictable than its variety suggests. Once you know how carbon likes to bond (four partners, in a tetrahedron), how oxygen likes to bond (two partners, with a bend), and a few dozen similar facts, you can look at a molecule you have never seen and predict, roughly, whether it dissolves in water, whether it burns, whether it is stable, and what it might react with. Chemists do this constantly. The periodic table, the subject of chapter 4, is the cheat sheet that makes it possible: arrange the elements by the structure of their atoms and their chemical behavior falls into columns.

## How chemistry makes progress

Chemistry grew up before physics could explain it, and that history shapes how the subject is taught. For most of its life chemistry was a craft of careful measurement: weigh the ingredients, weigh the products, notice the pattern. The great laws of the 1700s and 1800s, that mass is conserved in every reaction, that compounds always contain their elements in fixed proportions, that the elements fall into a repeating table, were all discovered decades before anyone knew what an atom was made of, let alone why it bonded.

Then, between 1913 and 1930, quantum mechanics arrived and explained the whole structure at once. Why does the periodic table have the shape it does? Because electrons fill shells of a particular size, for reasons that come from the wave equation (chapter 5). Why does carbon make four bonds? Same reason. The explanation arrived sixty years after the pattern. This guide tells the story in that order, pattern first and explanation second, because that is how the ideas actually make sense and because that is how chemists still think: empirical rule first, quantum justification when needed.

Like physics, chemistry does not so much overturn old ideas as bound them. The **phlogiston** theory of burning (chapter 3) was wrong, but it correctly grouped burning, rusting, and breathing as one kind of process, and that grouping survives. **Vitalism**, the belief that the molecules of life could not be made from ordinary chemicals, was wrong, but its collapse in 1828 is why organic chemistry exists as a field (chapter 12). Every wrong idea in this guide is presented with the reason intelligent people believed it.

:::warning
"Chemical" is not a synonym for "artificial" or "dangerous." Water is a chemical. So is the oxygen you breathe and the DNA that made you. Every substance is chemical; the question is only which one, how much, and where. This guide will use the word in its plain meaning.
:::

## The map of the territory

Chemistry asks six questions about any substance. The chapters are organized around them.

| Question | Field | Chapters |
|---|---|---|

| What holds the atoms together? | **Bonding** | 6, 7 |

| Will the change happen, and how far will it go? | **Thermodynamics, equilibrium, and acids** | 9, 11 |
| How fast? | **Kinetics** | 10 |

## The scales chemistry covers

Chemistry lives on a narrower range of sizes than physics, but it is the range where things get complicated.

| Scale | Size | What lives here |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic nucleus | $10^{-15}$ m | Physics territory; chemistry mostly ignores it |
| Atom | $10^{-10}$ m (0.1 nanometer) | Where electrons decide everything |
| Small molecule (water, caffeine) | $10^{-9}$ m | Drugs, fuels, flavors |
| Protein, polymer chain | $10^{-8}$ m | Enzymes, plastics, DNA |
| Virus, nanoparticle | $10^{-7}$ m | Where quantum size effects give nanomaterials their colors |
| Living cell | $10^{-5}$ m | Thousands of reactions at once, in water, at body temperature |
| A chemical plant | $10^{2}$ m | The Haber–Bosch process, which feeds about half the people on Earth[^3] |

The numbers you will meet most are on the small end. A single drop of water holds about $10^{21}$ molecules. Chemistry's special unit, the mole, exists to make such numbers manageable, and the next chapter explains it.

## Who does chemistry

Chemists divide, roughly, by what they make and how they look at it. **Synthetic** chemists build molecules, sometimes ones that have never existed. **Analytical** chemists find out what is in a sample and how much. **Physical** chemists measure and explain the energies and speeds of reactions. **Organic**, **inorganic**, and **biochemists** specialize by the kinds of substance they study: carbon compounds, everything else, and the molecules of living things. **Computational** chemists predict all of the above from the equations, a field that has grown enormously since about 2010 and that won a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize for predicting the shapes of proteins.[^4]

Chemistry is also the science most directly wired into industry. The largest single chemical process on Earth, the conversion of nitrogen from the air into ammonia for fertilizer, consumes about one to two percent of the world's energy and is the reason the planet can feed eight billion people.[^3] Its inventors, Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch, both won Nobel Prizes; Haber also directed Germany's chemical weapons program in the First World War. Chemistry's power has always cut both ways.

## How to read this guide

The conventions are the same as in the physics guide, and you do not need to have read that guide first.

- **Bold terms** are being defined right where they appear. The glossary (chapter 18) collects them all.
- Colored boxes mark six kinds of interruption: a **key idea** you should not skim, a **story** from the history, a **math box** that shows an equation and explains every symbol, a **think about it** prompt, a **common confusion** to avoid, and a **where it stands today** note on what is unsettled. The people are collected in chapter 17.
- Every factual claim points to a numbered source at the bottom of the chapter, with a link to the original paper where it is free to read.
- A separate **Latest research** section, reachable from the subject's front page, tracks recent results with a plain-English summary and an honest label: confirmed, preliminary, disputed, or retracted. Several chapters point you to it.
- Where a topic rests on physics (electrons, energy, entropy, quantum orbitals), the text gives you what you need and links to the relevant physics chapter for more.
- The math is real but limited. Chemistry uses arithmetic, ratios, logarithms, and a small number of equations that are worth knowing by sight. Each is read aloud as a sentence when it appears.

:::try
Before reading on, write down three things you believe about chemistry that you have never tested. (Examples: natural substances are safer than synthetic ones. Heating always speeds up a reaction. Water is a simple molecule.) By the end of this guide each will have been confirmed, overturned, or complicated.
:::

Read in order if you can. And keep one habit from the physics guide: when a result is announced, ask how strong the evidence is and whether anyone else has reproduced it. Chemistry has had its share of claims that did not survive that test, and chapter 16 has a checklist.
