## How do you identify what is inside an unremarkable vial?

A chemist extracts a promising substance from a sea sponge. The vial looks unremarkable. Is it one compound or a mixture? Which atoms does it contain, and how are they connected?

**Analytical chemistry** turns those questions into measurements. No single instrument answers them all: separation, mass, absorbed light, and magnetic response provide different clues. We will return to the vial as the tools come together. The same reasoning underlies a blood test, a food label, and a forensic analysis.

## Two questions

Analysis asks two things about a sample. **What is in it?** That is qualitative analysis: identity. **How much?** That is quantitative analysis: amount. For most of chemistry's history both were done by hand: dissolve the sample, add reagents that produce a colored precipitate with one element and not another, weigh what falls out. Titration (chapter 11) is the surviving classic. The flame test, dipping a wire in the sample and noting the flame's color, is the ancestor of everything in the next section.

## Light as a probe

The great insight of the 1800s was that matter and light exchange energy only in specific amounts, so the light a substance absorbs or emits is a fingerprint of its structure. Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff built the first spectroscope in 1859, passed the light of salts burning in Bunsen's new gas flame through a prism, and found that each element gave its own set of sharp colored lines. Within two years they had discovered two new elements, cesium and rubidium, by their lines alone, and shown that the dark lines in the Sun's spectrum matched the bright lines of elements on Earth.[^1] Chemistry could now be done on things too small to see and too far to touch.

Different parts of the spectrum probe different things, because they carry different amounts of energy.

**Ultraviolet and visible light** has enough energy to kick electrons to higher orbitals. Molecules with double bonds in a row (**conjugated** systems) absorb in this range, and the longer the row the redder the absorption, which is why carrots are orange and why dyes are built from long conjugated chains. The amount of light absorbed measures the concentration.

:::math The Beer–Lambert law
$$A = \varepsilon\, l\, c$$
$A$ is the **absorbance**, how much light the sample removes, measured as a logarithm of the fraction transmitted. $l$ is the path length through the sample, $c$ its concentration, and $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) is the **molar absorptivity**, a constant for each substance at each wavelength that says how strongly it absorbs. Pierre Bouguer and Johann Lambert established the path-length half of the law in the 1700s; August Beer added the concentration half in 1852.[^2] Read it as: absorbance is proportional to concentration. Measure $A$, know $\varepsilon$ and $l$, and you have $c$. This is how a spectrophotometer measures the protein in a sample, the chlorophyll in a lake, the glucose in blood, or the iron in wine, in seconds, without touching anything.
:::

**Infrared light** has less energy, enough to make bonds vibrate faster but not to move electrons. Each kind of bond stretches and bends at its own frequency, so an infrared spectrum reads off which functional groups a molecule contains: a strong absorption near 1700 cm⁻¹ says "carbonyl," a broad one near 3300 says "O–H." It is also why carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas: its bonds vibrate at frequencies that match the infrared light the warm Earth radiates, so the gas absorbs that light and re-emits some of it downward.

**Radio waves**, the lowest-energy light, probe the nuclei. Certain nuclei, hydrogen above all, behave as tiny magnets, and in a strong magnetic field they can absorb radio waves at a frequency that depends, minutely, on the electrons around them. Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell detected this **nuclear magnetic resonance** (NMR) in 1946, and within four years chemists found that hydrogen atoms in different parts of a molecule resonate at slightly different frequencies, the **chemical shift**.[^3] An NMR spectrum is therefore a map of every hydrogen (or carbon) in a molecule and, through subtler effects, of which ones are near which. It is the single most powerful tool for determining the structure of a new organic compound, and it does so without destroying a milligram of sample. Point the same physics at the hydrogen atoms of water in a human body and you have **MRI**, the medical scanner, for which Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield won the 2003 Nobel Prize in Medicine.[^4]

## Weighing molecules

**Mass spectrometry** does what its name says: it weighs molecules, one at a time. Knock an electron off a molecule to make it an ion, accelerate it with an electric field, and its path through a magnetic field bends by an amount that depends on its mass. J. J. Thomson used the method in 1913 to show that neon comes in two masses, 20 and 22, the first isotopes of a stable element; his student Francis Aston built the first precision instrument and used it to identify most of the naturally occurring isotopes, work that won the 1922 Nobel Prize.[^5]

For chemistry the payoff is twofold. The mass gives the molecular formula directly, to a precision that distinguishes molecules whose formulas differ by one hydrogen. And molecules break into characteristic fragments on the way through, whose masses reveal how they were assembled. The method was limited to small, sturdy molecules until the 1980s, when John Fenn's electrospray and Koichi Tanaka's laser methods found ways to launch intact proteins into the gas phase as ions (Nobel Prize 2002).[^6] Mass spectrometry now identifies thousands of proteins in a single cell sample, screens newborns for metabolic disorders from a drop of blood, detects explosives at airports, and catches athletes doping.

## Seeing atoms in crystals

The Braggs' X-ray diffraction (chapter 7) grew into **X-ray crystallography**, the method that has determined more molecular structures than any other. Grow a crystal, shine X-rays through it, record the pattern of spots, and compute backward to the arrangement of atoms that produced them. Dorothy Hodgkin solved penicillin (1945), vitamin B₁₂ (1955), and insulin (1969) this way, winning the 1964 Nobel Prize.[^7] John Kendrew and Max Perutz solved the first proteins, myoglobin and hemoglobin, in 1958 and 1960, revealing the folded shapes that chapter 13 takes for granted.[^8] DNA's helix was read from Franklin's diffraction photograph. Today more than 200,000 experimentally determined structures sit in the public Protein Data Bank, most of them from crystallography, and every drug designed against a target's structure rests on them.[^13]

The method's limit is the crystal. Many molecules, including most of the proteins in cell membranes, refuse to crystallize. **Cryo-electron microscopy** gets around this by freezing molecules in a thin film of glassy ice, imaging tens of thousands of them with an electron beam, and averaging the images by computer into a three-dimensional structure. Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank, and Richard Henderson shared the 2017 Nobel Prize for developing it, and in 2020 the method reached atomic resolution, showing individual hydrogen atoms.[^9] It can now image molecular machines inside intact cells (see Latest Research).

## Separating mixtures

Before you can identify something you usually have to separate it from everything else. **Chromatography** does this by letting a mixture flow past a stationary material to which its components stick with different strengths, so they emerge at different times. Mikhail Tsvet invented it in 1906 to separate plant pigments through a column of chalk, giving the method its name ("color writing").[^10] Archer Martin and Richard Synge made it quantitative in 1941 and shared the 1952 Nobel Prize; gas chromatography (1952) and high-performance liquid chromatography (1960s) followed.[^11] Coupled to a mass spectrometer, chromatography is the workhorse of every forensic, environmental, and pharmaceutical laboratory: the mixture is pulled apart and each component weighed and identified as it comes off.

## How small a trace

Modern instruments detect substances at parts per trillion, a drop in twenty Olympic pools, and in some cases single molecules. That sensitivity is why we know about pollutants that were invisible a generation ago, from PFAS in drinking water to microplastics in blood. It also creates a duty of interpretation: detecting a molecule is not the same as detecting a dangerous amount of it, and headlines routinely confuse the two.

Isotopes, which mass spectrometry separates cleanly, are among analysis's most elegant tools. Willard Libby realized in 1949 that carbon-14, made by cosmic rays and taken up by every living thing, stops being replenished at death and decays with a 5,730-year half-life, so the ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 in a bone or a beam dates it; he won the 1960 Nobel Prize.[^12] The ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in ice cores and seashells records past temperatures, because heavier water evaporates slightly less readily. Isotope ratios trace the origin of food, the diets of ancient people, and the sources of atmospheric carbon.

## Solving an unknown

Return to the sponge extract. Chromatography first separates its components so the chemist can examine one compound at a time. High-resolution mass spectrometry narrows down the molecular formula, say C₁₅H₂₂O₃, using accurate mass and isotope patterns. The infrared spectrum shows a carbonyl and an O–H. Proton NMR counts the hydrogens by type and shows which are next to which; carbon NMR counts the carbons. From these the chemist proposes a structure. A suitable crystal can let X-ray diffraction resolve the three-dimensional arrangement, though hydrogen positions and absolute configuration can require additional evidence. Chiral chromatography can separate enantiomers; assigning which one is which needs a reference or another suitable method.

A straightforward sample may yield to these tools in days. A scarce, unstable, or complicated molecule can take much longer. The result is convincing when independent clues fit the same structure, not simply because an instrument prints a name.

:::frontier
Analysis is getting smaller, faster, and closer to the sample. Handheld mass spectrometers and infrared scanners identify materials in the field; DNA sequencers the size of a phone read a genome from a single molecule at a time; cryo-electron tomography images ribosomes at work inside frozen cells. The other frontier is data: spectra are being read by machine-learning models trained on millions of examples, and the bottleneck is shifting from acquiring data to trusting the automated interpretation, exactly the problem that tripped up the materials-discovery claims in chapter 14.
:::

:::answer Show the reasoning
No: isomers can share a formula. NMR can constrain connections and local environments; infrared spectroscopy can identify functional groups; suitable diffraction data can resolve spatial structure. Choose the next method for the ambiguity that remains, then check whether the clues agree.

## Summary

- Analysis asks what and how much. Classical wet methods gave way to instruments that read matter's interaction with light, magnetism, and electric fields.
- Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy measures concentration (Beer–Lambert); infrared identifies functional groups; NMR maps a molecule's hydrogens and carbons and, applied to bodies, is MRI.
- Mass spectrometry weighs molecules and their fragments, found isotopes, and now handles whole proteins.
- X-ray crystallography reveals atomic positions in crystals; cryo-electron microscopy does the same for molecules that will not crystallize.
- Chromatography separates mixtures; coupled to mass spectrometry it runs forensic and environmental labs.
- Sensitivity reaches parts per trillion; isotope ratios date bones and reconstruct climates.
