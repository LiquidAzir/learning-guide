## Recap

Organic chemistry covers carbon; biochemistry covers carbon in cells. **Inorganic chemistry** is the rest of the periodic table, which is to say most of it, plus the carbon in rocks and air. Its territory runs from the metals humans have worked for five thousand years to the designed materials of the last thirty.

## Getting metals out of rock

Almost every metal in the Earth's crust is locked in an ore as an oxide or sulfide, because metals give up electrons readily and oxygen and sulfur take them. Extracting a metal is therefore a reduction (chapter 8): find something that wants the oxygen more, or supply electrons directly.

Copper came first, around 5000 BCE, because its ores reduce in a charcoal fire, and copper alloyed with tin gave **bronze**, harder than either, about 3300 BCE. Iron ores need higher temperatures and a stronger reducing agent, carbon monoxide from burning charcoal, and iron smelting spread from about 1200 BCE. Iron with a little carbon dissolved in it is **steel**, and the precise control of that carbon (0.2 to 2 percent) is what separates a sword from a hinge. Henry Bessemer's 1856 converter blew air through molten iron to burn out excess carbon in minutes, making cheap steel and with it railways, skyscrapers, and modern warfare.[^1] Aluminium, the most abundant metal in the crust, resisted everything until electrolysis (1886), because nothing chemical reduces its oxide.

Why are alloys stronger than pure metals? A pure metal's atoms slide past one another along planes, which is why it bends. Foreign atoms of a different size, or a second phase of tiny particles, pin those planes and stop the slipping. This is why bronze beat copper, why steel beats iron, and why a jet engine's turbine blades are nickel alloyed with a dozen other elements.

## Coordination chemistry: metals with attendants

In solution, and in most of their compounds, metal ions do not float free. They are surrounded by a shell of molecules or ions, called **ligands**, that donate electron pairs to the metal's empty orbitals. The whole assembly is a **coordination complex**. Alfred Werner worked out in 1893 that the number of ligands (the **coordination number**, typically four or six) and their geometry are fixed and distinct from the ion's charge, a proposal that contradicted the bonding theories of the day and won him the 1913 Nobel Prize, the first for an inorganic chemist.[^2]

Coordination chemistry explains two of the most visible properties of the transition metals: their colors and their magnetism. In a free metal ion the five d orbitals (chapter 5) have the same energy. Surround the ion with ligands and the orbitals pointing at the ligands are pushed up in energy while the others are not. The gap between them, worked out by Hans Bethe in 1929 in what became **crystal field theory**, typically matches the energy of visible light, so the complex absorbs one color and shows the complementary one.[^3] Copper sulfate is blue because its water ligands split copper's d orbitals by exactly the energy of orange light. Ruby is red because a trace of chromium in colorless aluminium oxide absorbs green. Change the ligands and the color changes, which is why cobalt chloride is pink when wet and blue when dry. And unpaired electrons in those d orbitals make the complex magnetic, which is how a chemist can count them with a balance.

Life is full of coordination complexes. Hemoglobin's oxygen-carrying site is an iron ion held by a flat ring called a **porphyrin**; chlorophyll is the same ring around magnesium; vitamin B₁₂ is cobalt. The enzyme nitrogenase, which bacteria use to do at room temperature what Haber–Bosch does at 450 °C, has an iron–molybdenum cluster at its heart that chemists have spent fifty years trying to imitate.[^4] And in 1965 Barnett Rosenberg noticed that platinum electrodes stopped bacteria dividing; the platinum complex responsible, **cisplatin**, became one of the most widely used anticancer drugs.[^5]

## Rock, glass, and concrete

Nine tenths of the Earth's crust is **silicates**: silicon and oxygen, with silicon at the center of a tetrahedron of four oxygens, the SiO₄ unit linked into chains, sheets, and frameworks. Quartz is a pure three-dimensional network of them; mica is sheets, which is why it peels; asbestos is chains, which is why it is fibrous. Clay is sheets that hold water between them, which is why it is plastic when wet and hard when fired into **ceramic**.

Melt silica with soda and lime and cool it too fast to crystallize and you get **glass**: a frozen liquid with no lattice, transparent because it has no grain boundaries to scatter light and no electronic transitions in the visible range (chapter 6). Add boron oxide and the glass expands less when heated, so it does not crack in the oven: borosilicate, sold as Pyrex from 1915. Add lead and it sparkles; add tiny amounts of transition metals and it takes on color, cobalt for blue, chromium for green, gold nanoparticles for ruby red.

**Cement** is limestone and clay roasted together to make calcium silicates; add water and they hydrate into an interlocking mass of crystals that binds sand and gravel into **concrete**. The Romans made it; the modern version dates from 1824. The world pours about four billion tonnes a year, and because roasting limestone drives off carbon dioxide, cement alone accounts for roughly 7 to 8 percent of global emissions, one of the hardest problems in industrial decarbonization.[^6]

## Sand into circuits

Silicon is a **semiconductor**: it conducts electricity poorly on its own, but sprinkle in one foreign atom per million, a process called **doping**, and its conductivity can be tuned over a millionfold range (the physics is in the [physics guide](#/physics/matter)). Phosphorus, with one more electron than silicon, donates electrons; boron, with one fewer, leaves holes. A junction between the two is a transistor.

The chemistry that makes this possible is purity. Chip-grade silicon is nine-nines pure, one foreign atom in a billion,[^14] achieved by converting sand to a volatile silicon compound, distilling it, decomposing it back to silicon, and then pulling a single crystal from the melt by a method Jan Czochralski discovered in 1916 when he accidentally dipped his pen into molten tin instead of his inkwell.[^7] The circuits themselves are then etched into the crystal by photochemistry, layer by layer, with features now smaller than a virus. A chip is the most chemically demanding object humans make.

## Small enough to be strange

Below about ten nanometers, a particle stops behaving like a lump of bulk material and starts behaving like a very large molecule. This is the domain of **nanomaterials**, and its clearest example is the **quantum dot**.

A quantum dot is a crystal of a semiconductor, such as cadmium selenide, a few nanometers across. Shine light on it and it glows, and the color depends on its size alone: larger dots glow red, smaller ones green, smaller still blue, all from the same material. The reason is the particle-in-a-box effect from quantum mechanics: confining an electron to a smaller space raises the energy it can have, and therefore the energy of the light it emits. Alexei Ekimov saw the effect in glass in 1981, Louis Brus in solutions in 1983, and Moungi Bawendi found in 1993 how to make dots of uniform size, which made them useful. The three shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; quantum dots are now in television screens and in dyes that light up tumors for surgeons.[^8]

Carbon's nanomaterials, fullerenes, nanotubes, and graphene, appeared in chapter 7. Graphene is the strongest material ever measured and conducts electricity better than copper; nanotubes are its rolled-up cousins. Their promise has been slow to turn into products, but they are appearing in composites, sensors, and batteries.

## Crystalline sponges

The newest class of designed materials is the **metal-organic framework**, or MOF: metal ions linked by rigid organic molecules into an open, crystalline scaffold that is mostly empty space. Richard Robson showed in 1989 that such frameworks could be built deliberately; Susumu Kitagawa showed in 1997 that gases could flow in and out of them; and Omar Yaghi in 1999 made MOF-5, which was stable when emptied and had an internal surface area of nearly 3,000 square meters per gram. A single gram, unfolded, would cover half a football field; the record frameworks since have doubled that. The three shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.[^9]

Because the metal and the linker can each be chosen from a vast menu, MOFs can be tailored to hold a particular gas: hydrogen for fuel, methane for vehicles, carbon dioxide pulled straight from the air, or water vapor harvested from desert air and released by sunlight (see Latest Research). Tens of thousands of distinct structures have been reported and catalogued.[^13] Their older cousins, the **zeolites**, are aluminosilicate frameworks with pores of molecular size that have been used since the 1960s to crack oil, soften water, and separate gases; the MOF is the zeolite made programmable.

## Materials for energy

The energy transition is a materials problem. Every electric car runs on the intercalation chemistry of chapter 8, and every improvement, higher-capacity anodes, solid electrolytes, cobalt-free cathodes, sodium instead of lithium, is inorganic chemistry. **Perovskite** solar cells, based on a class of crystal structures first described in 1839 and turned into a solar absorber only in 2009, went from 4 percent efficiency to over 26 percent in fifteen years, faster than any solar technology before them; the challenge is stability.[^10] The strongest permanent magnets, neodymium-iron-boron, discovered in 1984, sit inside every wind turbine and electric motor and have made the rare-earth elements a matter of geopolitics.[^11] High-temperature superconductors (see the [physics guide](#/physics/matter)) are copper oxides and now nickel oxides, discovered by inorganic chemists trying substitutions.

:::frontier
Two frontiers dominate. One is capture: MOFs and related frameworks that pull carbon dioxide from air cheaply enough to matter, and cements that emit less. The other is discovery by machine, where a widely publicized 2023 claim of millions of new crystals did not survive independent scrutiny; chapter 16 tells that story and draws the lesson. The tools are real and improving, but deciding which materials are worth making, and proving they are what they seem, is still done by people. The Latest Research section tracks both.
:::

## Summary

- Metals are won from ores by reduction; alloys are stronger because foreign atoms pin the sliding planes.
- Transition metal ions carry ligands in fixed geometries (Werner); the splitting of their d orbitals by those ligands explains their colors and magnetism, and life's iron, magnesium, and cobalt centers.
- Silicates make rock, glass, ceramic, and cement; cement is 7 to 8 percent of global emissions.
- Chip-grade silicon is nine-nines pure and doped one atom in a million.
- Below ten nanometers, size sets properties: quantum dots glow by color according to size.
- Metal-organic frameworks are programmable crystalline sponges (Nobel Prize 2025); batteries, perovskites, and magnets make the energy transition a chemistry problem.
