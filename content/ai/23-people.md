## How to use this chapter

Machine learning is young enough that most of its founders are alive and many are still working, which makes the roster unusual: it includes people whose most important work may be ahead of them. Each entry gives the central contribution and the chapter where it appears. Where credit is shared or contested, the entry says so. The Turing Award, computing's highest honor, is noted; so are the 2024 Nobel Prizes, the first given for this work.

## The founders (1940s–1960s)

**Warren McCulloch** (1898–1969) and **Walter Pitts** (1923–1969). The 1943 paper showing that networks of simplified neurons can compute any logical function. Pitts was a runaway teenager when they met. Chapters 2, 9.

**Alan Turing** (1912–1954). Defined computation (1936), asked whether machines can think and proposed the imitation game (1950), and predicted learning machines. Chapter 2.

**Arthur Samuel** (1901–1990). The checkers program (1959) that learned from self-play and beat its author; coined "machine learning." Chapters 1, 14.

**Frank Rosenblatt** (1928–1971). The perceptron (1958), the first learning machine with a proof of convergence, and the first overpromise. Died in a boating accident at 43. Chapters 2, 9.

**John McCarthy** (1927–2011). Organized the 1956 Dartmouth workshop and coined "artificial intelligence"; invented the Lisp language. Chapter 2.

**Marvin Minsky** (1927–2016) and **Seymour Papert** (1928–2016). *Perceptrons* (1969), the rigorous critique that redirected funding away from neural networks for fifteen years. Minsky co-founded the MIT AI Lab. Chapters 2, 9.

**Richard Bellman** (1920–1984). Dynamic programming and the equation that defines the value of a state in terms of its successors (1957). Chapter 14.

## The statistical foundation

**Adrien-Marie Legendre** (1752–1833) and **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777–1855). Least squares (1805, 1809), the first fitting algorithm. Chapter 4.

**Thomas Bayes** (1701–1761). The theorem behind naive Bayes and every probabilistic model. Chapter 5.

**Ronald Fisher** (1890–1962). Maximum likelihood, the principle under every loss function. Chapter 8.

**David Cox** (1924–2022). Logistic regression (1958). Chapter 4.

**Leo Breiman** (1928–2005). Classification and regression trees (1984), bagging (1996), random forests (2001), and the 2001 essay on the "two cultures" of statistics and prediction. Chapters 6, 8.

**Vladimir Vapnik** (b. 1936). Statistical learning theory and, with Corinna Cortes, the support vector machine (1995): the first rigorous account of when learning generalizes. Chapters 2, 6.

**Jerome Friedman** (b. 1939). Gradient boosting (2001) and, with Hastie and Tibshirani, the textbook that taught the field. Chapter 6.

**Robert Tibshirani** (b. 1956). The lasso (1996). Chapter 4.

**Judea Pearl** (b. 1936). Bayesian networks and the mathematics of causation. Turing Award 2011. Chapter 8.

## The connectionists (1970s–2000s)

**Paul Werbos** (b. 1947) and **Seppo Linnainmaa** (b. 1945). Early formulations of backpropagation (1974, 1970), unnoticed by the field. Chapter 2.

**David Rumelhart** (1942–2011). Led the 1986 paper that made backpropagation the standard and the *Parallel Distributed Processing* volumes that revived connectionism. Chapters 2, 10.

**Geoffrey Hinton** (b. 1947). Backpropagation (1986), Boltzmann machines, dropout, deep belief networks, and the 2012 ImageNet result from his lab; kept the field alive through two winters. Turing Award 2018; Nobel Prize in Physics 2024. Left Google in 2023 to warn of risks. Chapters 2, 9, 10, 11.

**Yann LeCun** (b. 1960). Convolutional networks (1989), applied to zip codes and checks; chief AI scientist at Meta; skeptic of loss-of-control risk. Turing Award 2018. Chapters 2, 11.

**Yoshua Bengio** (b. 1964). Neural language models, attention (with Bahdanau and Cho, 2014), GANs (as advisor), and the vanishing-gradient analysis; chaired the International AI Safety Reports. Turing Award 2018. Chapters 10, 12, 21.

**Jürgen Schmidhuber** (b. 1963) and **Sepp Hochreiter** (b. 1967). Long short-term memory (1997), which ran language technology from 2014 to 2018. Chapter 12.

**John Hopfield** (b. 1933). The associative memory network (1982) that connected neural networks to statistical physics. Nobel Prize in Physics 2024. Chapter 9.

**Richard Sutton** (b. 1957) and **Andrew Barto** (b. 1948). Temporal-difference learning (1988) and the foundations of reinforcement learning; authors of its standard text. Turing Award 2024. Chapter 14.

**Fei-Fei Li** (b. 1976). ImageNet (2009), the dataset that made the 2012 breakthrough possible. Chapters 1, 11.

## The deep learning era (2010s)

**Alex Krizhevsky** (b. 1986) and **Ilya Sutskever** (b. 1986). AlexNet (2012), the result that started the deep learning era. Sutskever co-founded OpenAI (2015), led its research, and left in 2024 to found a safety-focused lab. Chapters 2, 11.

**Ian Goodfellow** (b. 1987). Generative adversarial networks (2014); the standard deep learning textbook. Chapter 13.

**Kaiming He** (b. 1984). Residual networks (2015), the most cited paper of the era. Chapter 10.

**Diederik Kingma** (b. 1987). The variational autoencoder (2013) and the Adam optimizer (2014). Chapters 10, 13.

**Tomas Mikolov** (b. 1982). word2vec (2013): meaning as vector arithmetic. Chapter 12.

**Demis Hassabis** (b. 1976) co-founded DeepMind in 2010 with Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman and has led it since; **David Silver** (b. 1976) led its reinforcement-learning research: AlphaGo (2016), AlphaZero (2017). Hassabis shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2024 with **John Jumper** (b. 1985), who led AlphaFold. Chapters 14, 22.

**Ashish Vaswani**, **Noam Shazeer** (b. 1976), **Jakob Uszkoreit**, and five co-authors. "Attention Is All You Need" (2017), the transformer. All eight have since left Google, most to found companies. Chapter 15.

**Dzmitry Bahdanau** and **Kyunghyun Cho** (b. 1985). Attention for translation (2014), the idea the transformer generalized. Chapter 12.

**Jonathan Ho** and colleagues. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (2020), the method behind image generators. Chapter 13.

**Alec Radford** (b. 1993). GPT-1 (2018), GPT-2 (2019), CLIP (2021): the papers that established the generative pretraining path at OpenAI. Chapters 11, 15.

## The language model era (2020s)

**Jared Kaplan** (b. 1984). Scaling laws (2020); co-founded Anthropic. Chapter 16.

**Tom Brown** and the GPT-3 team. "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (2020), in-context learning. Chapter 16.

**Dario Amodei** (b. 1983) and **Daniela Amodei**. Led safety and scaling research at OpenAI; co-founded Anthropic (2021). Chapters 17, 21.

**Sam Altman** (b. 1985). CEO of OpenAI; released ChatGPT (2022) and led the company through its 2023 governance crisis. Chapter 2.

**Paul Christiano** (b. 1988). Reinforcement learning from human preferences (2017), the technique behind RLHF; founded the Alignment Research Center; since 2024 head of safety at the US government's AI standards institute. Chapters 14, 17.

**Long Ouyang** and the InstructGPT team. RLHF applied to language models (2022), the step that made ChatGPT. Chapter 17.

**Yuntao Bai**, **Amanda Askell**, and colleagues. Constitutional AI (2022). Chapter 17.

**Chris Olah** (b. 1992). Feature visualization, circuits, and the mechanistic interpretability program; co-founded Anthropic. Chapters 11, 19.

**Emily Bender** (b. 1973) and **Timnit Gebru** (b. 1983). "Stochastic parrots" (2021); Gebru's departure from Google over the paper became a landmark in AI ethics. Chapters 19, 21.

**Joy Buolamwini** (b. 1989). "Gender Shades" (2018, with Gebru), the audit that documented facial-recognition error rates up to 35 percent for dark-skinned women; founded the Algorithmic Justice League. Chapters 5, 21.

**Liang Wenfeng** (b. 1985). Founded DeepSeek; its R1 model (2025) showed reasoning emerging from pure reinforcement learning and matched the frontier at low reported cost. Chapters 14, 17.

**Erik Brynjolfsson** (b. 1962). The controlled studies of generative AI's productivity effects and the "canaries" payroll data on entry-level employment. Chapters 18, 21.

## The pattern

Three things stand out. The first is persistence against consensus: the connectionists were told for twenty years that their approach was dead, and the three who kept working, Hinton, LeCun, and Bengio, received the Turing Award together for not stopping. The second is how much of the decisive work was done by graduate students and researchers in their twenties: Pitts, Rosenblatt, Krizhevsky, Goodfellow, Kingma, Radford. The third is the move from universities to companies. Nearly every name in the last two sections works at or founded a corporation, the frontier is now closed to academic labs by cost, and the field's founders are divided, sometimes bitterly, about whether what they built is safe. That division, between Hinton and Bengio warning of catastrophe and LeCun calling the warnings absurd, is the roster's most important fact about the present.
