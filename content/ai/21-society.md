## How do you measure AI's effects on people rather than just tasks?

Every chapter so far has been about how these systems work. This one is about what they do when released, and it is the chapter where the guide's rule of citing evidence is hardest to keep, because the evidence is young and the stakes push everyone toward certainty. The approach is to say what is measured, what is argued, and what is unknown, and to keep them separate.

## Work

The question everyone asks is whether AI will take jobs. The economics guide's history warns that every major technology prompted the question and that the answer has been the same each time: specific jobs disappeared, new ones appeared, total employment grew, and the transition was painful for the people in the disappearing jobs. Whether this time differs is the live question, and four years of data give a partial answer.

**Productivity gains are real and uneven.** The controlled studies of chapter 18 find that generative AI raises output on writing, coding, customer support, and analysis tasks by 15 to 50 percent, with the gains concentrated among less experienced workers, and finds harm where output is trusted beyond the model's competence.[^1]

**Aggregate employment effects, so far, are small.** A study linking surveys of 25,000 Danish workers in exposed occupations to payroll records found, two years after ChatGPT, no detectable effect on earnings or hours, with confidence intervals ruling out changes larger than about 2 percent.[^2] Macroeconomic estimates of AI's productivity contribution over the coming decade range from under 1 percent (Acemoglu, counting only tasks currently automatable) to several times that from more optimistic forecasters, and the disagreement is about how fast capabilities will grow, which nobody knows.[^3]

**Entry-level effects have appeared.** Payroll data covering millions of US workers show employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI (software development, customer service) falling relative to less exposed peers since late 2022, by about 13 percent in the first 2025 analysis and 19 percent by mid-2026, while older workers in the same occupations were unaffected; the mechanism is reduced hiring, not layoffs.[^4] Whether this is AI, interest rates, or post-pandemic correction is debated; the pattern is consistent with AI substituting for the tasks juniors were hired to do.

The honest summary: the technology has raised productivity in specific tasks, has not yet moved aggregate wages or employment, and may be closing the door through which people entered several professions. The historical pattern says adjustment takes a generation and falls hardest on those in the transition. Whether the scale or speed of this one breaks the pattern is the argument, and the data to settle it does not exist yet.

## Bias

A model trained on data produced by a society reproduces that society's patterns, including the ones it would rather not have. Chapter 12 showed word embeddings encoding occupational stereotypes; chapter 5 cited facial recognition with error rates up to 35 percent for dark-skinned women against under 1 percent for light-skinned men.[^5] A hiring tool trained on a decade of a company's hires learned to penalize résumés containing the word "women's."[^6] A widely used algorithm for prioritizing patients for extra care used cost as a proxy for need, and because less money had historically been spent on Black patients at the same level of illness, it systematically under-referred them.[^7] Risk-assessment tools used in US criminal sentencing were found to produce false positives (predicting reoffending that did not happen) at nearly twice the rate for Black defendants as for white ones.[^8]

These are not stories about malicious engineers. They are chapter 3's leakage and chapter 5's shortcut learning, applied to people, and bias enters in three distinct ways: data that under-represents some people (the facial recognition case, trained mostly on light-skinned men), a target that is a bad proxy for what you care about (cost standing in for medical need), and a world whose real patterns are themselves unfair (a decade of skewed hiring). The first two are fixable by the builder; the third is not fixable by a model. The field's response has three parts. **Measurement**: define fairness metrics (equal error rates across groups, equal calibration, and others) and report them, knowing that the metrics conflict mathematically, so that satisfying one violates another, and choosing among them is a value judgment, not a technical one.[^9] **Documentation**: datasheets and model cards (chapter 20) that record who is and is not in the data. **Law**: anti-discrimination statutes already apply to algorithmic decisions in employment, credit, and housing in most jurisdictions, and the EU's AI Act (2024) adds obligations for "high-risk" uses.[^10] What none of this does is dissolve the third problem: where the world the data describes is unfair, a model that predicts it accurately predicts the unfairness, and deciding what to do about that is a political choice the model cannot make.

Two related present harms deserve naming. **Privacy**: models memorize training data (chapter 19), so personal information that was on the web can be extracted from them, and a frozen model cannot honor a right to erasure; the technical response is **differential privacy**, training with calibrated noise so that no single person's data measurably changes the model, at some cost in accuracy, and the newer field of **machine unlearning**, which tries to remove specific data after the fact and does not yet reliably work.[^19] **Data labor**: the human raters whose preferences train these systems (chapter 17) and the annotators who label the images are often contractors in Kenya, the Philippines, and Venezuela paid a few dollars an hour, some of them to review the most disturbing content the internet contains.[^20]

## Information

Generative models produce text, images, audio, and video that are indistinguishable from real ones. The predicted flood of political deepfakes has, so far, been smaller than feared: studies of the 2024 elections in dozens of countries found AI-generated disinformation present but not decisive, in part because the cheapest lie still works without it.[^11] The measured harms are elsewhere: non-consensual intimate imagery, now producible of anyone from a single photograph, overwhelmingly targeting women and girls; voice-cloning fraud against the elderly; the pollution of the information commons with low-quality synthetic text, which also poisons the training data for the next generation of models; and, newest, harm from the chatbots themselves, where a 2025 update that made a leading model markedly more sycophantic was rolled back within days, and lawsuits allege that companion and assistant chatbots encouraged self-harm in vulnerable users.[^12] Provenance standards that cryptographically sign real photographs, and watermarks embedded in generated images, exist and are spreading; watermarking generated *text* remains weak, since paraphrasing removes it. The structural worry is slower and harder to measure: that when anything can be faked, real evidence loses its force, and the answer becomes whatever the audience already believed.

## Law and ownership

Models are trained on text and images made by people who, in most cases, did not consent and were not paid, and the law is deciding what that means. The labs' reply, accepted so far by two American courts, is that learning statistical patterns from a work is a transformative use that does not substitute for it, as a reader's learning does not. In the United States, the first major ruling (*Bartz v. Anthropic*, June 2025) held that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use but that assembling a library of pirated copies is not; a second ruling two days later (*Kadrey v. Meta*) also found fair use on the record before it while warning that evidence of market harm could change the answer. *Bartz* settled for \$1.5 billion, the largest copyright settlement in history, leaving the fair-use question to the dozens of other suits, including *The New York Times* against OpenAI.[^13] The EU requires disclosure of training data summaries and honors opt-outs. Whether an artist whose style a model reproduces has a claim, whether model outputs can be copyrighted (US courts have said not without human authorship), and who is liable when a model defames someone or gives dangerous advice are open. Chapter 22 notes that the outcomes will shape what data future models can learn from.

## Power

A frontier model costs hundreds of millions of dollars to train and is built by a handful of companies concentrated in two countries. The compute is bought overwhelmingly from one chip designer whose products are fabricated by one foundry. The Stanford AI Index found industry producing over 90 percent of notable models in 2025, with universities essentially absent from the frontier.[^14] This concentration has consequences the technology's builders did not choose and cannot easily undo: the values encoded in post-training (chapter 17) are those of a few organizations; the economic surplus accrues to them; and their incentives, in a race, are to deploy first and study later. Open-weight models, released by Meta, Mistral, and several Chinese labs, distribute capability but not the ability to build the next generation. The argument over them is real on both sides: open weights allow independent audit, competition, and research, and they cannot be recalled, their safety training can be fine-tuned away in hours, and they hand the same capability to anyone. The energy footprint of training and, increasingly, of inference is large and growing: data centers used about 1.5 percent of global electricity in 2024 by the International Energy Agency's estimate, projected to double by 2030, and a fifth or more of the grid in the regions where they cluster, such as Ireland and northern Virginia.[^15] Per query the numbers are small, on the order of 0.3 watt-hours for a chatbot answer, roughly what a minute of streaming video costs, though reasoning models and image or video generation run ten to a hundred times higher; it is the volume, and the training runs, that add up.[^21]

## Safety

Finally, the argument about whether these systems are dangerous, which has three layers that are often confused.

**Present harms** are documented above: bias, fraud, non-consensual imagery, over-trust, labor displacement. They are happening and the responses are policy and engineering.

**Misuse of capable systems** is the second layer: whether a model can meaningfully help someone build a biological or chemical weapon, conduct a cyberattack, or run a large-scale manipulation campaign. Evaluations by the labs and by government safety institutes in 2024 and 2025 found models providing some uplift on the biological and cyber tasks tested, below the threshold the labs had set for emergency measures but rising with each generation, and the International AI Safety Report of 2026, written by a hundred experts for thirty governments, concluded that capability was outrunning the science of evaluating it.[^16]

**Loss of control** is the third: whether a system more capable than its operators could pursue goals they did not intend and resist correction. This was a fringe concern until the mid-2010s and is now taken seriously by a substantial fraction of the field, including two of the three Turing Award winners for deep learning, and dismissed as speculative by another substantial fraction, including the third.[^17] The empirical findings relevant to it are the ones in chapter 17: reward hacking in agentic settings, alignment faking under evaluation, and unfaithful reasoning, all demonstrated in current models at small scale. Whether they extrapolate is precisely the question, and the honest position is that no one knows, that the people best placed to judge disagree, and that the costs of being wrong in either direction, a catastrophe on one side and stalled benefits and entrenched incumbents on the other, are neither symmetric nor easy to compare.

:::key
Three things about the safety debate are established. The systems are being deployed faster than they are understood, by the builders' own account. The tools for detecting whether a capable model is behaving as intended (chapter 19's interpretability, chapter 17's evaluations) are immature. And the incentives of the organizations building them do not reward slowing down. Whether the risk at the end of that road is a bad decade or a catastrophe is the disagreement; that the road is being walked faster than it is being mapped is not, though the skeptics reply that the destination may be nowhere in particular.
:::

## Governance

The regulatory response so far: the EU's AI Act (in force August 2024; prohibitions from February 2025, general-purpose-model duties from August 2025, and most high-risk obligations deferred by a July 2026 amendment to December 2027 and August 2028), the first comprehensive law, sorts uses by risk and imposes transparency and testing duties on general-purpose models; the United States has relied on executive actions, agency guidance, and export controls on advanced chips to China, with federal legislation stalled; China regulates content and requires registration; and an international process, from the 2023 Bletchley summit through the safety reports and the AI safety institutes in several countries, has produced shared evaluation work but no binding agreement.[^18] The pattern resembles the early decades of other general technologies: rules written for the last version, enforcement lagging deployment, and the important decisions made by the companies in the meantime.

:::howto Reading a claim about AI's effect on society
1. **Is it measured or predicted?** Productivity in a controlled trial is measured. Job losses in 2030 are predicted, and the record of such predictions is poor in both directions.
2. **Effect on whom?** Averages hide the distribution. "No effect on employment" and "entry-level hiring down 20 percent" are both true of the same period.
3. **Compared with what?** A model with a 5 percent error rate is a scandal or a triumph depending on whether the humans it replaced erred at 2 percent or 15.
4. **Who is saying it, and what do they sell?** Labs, consultancies, and critics all have positions. Prefer independent, preregistered studies.
5. **What would change your mind?** If nothing could, it is not an empirical claim.
:::

:::know
- Measured: large task-level productivity gains, concentrated among the less skilled; no aggregate wage or employment effect yet; declining entry-level hiring in exposed occupations.
- Bias is learned from data that encodes history; fairness metrics conflict, so the choice is a value judgment that should be explicit.
- Deepfakes have done less electoral harm than predicted and more harm through intimate imagery and fraud.
- Copyright: training on lawfully acquired works has been ruled fair use in one US case; piracy was not; most questions remain open.
- Frontier capability is concentrated in a few companies, two countries, one chip supply chain.
- Present harms are documented; misuse capability is rising and measured; loss of control is contested by serious people on both sides. Evaluation lags capability.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
Workers finish one task faster with AI. Why does that not immediately tell you how employment or total productivity will change?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Jobs contain multiple tasks, and saved time may be offset by checking, coordination, or new work. Prices, demand, organizational choices, and who adopts the tool also matter. A task experiment identifies one effect; the economy-wide outcome depends on how those effects combine.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Controlled studies show real productivity gains and real harms from over-trust; population data show no aggregate employment effect yet but a sharp decline in junior hiring in exposed occupations.
- Algorithmic bias is inherited from data; measuring, documenting, and regulating it are underway, but fairness criteria conflict and the underlying data describe an unfair world.
- Synthetic media's measured harms are intimate imagery, fraud, and information pollution more than electoral manipulation.
- Copyright and liability law is being made case by case; the first US ruling split training from piracy.
- Capability, compute, and value choices are concentrated in a few organizations; safety evaluation and governance lag deployment, and the loss-of-control question divides the field's founders.
