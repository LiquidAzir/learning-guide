## A world with your eyes closed

During a vivid dream, an experience unfolds while you are disconnected from much of the surrounding room. Conversely, someone can be awake yet unable to carry out an instruction. Consciousness cannot be read directly from whether the eyes are open or a hand moves.

**Arousal** concerns the level and regulation of wakefulness. **Awareness** concerns experience of oneself or the environment. **Responsiveness** is an observable capacity to react. Their relationships are informative, but none is a perfect substitute for the others.

## Sleep is organized activity

Sleep includes REM and non-REM stages, identified using combinations of brain activity, eye movements, and muscle activity. Non-REM sleep includes coordinated slow oscillations, spindles, and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples. These events are investigated for their roles in communication between memory systems. Dream reports occur in both REM and non-REM sleep, so REM should not be equated with the presence of all experience.

Two interacting processes help organize sleep timing: a circadian system responsive to cues such as light, and sleep pressure related to prior wakefulness. The interaction explains why an exhausted person can sometimes feel temporarily more alert at a particular time of day. It also shows why time in bed alone is an incomplete account of sleep regulation.

A mouse study used pupil changes to identify substates within non-REM sleep and linked them to replay of newer or older memories. Targeted disruption provided evidence beyond a simple correlation. It does not establish that manipulating human pupils would improve memory.[^1]

## The challenge of a silent patient

Clinical assessments must distinguish lack of response from lack of experience. Researchers sometimes ask patients to perform mental tasks and look for task-specific brain activity. A reliable instructed pattern can be important evidence of preserved cognitive capacity; a missing pattern may reflect hearing, language, attention, task difficulty, or measurement failure.[^2]

This is why consciousness measures need estimates of both false positives and false negatives. An appealing brain signature is not automatically a diagnostic tool. Anesthesia, sleep, severe injury, and seizures produce different physiological conditions; evidence from one cannot simply be pasted onto another.

:::try No button press
A participant gives no button response during an experiment. List two explanations besides absence of experience that the experiment must consider.

:::answer Show the reasoning
They may be unable to move, or they may not understand or remember the instruction. Other possibilities include inattention and equipment failure. A stronger design uses independent checks and, where appropriate, alternative ways to demonstrate comprehension or intentional response.
:::
