## Everyone else seems certain

In a meeting, everyone supports a plan you privately doubt. Their agreement could mean they know something you do not. It could also mean that each person is hiding similar doubts. Observing agreement alone does not distinguish informed consensus from shared silence.

**Informational influence** occurs when other people's judgments serve as evidence. **Normative influence** occurs when approval, belonging, or sanctions affect behavior. Both can operate at once. Anonymous voting and public discussion may therefore produce different outcomes without anyone's private evidence changing.

Classic conformity experiments made this distinction experimentally visible, but their settings should not be treated as exact miniature versions of every workplace or society.[^1] Social effects depend on relationships, incentives, culture, and what participants understand the situation to mean.

## Explaining an action

Someone responds curtly. Perhaps they are habitually unfriendly; perhaps they are under time pressure, in pain, or struggling to understand you. **Attribution** concerns how we infer causes of behavior. A single action usually underdetermines the explanation.

Repeated observations help: do they act similarly with other people, across settings, and when circumstances change? This is the same logic as experimental comparison. It does not require excusing harmful behavior; it improves the causal explanation of it.

**Theory of mind** refers to reasoning about other people's beliefs, desires, and knowledge. A person can act on a false belief. To predict their action, you may need to represent what they think rather than what you know. Performance on any one task also depends on language, attention, and familiarity.

## Cooperation has a structure

Suppose two people each choose whether to contribute to a shared task. Each contribution benefits both, while the effort cost falls on its contributor. The group may prefer mutual contribution, yet each individual has an incentive to let the other do the work. Repeated interaction, credible commitments, reputation, and fair monitoring can change those incentives.

Social identity can organize trust and obligation, but group membership does not dictate an individual's views. Treat a group average as a description of a distribution, with variation and context attached. Avoid replacing a psychological mechanism with a stereotype.

How could a meeting distinguish genuine agreement from people withholding doubts because they fear disapproval?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Collect independent, private judgments before discussion and compare them with public statements. Ask for reasons and uncertainty, not only yes/no votes. The comparison can expose social pressure, though anonymity and question wording must be credible.
:::

## Connect the scales

Social psychology explains mechanisms of influence and cooperation. [Political institutions](#/politics) organize those mechanisms through rules, offices, and accountability. Neither level makes the other unnecessary.
