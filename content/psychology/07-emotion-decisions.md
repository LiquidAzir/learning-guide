## The same risk feels different

A difficult decision looks manageable after breakfast and disastrous after a sleepless night. The probabilities may be unchanged, but the state of the decision-maker is not. A theory of choice needs more than a spreadsheet of outcomes.

**Emotion** involves coordinated changes in appraisal, physiology, attention, action tendencies, and experience. Researchers disagree about how best to divide emotions into categories and how those components produce particular experiences. A facial expression alone is not an infallible readout of an inner state.[^1]

**Motivation** concerns the processes that energize and direct action. Hunger changes the value of food; a deadline changes the cost of delay; a social audience changes the consequences of an error. These influences need not be irrational. A system that ignored current needs would make poor decisions too.

## Value, uncertainty, and learning

Expected value multiplies each outcome by its probability and sums the products. A 50% chance of 20 points has expected value 10 points. But a person's utility for points may be nonlinear, and uncertainty about the probabilities introduces another problem. Two people can choose differently without either having made an arithmetic error.

**Loss aversion** describes greater sensitivity to losses than comparable gains around a reference point in some choice settings. It is not a fixed law governing every person and task. Framing, experience, stakes, and the reference point affect the interpretation. The [economics chapter on choice](#/economics/choice) develops related models.[^2]

The brain's reward systems distinguish learning, wanting, and pleasurable experience more than the phrase “dopamine equals pleasure” suggests. Dopamine signals can relate to prediction errors and action, depending on the neurons and task. A chemical's involvement in a process does not make it the process's single cause.

## Two speeds are not two little people

“Fast” and “slow” thinking are useful descriptions of contrasting processing characteristics. They should not be mistaken for two anatomically separate agents inside the skull. Deliberation can correct an intuitive mistake, but it can also construct a sophisticated defense of a favored conclusion.

A good decision procedure makes the comparison explicit: options, outcomes, probabilities, reversibility, and what information would change the choice. It separates the quality of the decision from the luck of its result.

:::try Good choice, bad outcome
You select a route with a 90% chance of arriving on time rather than one with a 60% chance. An unusual breakdown makes you late. Does lateness prove the choice was poor?

:::answer Show the reasoning
No. A well-supported probabilistic decision can have an unfavorable outcome. Reassess whether the probabilities were justified and whether new evidence changes them. Do not judge the procedure solely by one realization.
:::
