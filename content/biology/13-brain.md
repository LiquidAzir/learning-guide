## Recap

Chapter 12 listed the nervous system among the body's systems and deferred it. This chapter takes it on: the mechanism at the cellular level, which is well understood, the systems level, which is partly understood, and consciousness, which is not.

## The neuron

The brain has about 86 billion **neurons** and a similar number of glial cells, the support cells once thought to outnumber neurons ten to one, a figure corrected in 2009 by counting rather than estimating.[^1] A neuron receives signals through branching **dendrites**, integrates them in its cell body, and sends its own signal down a single **axon**, which may be a millimeter long or, for the nerve running from your spine to your foot, a meter. A typical cortical neuron makes several thousand connections; the total is on the order of 100 trillion.

The signal is electrical, and its mechanism was worked out by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley on the giant axon of the squid, in work interrupted by the Second World War and published in 1952. A resting neuron pumps sodium out and potassium in, so the inside sits at about −70 millivolts relative to the outside. If input pushes the voltage past a threshold of about −55 mV, voltage-sensitive sodium channels snap open, sodium floods in, and the voltage swings positive within a millisecond; the channels then close, potassium channels open, and the voltage crashes back. That spike, the **action potential**, propagates down the axon at 1 to 100 meters per second, faster in axons wrapped in the fatty insulation called **myelin**, and it is all-or-nothing: a stronger stimulus produces more spikes per second, not bigger ones.[^2] Hodgkin and Huxley described this with four differential equations that predicted the spike's shape quantitatively and won the 1963 Nobel Prize; they remain the foundation of computational neuroscience, and they are the ancestor of the artificial neuron in the AI guide, which kept the summation and threshold and dropped everything else.

At the axon's end, the spike triggers release of **neurotransmitters** into the **synapse**, a gap of about 20 nanometers, where they bind receptors on the next cell and make it more or less likely to fire. Glutamate excites, GABA inhibits, and these two do most of the traffic; dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, and acetylcholine modulate. Nearly every psychiatric and recreational drug acts here: SSRIs block serotonin's reuptake, benzodiazepines enhance GABA, cocaine blocks dopamine reuptake, opioids activate receptors for the body's own painkillers, and nicotine mimics acetylcholine.

## How memory is stored

The rule was guessed before it was found. Donald Hebb proposed in 1949 that when one neuron repeatedly helps fire another, the connection between them strengthens, usually paraphrased as "cells that fire together wire together." In 1973 Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo found the physical version in the rabbit hippocampus: a burst of stimulation strengthened a synapse for hours or longer, **long-term potentiation**.[^3] The mechanism involves a receptor that opens only when the cell is already depolarized and glutamate is present, so it acts as a coincidence detector, and the strengthening involves adding more receptors and, over time, growing the synapse. Memory, at the physical level, is a pattern of synaptic weights.

Memory divides into kinds with different anatomy. **Working memory** holds a few items for seconds in prefrontal circuits. **Episodic memory** for events requires the **hippocampus** to form but not to store: this was learned from the patient Henry Molaison, whose hippocampi were removed in 1953 to treat epilepsy and who from then until his death in 2008 could form no new conscious memories, while retaining his childhood memories, his vocabulary, and, crucially, the ability to learn new motor skills without any awareness of having practiced them.[^4] That dissociation established that memory is not one system. **Procedural memory** for skills lives in the basal ganglia and cerebellum; **semantic memory** for facts is distributed in the cortex.

Memory is reconstructive, not a recording. Every recall re-encodes, and details can be altered or inserted; Elizabeth Loftus's experiments implanted entirely false childhood memories in a substantial minority of subjects, and the same mechanism produces confident, wrong eyewitness testimony, which DNA exoneration cases later confirmed on a large scale.[^5]

## Maps of the brain

The cortex, the folded outer sheet, is organized by function: a strip for movement and behind it one for touch, each mapped onto the body with area proportional to precision rather than size, so hands and lips take up disproportionate space. At the back is vision, processed in a hierarchy from edges and motion to objects and faces. Language sits mostly in the left hemisphere in most people, with production near Broca's area and comprehension near Wernicke's, though the classical two-box model is now known to be much too simple. Beneath the cortex are the older structures: the thalamus (relay), hypothalamus (homeostasis, chapter 12), amygdala (threat and emotional salience), basal ganglia (action selection and habit), and cerebellum (coordination and timing, with more neurons than the rest of the brain combined).[^6]

The methods used to draw these maps each have limits. **Lesions**, natural or surgical, show what a region is necessary for. **Electrodes** record single cells, precisely but sparsely. **fMRI** measures blood oxygenation as a proxy for activity, over seconds, in blocks of a few cubic millimeters containing hundreds of thousands of neurons; it is correlational, its statistics were shown in 2016 to have been too liberal in a large share of published studies, and it famously produced "activation" in a dead salmon when uncorrected.[^7] Nothing in a brain scan shows a thought, a lie, or love; it shows where blood went, on average, in a group, compared to another condition.

Two recent efforts change the scale. In 2024 the FlyWire project published the complete wiring diagram of an adult fruit fly brain: 139,255 neurons and about 54.5 million connections, the first whole-brain **connectome** of an adult animal. In 2025 the MICrONS consortium published a cubic millimeter of mouse visual cortex, about 200,000 cells and 500 million synapses, with the activity of the same neurons recorded while the mouse watched movies.[^8] A human brain is about a million times the volume of that cube, so a human connectome is not close; what these give is the first ground truth about how real circuits are wired.

## Consciousness

Everything above is mechanism. None of it explains why any of it is *like* anything: why the firing of neurons in your visual cortex is accompanied by the experience of red rather than by nothing at all. David Chalmers named this the **hard problem** in 1995, distinguishing it from the "easy" problems of explaining attention, report, and integration, which are hard in the ordinary sense but not mysterious in kind.[^9]

The main scientific theories: **global workspace theory** holds that consciousness is what happens when information is broadcast widely across the cortex, becoming available to many systems at once; **integrated information theory** holds that consciousness is intrinsic to any system with the right kind of integrated causal structure, and assigns a quantity to it. An adversarial collaboration, in which proponents of both agreed in advance on experiments that would count against their view, announced results in 2023 and published them in 2025; they fit neither theory cleanly.[^10] The same year, 124 researchers published a letter calling integrated information theory pseudoscience, and others defended it; the field is genuinely unsettled and its practitioners disagree about what would count as an answer.

What can be said: consciousness depends on the brain in detail (anesthesia, sleep, and damage abolish or alter it in specific ways); it is not located in one place; it is not the same as wakefulness, since patients can be awake and unaware and, in rare cases, aware and unresponsive, which bedside tests now sometimes detect. And nobody has a theory that would let you look at a physical system and say whether there is something it is like to be it. Anyone who tells you the problem is solved, in either the "it is obviously just computation" or the "it is obviously beyond science" direction, is ahead of the evidence.

:::key
The cellular mechanism of the brain is well understood: neurons integrate inputs, fire all-or-nothing spikes, and communicate at synapses whose strengths change with use, and that changing is memory. The systems level is partly understood: there are maps, hierarchies, and specialized regions, and the tools that reveal them are indirect and easy to over-read. The question of how any of this produces experience is not merely unsolved but lacks an agreed method of solution, and that is worth stating plainly in a chapter that otherwise sounds confident.
:::

## Summary

- The brain has about 86 billion neurons and roughly 100 trillion synapses, and runs on about 20 watts.
- Neurons signal with all-or-nothing action potentials, described quantitatively by Hodgkin and Huxley in 1952, and communicate by neurotransmitters at synapses, where nearly all psychoactive drugs act.
- Memory is stored as synaptic strengths (long-term potentiation, 1973) and comes in dissociable kinds, as patient H. M. showed; recall is reconstructive and false memories can be implanted.
- Cortical maps are drawn from lesions, electrodes, and fMRI, the last of which is indirect, coarse, and often over-interpreted; complete connectomes now exist for a fly brain and a cubic millimeter of mouse cortex.
- No theory explains why brain activity is accompanied by experience; the leading candidates were tested against each other in an adversarial collaboration announced in 2023 and published in 2025, and neither won.
