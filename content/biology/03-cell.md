## Recap

Chapter 2 traced the idea that all living things are made of cells and that every cell comes from another. This chapter opens one up. Everything in Part II happens inside the structure described here, so it is worth a slow read.

## The numbers

You are made of 30 to 37 trillion human cells, depending on how they are counted, and carry roughly 38 trillion bacteria, mostly in your gut: about one for one, not the ten to one of the old textbook claim, which was a guess that survived forty years before anyone checked.[^1] A typical human cell is about 10 to 20 micrometers across, a hundredth of a millimeter, small enough that a thousand fit across a fingernail. A bacterium is about a tenth of that in each dimension, a thousandth of the volume. Your body makes about 330 billion new cells a day, mostly blood and gut lining, and destroys about as many. It replaces most of its cells by number within a few months, though many tissues turn over slowly or not at all: fat, muscle, bone, the lens of the eye, and the neurons and much of the heart.

## What every cell has

Every cell, from a bacterium to a neuron, has four things.

A **membrane**: a double layer of fatty molecules (**phospholipids**), each with a water-loving head and two water-hating tails, that arrange themselves into a sheet two molecules thick, tails inward, because that is the lowest-energy arrangement in water. The membrane is what makes a cell a cell: a boundary that keeps the inside different from the outside. It is studded with **proteins** that act as gates, pumps, sensors, and anchors, and it is about 5 nanometers thick, so thin that in a cell 10 micrometers across it is proportionally thinner than the film of a soap bubble is to the bubble.[^2]

**Cytoplasm**: the crowded water inside. Not a dilute soup but a gel in which proteins are packed at concentrations near 300 milligrams per milliliter, jostling, with molecules of everything diffusing among them at speeds that let a small molecule cross a bacterium in a millisecond.

**DNA**: the instructions, as a long double-helical molecule (chapter 6). A bacterium's is one circular chromosome of a few million letters; a human cell's is 46 linear chromosomes holding two copies of a 3.1-billion-pair genome, about 6.2 billion pairs in all, which if straightened would run two meters and is packed into a nucleus 6 micrometers across.

**Ribosomes**: the machines that read genetic instructions and build proteins. A bacterium has about 20,000; a human liver cell, some millions. They are the most conserved structures in life, nearly identical in every organism, and their RNA is what Woese used to draw the tree of life (chapter 9).

## Two kinds of cell

Life divides, at the deepest level, into cells without a nucleus and cells with one.

**Prokaryotes** (bacteria and archaea) are small, usually single, and simple in plan: the DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, there are no internal compartments, and everything happens in one room. They make up in speed and numbers for what they lack in structure: a bacterium can divide every twenty minutes, and there are about 10^30 of them on Earth, more than the stars in the observable universe, with a total mass greater than all animals combined.[^3]

**Eukaryotes** (everything else: animals, plants, fungi, and a vast diversity of single-celled protists) are typically a thousand times larger by volume and divided into **organelles**, membrane-bounded compartments each doing a job. The **nucleus** holds the DNA behind a double membrane with gated pores, separating the storage of information from its use. The **endoplasmic reticulum** is a folded membrane factory where proteins destined for export or the membrane are made and lipids are synthesized; the **Golgi apparatus** sorts and ships them. **Lysosomes** are acid-filled bags that digest worn-out parts and swallowed material. **Mitochondria** burn sugar and fat with oxygen to make ATP, the cell's energy currency (chapter 5); a liver cell has a thousand or two. Plant cells add **chloroplasts**, which capture sunlight, a rigid **cell wall** of cellulose, and a large water-filled **vacuole** that gives the cell its pressure. Threading through all of it is the **cytoskeleton**, a scaffold of protein filaments that gives the cell shape, lets it move and divide, and serves as rails for motor proteins that haul cargo at about a micrometer per second.[^4]

## The merger

Mitochondria have their own DNA, their own ribosomes of the bacterial type, and a double membrane; they reproduce by splitting, like bacteria; and their genes are most similar to those of a group of free-living bacteria. In 1967 Lynn Sagan, later Lynn Margulis, argued, against considerable resistance, that they *are* bacteria: the descendants of a bacterium that was engulfed by another cell, on current estimates somewhere between 1.8 and 2.5 billion years ago, and, instead of being digested, stayed and paid rent in ATP.[^5] The same is true of chloroplasts, which descend from photosynthetic cyanobacteria captured roughly 1.5 billion years ago (estimates run from one to two billion). **Endosymbiosis**, one cell living inside another, is now the accepted origin of both, confirmed by their genomes; over time most of the bacterium's genes moved to the host nucleus, leaving the human mitochondrion with only 37.

The host, on current evidence, was an archaeon. In 2015 a group of archaea recovered from deep-sea sediments, the Asgard archaea, turned out to carry many genes previously thought unique to eukaryotes, and the first one grown in culture, reported in 2019 and published in 2020, sprouts long protrusions that could have wrapped around a bacterium.[^6] The eukaryotic cell, the kind you are made of, seems to have been born from a merger between an archaeon and a bacterium: not a gradual evolution from a simpler cell but an event that appears to have happened once in this lineage, and every plant, animal, and fungus descends from it. Bacteria have been converted into organelles since, in the amoeba *Paulinella* and in the nitrogen-fixing "nitroplast" described in 2024, but only that first merger founded a kingdom.

:::key
The cell is where life's chemistry is kept separate from the world's, and every cell is one of two kinds. Prokaryotes are small, fast, and numerous, and have been the main form of life for most of its history. Eukaryotes are large, compartmentalized, and the only kind that ever became multicellular, and they exist because of a single merger between two prokaryotes about two billion years ago that gave the host a power plant. You are a colony of trillions of descendants of that merger, each carrying the descendants of the captured bacterium.
:::

## How cells divide

A cell reproduces by dividing in two, and the hard part is giving each daughter a complete copy of the DNA. In bacteria the single chromosome is copied and the two copies are pulled to opposite ends before the cell pinches in half. In eukaryotes the process, **mitosis**, is choreographed: the DNA is copied during the cell's growth phase; at division the chromosomes condense into the X-shapes visible under a microscope (each X is two identical copies joined at the middle), line up at the cell's equator, are pulled apart by the cytoskeleton's fibers, and the cell divides between them. The **cell cycle** that governs this is run by a clock of proteins whose discovery won the 2001 Nobel Prize, with checkpoints that stop division if the DNA is damaged or the copying incomplete; cancer, at bottom, is the failure of those checkpoints (chapter 17).[^7] A second kind of division, **meiosis**, halves the chromosome number to make eggs and sperm and is the subject of chapter 7.

## How cells talk

A cell in a body is not alone. It receives chemical signals, **hormones** from distant glands, **neurotransmitters** from neighboring nerve cells, growth factors from neighbors, and it responds through **receptors**, proteins on its surface or inside it that bind a specific signal and change shape, setting off a cascade of changes inside. Most drugs work on receptors. Insulin binds its receptor and the cell takes up glucose; adrenaline binds its receptor and the cell burns it; a mutated receptor stuck "on" is a common cause of cancer. About a third of all human proteins are involved in signaling in some way, and the logic of it, a signal, a receptor, an amplifying cascade, a response, and then a shutoff, is the same from bacteria sensing food to neurons forming a memory.[^8]

:::story Henrietta Lacks
In 1951 a thirty-one-year-old woman named Henrietta Lacks was treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins, and a sample of her tumor was taken without her knowledge, as was routine. Her cells did something no human cells had done before in culture: they kept dividing, indefinitely, and did not die. **HeLa** cells became the workhorse of cell biology, used to test the polio vaccine, to study viruses and cancer and the effects of radiation and zero gravity, and to win several Nobel Prizes; more than 110,000 papers had used them by 2018, and the count keeps rising, and by mass more HeLa cells have been grown than Lacks weighed many times over.[^9] She died in 1951 without knowing; her family learned in 1973, and the questions her case raised, about consent, ownership, and who profits from a person's cells, were not seriously addressed until the 2010s. Every cell line in this guide has a person behind it, and this is the one to remember.
:::

## What we still do not know

How the first cell formed (chapter 19). How a cell knows how big it is, and stops growing. How the cytoplasm's crowding is organized; the discovery around 2009 that many proteins form liquid droplets inside cells, like oil in water, opened a field that is still being mapped. How many kinds of cell a human has: the Human Cell Atlas, which is cataloguing them by the genes each one expresses, has passed several thousand types and is not finished.[^10] The cell was described in 1665 and named in 1839; its inventory is still being taken.

## Summary

- You have about 37 trillion human cells and about as many bacteria; a cell is 10 to 20 micrometers across; a bacterium a tenth of that.
- Every cell has a phospholipid membrane, crowded cytoplasm, DNA, and ribosomes; eukaryotes add a nucleus and organelles, including mitochondria and (in plants) chloroplasts.
- Mitochondria and chloroplasts are captured bacteria; the eukaryotic cell arose once, about two billion years ago, from a merger between an archaeon and a bacterium.
- Cells divide by mitosis under a checkpointed clock whose failure is cancer, and communicate through receptors whose logic is shared from bacteria to neurons.
- HeLa cells, taken without consent in 1951, built modern cell biology; the count of human cell types is still being made.
