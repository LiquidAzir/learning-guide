## Recap

Chapter 1 said biology's wrong ideas were held by intelligent people for good reasons. This chapter goes through them: what people believed about life, why, and what changed their minds. Later chapters return to each episode in more detail; this one gives the shape, so that when Mendel or Darwin appears you know where they stand.

## Antiquity to 1600: describing

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) dissected hundreds of animals, described the development of chick embryos day by day, classified some 500 species by shared features, and concluded that living things had a purpose built into them, a "final cause," and that some, like flies from rotting meat, arose spontaneously from non-living matter. He was wrong about spontaneous generation and about purpose, and right about almost everything he actually looked at; his biology stood for nearly two thousand years because nobody looked as carefully again.[^1] Galen (129–c. 216 CE) dissected animals (human dissection was forbidden) and built an anatomy and physiology that medicine used until the sixteenth century, including the belief that blood was made in the liver and consumed in the body. Medieval Islamic scholars, above all Ibn al-Nafis, who described the pulmonary circulation in 1242, corrected parts of Galen; Europe mostly copied him.

## 1600 to 1800: looking closer

Three tools changed the subject. **Dissection** of humans, made respectable by Vesalius (1543), corrected Galen's anatomy. **Experiment**: William Harvey, in 1628, measured how much blood the heart pumps per hour, showed it was more than the body could possibly make, and concluded that blood circulates in a closed circuit, which nobody had demonstrated; Ibn al-Nafis and Colombo had described only the lesser, pulmonary transit.[^2] **The microscope**: Robert Hooke saw the compartments of cork in 1665 and called them cells; Leeuwenhoek saw the microbes (chapter 1); and the first arguments began about whether these tiny things arose spontaneously.

Classification became systematic. Carl Linnaeus, consistently from *Species Plantarum* in 1753, gave every species a two-part Latin name (*Homo sapiens*) and ranked them into genus, order, class, and kingdom, a system still used, though he built it on the assumption that species were fixed as created.[^3] The eighteenth century also argued about generation: **preformationists** held that the embryo was already present, tiny and complete, in the egg or sperm; **epigenesists** that it formed gradually from unorganized matter. The epigenesists were right, but the preformationists had a good argument: how could organized complexity arise from nothing? Chapter 15 gives the answer, which took two centuries.

## 1800 to 1860: cells, chemistry, and Darwin

The first half of the nineteenth century built the foundations. **Cell theory** (Schleiden 1838, Schwann 1839, Virchow 1855): all living things are made of cells, and every cell comes from another cell, so there is no spontaneous generation at the cellular level.[^4] **Organic chemistry**: Wöhler synthesized urea, a substance of life, from inorganic chemicals in 1828, the first crack in **vitalism**, the doctrine that living matter had a special force chemistry could not touch; by 1900 the doctrine was dead, though it took Buchner's demonstration in 1897 that yeast juice, with no living cells, could ferment sugar to finish it.[^5] **Physiology** became experimental in Claude Bernard's laboratory, which established that the body keeps its internal environment constant, the idea later named homeostasis.

And **evolution**. That species change was suspected before Darwin: Lamarck in 1809 proposed that organisms adapt to their environment and pass the adaptation on, so that giraffes stretching for leaves produce longer-necked offspring. It was a reasonable theory and it was wrong, because acquired characteristics are not inherited (chapter 7 says why, and chapter 8 says what the small exceptions are). Charles Darwin, after five years on HMS *Beagle* and twenty years of hesitation, published *On the Origin of Species* in 1859 with a different mechanism: individuals vary; some variations are inherited; more are born than can survive; those whose variations suit them survive and breed more; over generations the population changes. **Natural selection** required no purpose, no force, and no designer, only variation, inheritance, and differential survival, and it explained adaptation, the fossil record, biogeography, and the tree-like pattern of similarities among species in one stroke.[^6] Alfred Russel Wallace reached the same idea independently in 1858. What Darwin did not have was a theory of inheritance, and without one his theory had a hole that critics pointed to for fifty years.

## 1860 to 1900: germs and genes

Louis Pasteur settled spontaneous generation in 1862 with sealed flasks that stayed sterile until opened, and with Robert Koch established the **germ theory of disease**: specific microbes cause specific diseases. Koch's postulates for proving it (1884), his identification of the anthrax, tuberculosis, and cholera bacteria, and Pasteur's vaccines for anthrax and rabies turned medicine from an art into a science within a generation; Lister's antiseptic surgery and the public health movement followed, and life expectancy in the industrial world began the rise that has not stopped.[^7]

Gregor Mendel, an Augustinian friar in Brno, crossed pea plants for eight years and in 1866 published the result: traits are passed on as discrete units, in pairs, one from each parent, and they segregate and recombine according to simple ratios. It was the theory of inheritance Darwin needed. It was ignored for thirty-four years.[^8]

## 1900 to 1953: the synthesis

Mendel was rediscovered in 1900 by three botanists at once, and the twentieth century's first task was to reconcile him with Darwin. At first they seemed opposed: Mendel's traits came in discrete jumps, Darwin's selection worked on continuous variation. Thomas Hunt Morgan's fruit-fly laboratory at Columbia showed from 1910 that Mendel's units, now called **genes**, sat on the **chromosomes** in the cell nucleus in a linear order; Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, and Sewall Wright showed mathematically in the 1920s that many genes of small effect produce continuous variation on which selection can act; and by the 1940s the **modern synthesis** had joined genetics, selection, paleontology, and systematics into one theory of evolution that is, with additions, the one held today.[^9]

The other question was what a gene was made of. Chromosomes contained protein and DNA, and protein, with twenty building blocks, seemed the better candidate for carrying information; DNA, with four, looked too simple. Oswald Avery showed in 1944 that DNA alone could transfer a heritable trait between bacteria; Hershey and Chase confirmed it in 1952; and in 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photographs of DNA fibers, worked out its structure: two strands wound in a **double helix**, held together by pairs of bases (A with T, G with C), so that each strand is a template for the other. The structure explained how the molecule could be copied, and the paper's one-sentence hint that it "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism" was the beginning of molecular biology.[^10]

## 1953 to 2000: the code and the tools

The next twenty years read the code. Crick stated the **central dogma**: once sequence information has passed into a protein it cannot get out again. (The popular version, "DNA to RNA to protein and never back," is a later corruption; Crick always allowed DNA and RNA to be copied in either direction.) Nirenberg, Khorana, and others cracked the **genetic code**, the table that maps three-letter DNA words to amino acids, by 1966; Jacob and Monod showed how genes are switched on and off. In 1973 Cohen and Boyer cut a gene from one organism and pasted it into a bacterium, **recombinant DNA**, and biotechnology began; in 1977 Sanger worked out how to read DNA sequence; in 1983 Mullis invented PCR, which copies any DNA fragment a billion-fold in an afternoon. The **Human Genome Project** (1990–2003) read the euchromatic 92 percent of a human genome, some three billion letters, at a cost of about $3 billion, and the remaining gaps were closed only in 2022; the same job now costs a few hundred dollars and a day.[^11]

Meanwhile the rest of biology transformed. Neuroscience worked out how nerve cells fire (Hodgkin and Huxley, 1952) and communicate. Immunology explained how the body recognizes self from non-self. Ecology became quantitative. Developmental biology found the small set of genes, shared from flies to humans, that lay out a body. And the microbial world turned out to be far larger and stranger than anyone thought: Carl Woese showed in 1977 that there are not two fundamental kinds of life but three, and that the third, the **archaea**, had been hiding in plain sight; current evidence suggests the deepest split is really two-way, with eukaryotes arising from within the archaea (chapter 9).[^12]

## 2000 to now: reading and writing

The twenty-first century's biology is defined by two capacities. **Reading**: genomes of hundreds of thousands of species and millions of people, the DNA of single cells, the RNA of every cell in a tissue, the proteins' shapes predicted by artificial intelligence (AlphaFold, 2020–2024) for essentially every known protein. **Writing**: **CRISPR**, adapted from a bacterial immune system in 2012, edits DNA in living cells cheaply and precisely, and the first CRISPR cure, for sickle-cell disease, was approved in 2023; mRNA vaccines, developed over decades and deployed in 2020, showed that a genetic message could be a drug; embryos, organs, and brains can be grown in miniature from stem cells.[^13] Chapters 18 and 19 are about what this makes possible and what it makes dangerous. The history is not over; biology is the science whose next chapter is least predictable.

:::history How we got here
Every wrong idea in this chapter was reasonable on its evidence. Spontaneous generation: maggots do appear in meat, and nobody had seen a fly lay eggs. Vitalism: no chemist could make anything living, so life seemed to need something beyond chemistry. Lamarck: organisms are visibly suited to their environments and offspring do resemble parents, so the simplest story was that the suiting was passed on. Preformation: organized complexity had never been seen to arise from disorder. Protein as the genetic material: it was the more complex molecule. Two fundamental kinds of life: that is what the microscope showed. In every case the correction came from a new tool (the sealed flask, the synthesis of urea, the fruit-fly cross, the X-ray photograph, the ribosomal RNA sequence) that made visible what had been invisible. The lesson for reading current biology is that today's reasonable ideas will be corrected in the same way, and the ones held with the most confidence on the least direct evidence are the ones to watch.
:::

## Summary

- Aristotle and Galen described life carefully and built theories (spontaneous generation, final causes, Galenic physiology) that stood for two thousand years for lack of better tools.
- Dissection, experiment, and the microscope (1543–1677) corrected anatomy, discovered circulation and cells, and opened the argument about where life comes from.
- The nineteenth century established cell theory, killed vitalism with chemistry, disproved spontaneous generation, founded germ theory, and produced Darwin's natural selection and Mendel's laws, which took forty years to be joined.
- The twentieth century joined them (the modern synthesis), found that DNA carries heredity and how (1944–1953), read the genetic code, and built the tools to cut, paste, copy, and read DNA.
- The twenty-first reads and writes life at scale: genomes, single cells, predicted protein shapes, CRISPR, mRNA, and stem-cell organs.
