## How does a cell make energy usable?

Chapter 4 introduced ATP as the cell's energy currency and enzymes as the machinery. This chapter is about the two great processes that make and spend the currency: **photosynthesis**, which captures sunlight into sugar, and **respiration**, which burns sugar back to make ATP. Between them they move nearly all the energy in the living world, and they are, at the molecular level, the same machinery run in opposite directions.

## The problem

Living things are ordered, and order costs energy to build and maintain. The second law of thermodynamics (physics guide, chapter 5) says that disorder increases overall, so a cell can stay ordered only by exporting disorder: taking in high-quality energy (sunlight, or the chemical energy in food) and releasing low-quality energy (heat) and simple waste (carbon dioxide, water). Life does not violate the second law; it rides it, and the riding is what metabolism is.[^1]

The energy is delivered in a standard unit. **ATP** carries energy in the bond to its third phosphate group; when that bond is broken (hydrolyzed), about 50 kilojoules per mole are released under cellular conditions, enough to drive most of the cell's reactions when coupled to them, and the ADP left behind is recharged to ATP by the processes below.[^2] The cell's ATP supply would last a few seconds if not renewed; you make and use about your own body weight of it a day, recycling each molecule a thousand times.

## Respiration: burning sugar slowly

Glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide and water and releases energy: the same overall reaction as burning sugar in air, about 2,870 kilojoules per mole. A cell does it in about thirty enzyme-catalyzed steps so that the energy comes out in small parcels it can capture, rather than as heat. Three stages.

**Glycolysis**, in the cytoplasm, splits glucose (six carbons) into two molecules of pyruvate (three carbons each), yielding 2 ATP and stripping off electrons onto a carrier molecule. It needs no oxygen and is the most ancient part of metabolism, present in every cell on Earth. When oxygen is short, cells stop here and dump the pyruvate as **lactate** (muscle during a sprint) or, in yeast, as **ethanol** and carbon dioxide (which is beer, bread, and wine). This **fermentation** yields only 2 ATP per glucose, about 5 percent of what is available.

**The citric acid cycle** (Krebs cycle), inside the mitochondrion, breaks pyruvate down completely to carbon dioxide, which you exhale, and loads the released electrons onto more carriers. Fats and proteins enter here too, which is why you can burn any food.

**Oxidative phosphorylation**, in the mitochondrion's inner membrane, is where the money is made. The electron carriers hand their electrons to a chain of proteins embedded in the membrane; as the electrons pass down the chain toward oxygen (which finally accepts them, forming water), the proteins use the energy to pump protons (hydrogen ions) across the membrane, building up a concentration difference like water behind a dam. The protons flow back through a single enzyme, **ATP synthase**, which is a rotary motor: the flow of protons turns a shaft at about 100 revolutions per second, and each turn cranks out three ATP.[^3] Peter Mitchell proposed this **chemiosmotic** mechanism in 1961 and was disbelieved for a decade; Paul Boyer and John Walker worked out the motor and shared a Nobel Prize in 1997. It yields about 26 to 28 ATP per glucose, bringing the total to roughly 30. Valued at the standard 30.5 kilojoules per mole that is about a third of the sugar's energy; at the roughly 50 kilojoules per mole that ATP is actually worth inside a cell, closer to half. Either figure beats a car engine's 25 to 35 percent.

The reason you breathe is the last step. Oxygen is the final acceptor of the electrons; without it the chain backs up, the protons stop flowing, ATP production collapses, and cells that depend on it (neurons, above all) die within minutes. Cyanide kills by blocking the last protein in the chain; carbon monoxide by taking oxygen's place on hemoglobin so none arrives.

:::key
Respiration is a controlled burn. Glucose is taken apart in about thirty steps, its electrons are passed down a chain of proteins to oxygen, and the energy of that descent pumps protons across a membrane; the protons flowing back turn a molecular turbine that makes ATP. Thirty ATP per glucose, about 35 percent efficiency, and a design that is the same in every mitochondrion and, with different fuels and acceptors, in every bacterium. Breathing exists to deliver the oxygen that catches the electrons at the bottom.
:::

## Photosynthesis: sugar from light

Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria run the process the other way. **Photosynthesis** takes carbon dioxide and water and, with the energy of sunlight, builds glucose and releases oxygen: the reverse of respiration, and the source of every molecule of oxygen in the air and nearly every molecule of food.

It happens in **chloroplasts** (chapter 3), in two stages. In the **light reactions**, chlorophyll, the green pigment, absorbs photons (red and blue; it reflects green, which is why plants are the color they are), and the energy knocks electrons loose from water, splitting it into oxygen (released) and protons and electrons (kept). The electrons pass down a protein chain that pumps protons across a membrane, and an ATP synthase, nearly identical to the mitochondrial one, makes ATP; the electrons end up on a carrier molecule, NADPH. In the **Calvin cycle**, that ATP and NADPH are spent to bolt carbon dioxide onto a five-carbon sugar, using the enzyme **RuBisCO**, and to rearrange the products into glucose. RuBisCO is the most abundant protein on Earth and one of the slowest enzymes known, processing about three carbon dioxide molecules per second and confusing oxygen for carbon dioxide in roughly a quarter of its reactions in an ordinary plant at today's carbon dioxide levels, more when it is hot, which is why plants make so much of it and why engineering a better one is a live research goal.[^4]

The overall efficiency is low: about 1 to 2 percent of the sunlight falling on a field ends up as chemical energy in the crop, against 20 percent for a solar panel. But it is self-building, self-repairing, and running over about 150 million square kilometers of land and most of the ocean's surface, fixing about 105 billion tons of carbon a year net, split roughly evenly between land and sea, much of the marine half by organisms too small to see.[^5]

## Why it is one machinery

The two processes share their central part, a membrane with an electron-transport chain that pumps protons and an ATP synthase that spends them, and the sharing is historical. Chloroplasts are captured cyanobacteria and mitochondria are captured bacteria of another kind (chapter 3), and both inherited the machinery from ancestors that used it, in various forms, before there was any oxygen at all. The earliest life, some 3.8 billion years ago, ran on chemical energy from rocks and gases; cyanobacteria invented water-splitting photosynthesis about 3 billion years ago and, over the following billion years, filled the atmosphere with oxygen, poisoning most existing life and making possible the respiration that eukaryotes and animals depend on. Every breath you take is a consequence of that event, and every calorie you eat was captured from sunlight by a chloroplast.[^6]

## Metabolism, the map

**Metabolism** is the whole network of a cell's chemical reactions, a few thousand of them in a human cell, connected into pathways. It divides into **catabolism** (breaking down, releasing energy: respiration, digestion) and **anabolism** (building up, costing energy: making proteins, DNA, membranes, sugar). The two are coupled through ATP and the electron carriers, and the network is regulated at hundreds of points so that supply matches demand: after a meal, insulin tells cells to take up glucose and store it as glycogen and fat; between meals, glucagon and adrenaline tell them to release it; during a fast, the liver makes glucose from protein and the body burns fat, producing the ketones that fuel the brain when sugar runs low.[^7]

:::howto Reading a calorie
A food **calorie** (properly a kilocalorie, 4.18 kilojoules) is the energy released when the food is burned completely, measured in a calorimeter and adjusted for what the body cannot digest. The numbers on a label are approximations from standard factors: 4 kcal per gram of protein or carbohydrate, 9 per gram of fat, 7 per gram of alcohol. To use them:
1. **Estimate what you burn.** A resting adult uses about 1,500 to 2,000 kcal a day just to stay alive (the basal rate, roughly 20 kcal per kilogram of body weight, less for women and older people, more for the muscular), plus activity: about 100 kcal per mile walked.
2. **Know what the body does with a surplus.** Excess carbohydrate is stored as glycogen (about 500 grams' worth, a day's supply) and then as fat; excess fat is stored as fat. A kilogram of body fat holds about 7,700 kcal, so a sustained surplus or deficit of 500 kcal a day moves body weight by roughly half a kilogram a week at first, less as the body adapts.
3. **Distrust precision.** Label values are allowed to be off by 20 percent; absorption varies with food processing and the gut microbiome; and the body defends its weight by adjusting how much it burns. The arithmetic works on average and over months, not per meal.[^8]
:::

:::story Mitchell's barn
Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961 that mitochondria made ATP not by a chemical intermediate, as everyone assumed, but by pumping protons across a membrane and letting them flow back: energy stored as a gradient, like water behind a dam. The idea was so foreign to biochemists, who thought in terms of molecules reacting, that it was largely dismissed; Mitchell, in poor health, left academia in 1963, restored a manor house in Cornwall with his own money, set up a private laboratory in it, where he and Jennifer Moyle spent fifteen years accumulating the evidence.[^9] By the mid-1970s the field had come round; he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978, alone. The story is told to students as a lesson in persistence. It is also a lesson in how a correct idea can be invisible to a whole discipline because it is stated in the wrong vocabulary: Mitchell was talking physics to chemists.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
A cell couples an unfavorable reaction to ATP hydrolysis. Does breaking ATP's bond, by itself, explain the energy release?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Breaking a bond costs energy. The net free-energy change includes the entire reaction: bonds broken and formed, interactions with water, and the conditions in the cell. Coupling works when the combined process has a favorable free-energy change, not because one bond releases energy merely by breaking.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Life stays ordered by taking in high-quality energy and releasing heat and waste; ATP is the currency, and you recycle your body weight of it daily.
- Respiration burns glucose in about thirty steps: glycolysis (2 ATP, no oxygen needed), the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, where electrons descend a protein chain to oxygen, pump protons, and drive the ATP-synthase turbine; about 30 ATP per glucose.
- Photosynthesis runs the same machinery in reverse: light splits water, the electron chain makes ATP and NADPH, and RuBisCO fixes carbon dioxide into sugar, at 1 to 2 percent of incoming sunlight.
- Both inherited the proton-pumping design from ancient bacteria; cyanobacteria oxygenated the atmosphere and made animal life possible.
- Metabolism is a regulated network coupling breakdown to building; calories are measured in a calorimeter and the arithmetic works over months, not meals.
