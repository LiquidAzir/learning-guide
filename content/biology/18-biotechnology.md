## Recap

Chapter 6 explained how genes work; this chapter is about the tools that read and rewrite them, what they have already delivered in the clinic, and the safety and ethics questions that are now urgent rather than hypothetical.

## Reading

**Sequencing** determines the order of bases in a DNA molecule. Frederick Sanger's 1977 method dominated for thirty years and read the first human genome over thirteen years for about $3 billion. From 2005 "next-generation" methods read hundreds of millions of short fragments in parallel and assembled them computationally; the cost of a human genome fell to about $1,000 by 2015 and to a few hundred dollars now, a decline steeper and longer-sustained than Moore's law for computer chips.[^1] Long-read technologies, which read hundreds of thousands of bases at once, finished the job: the first truly complete human genome, with no gaps, was published in 2022, adding about 200 million bases the old reference had left blank, including every centromere.

The applications are now routine. **Diagnosis**: sequencing a sick newborn's genome can identify a treatable genetic disease in days. **Cancer**: tumors are sequenced to choose targeted drugs, and fragments of tumor DNA circulating in blood allow monitoring without biopsy. **Pathogens**: the SARS-CoV-2 genome was published on 10 January 2020, ten days after the cluster was reported to the World Health Organization, and the vaccine design followed within days. **Populations**: biobanks of hundreds of thousands of people link genomes to health records, and the **pangenome**, a graph of 47 diverse individuals published in 2023, is replacing the single reference that was mostly one man's DNA.[^2]

## Writing

**Recombinant DNA** (1973) let a gene be moved between organisms, and gave bacteria that make human insulin, clotting factors, and growth hormone. **Transgenic crops** followed from the 1990s. But precise editing of a chosen site in a living genome was hard until bacteria supplied the tool.

**CRISPR** is a bacterial immune system: bacteria keep fragments of past viral invaders and use them as guides for a protein that cuts matching DNA. In 2012 Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that the system could be programmed with a synthetic guide RNA to cut any chosen sequence, and within a year several groups had it working in human cells.[^3] They shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The cut is then repaired by the cell, either sloppily, which disables a gene, or from a supplied template, which rewrites it.

Two refinements matter. **Base editing** (David Liu's laboratory, 2016) changes a single letter without cutting both strands, which is safer. **Prime editing** (2019) writes short new sequences at a chosen site. Both avoid the double-strand break that makes classical CRISPR occasionally produce large unintended deletions.

## What has actually been delivered

**Casgevy**, approved in the UK in November 2023 and by the US FDA in December 2023, is the first approved therapy based on CRISPR. A patient's own blood stem cells are removed, edited to switch on fetal hemoglobin, and returned after chemotherapy; in trials nearly all patients with sickle-cell disease stopped having severe pain crises. It costs about $2.2 million, requires months in a specialist center, and by 2026 was being given at specialist centers worldwide, with the label extended in July 2026 to children as young as two.[^4]

**In-vivo editing.** In 2025 an infant with a fatal urea-cycle disorder received a base-editing therapy designed and manufactured for his specific mutation in about six months, delivered to the liver in lipid nanoparticles, the fatty bubbles that also carry mRNA vaccines. He improved. It is one patient with short follow-up, and it demonstrates that bespoke gene editing for a single person is now technically and, in that case, regulatorily possible.[^5]

**mRNA as a drug.** The COVID-19 vaccines showed that genetic instructions in a lipid bubble can be a medicine, and that the manufacturing does not change when the target does. Personalized cancer vaccines built on the same platform, encoding up to 34 mutations unique to a patient's tumor, cut recurrence in a randomized phase 2 melanoma trial; phase 3 results are pending.

**Protein design.** AlphaFold predicted the shapes of essentially all known proteins (chapter 4); David Baker's laboratory designs proteins that never existed, including new enzymes, vaccine scaffolds, and binders against specific targets. The 2024 Chemistry Nobel recognized both.

**Synthetic biology.** Yeast engineered to make artemisinin (an antimalarial), insulin, vanilla flavoring, and the casein proteins in animal-free dairy; bacteria engineered as biosensors; a bacterium with a fully synthetic genome (2010) and a yeast with synthetic chromosomes (2023–2025). The field's ambition is to make biology an engineering discipline with standard parts, and its recurring lesson is that living systems are less modular than the analogy suggests.[^6]

## What has not

**Gene therapy for common disease.** Most approved gene therapies treat rare single-gene conditions, because those are the ones where fixing one gene fixes the problem. Heart disease, diabetes, and psychiatric illness are polygenic and environmental (chapter 7), and no editing strategy addresses them.

**Germline editing.** In 2018 He Jiankui announced that he had edited the embryos of twin girls, with a third child born the following year, targeting a gene to confer HIV resistance. The work was scientifically unnecessary (there are established ways to prevent transmission), technically sloppy (the edits were not the intended ones, and the girls are mosaics, meaning different cells in them carry different edits), performed with falsified ethics approval, and universally condemned; he was imprisoned for three years.[^7] The scientific consensus, stated by an international commission in 2020, is that heritable human genome editing is not currently safe or justified, and that if it is ever used it should be limited to serious monogenic disease where no alternative exists. The girls, now school-aged, carry edits of unknown consequence in every cell and will pass them on.

**De-extinction.** A company's 2025 announcement that it had "de-extincted" the dire wolf involved editing 14 sites in a gray wolf genome; the animals are gray wolves with altered traits, the supporting work was a preprint, and most biologists reject the framing. The underlying technology, editing and cloning, is real; the claim is marketing. George Church's laboratory at Harvard has pursued a genuine version of the idea for years, editing cold-tolerance genes from mammoths into elephant cells, and even success there would produce an edited elephant rather than a mammoth.

## Biosecurity

The same tools cut both ways, and the concern has moved from theoretical to concrete. Sequences of dangerous pathogens are public; DNA can be synthesized to order; and AI systems trained on biological data can now suggest designs. The recognized risks: engineering a pathogen for greater transmissibility or lethality; resurrecting an extinct one (horsepox was reconstructed from mail-order DNA in 2016 and published in 2018, demonstrating that smallpox could be); and gain-of-function research, in which a pathogen is deliberately made more transmissible or more dangerous to study it, whose accident risk may exceed its benefit, an argument that the COVID-19 origin debate made bitter and has not resolved.[^8]

In December 2024, thirty-eight scientists, several of whom had worked toward it, called for a halt to efforts to build **mirror life**: synthetic organisms made from mirror-image molecules, which could evade immune systems, predators, and antibiotics across all species because those defenses recognize normal handedness. No such organism exists and building one is likely a decade or more away, which is why they wanted the discussion now. Other researchers argue that mirror organisms would grow poorly outside a laboratory, so the level of risk is itself disputed.[^9]

The countermeasures are screening of synthesis orders, biosafety rules, international agreements, and, for AI systems, safeguards against making it easier to build a weapon; none is comprehensive and all depend on voluntary compliance.

:::key
Reading DNA has become cheap and routine; writing it has become precise enough to cure single-gene diseases and, in 2025, to be customized for one patient. What that does not deliver is treatment for the common polygenic diseases, and what it does raise is a set of safety questions, germline editing, engineered pathogens, and mirror life, on which the scientific community has issued moratoria and warnings that carry no legal force. The gap between capability and governance is the defining feature of biotechnology in 2026.
:::

:::story Asilomar
In 1974, a year after the first recombinant DNA experiment, the scientists doing the work called for a voluntary moratorium on the most worrying experiments, and in February 1975 about 140 of them, with lawyers and journalists present, met at the Asilomar conference center in California to decide what to do. Over four days they agreed on a framework of containment matched to risk, banned a few classes of experiment outright, and lifted the moratorium for the rest.[^10] The resulting guidelines governed the field for decades, the feared accidents did not happen, and the biotechnology industry grew from it. Asilomar is invoked whenever a new technology alarms its creators, and two features of it are usually forgotten: the participants were a small, homogeneous group deciding for everyone, and the moratorium worked partly because the field was small enough that everyone who could do the work was in the room. Neither condition holds for gene editing or for AI.
:::

## Summary

- Sequencing cost has fallen from about $50 million per genome in 2003 to a few hundred dollars, roughly a hundred-thousand-fold; the first gapless human genome came in 2022 and a 47-person pangenome in 2023.
- CRISPR, adapted from a bacterial immune system in 2012, made precise editing routine; base and prime editing avoid cutting both DNA strands.
- Casgevy (2023) is the first approved CRISPR therapy; a bespoke base-editing treatment for one infant was given in 2025; mRNA is now a drug platform, and designed proteins won the 2024 Chemistry Nobel.
- Gene therapy works for rare single-gene diseases and not for common polygenic ones; germline editing was done once, badly and illegally, in 2018 and remains outside consensus.
- Biosecurity risks, engineered pathogens, synthesis of extinct viruses, and proposed mirror life, are governed by voluntary agreements, and the gap between capability and governance is the field's central problem.
