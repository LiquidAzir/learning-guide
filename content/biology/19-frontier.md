## Recap

Each chapter has marked its own uncertainties. This one collects the largest, states what is actually known, and says what evidence would settle them. It is the chapter most likely to be out of date, which is what the research feed on this subject's page is for.

## 1. How did life begin?

Every cell comes from a cell (chapter 2), but the first one did not. Between the chemistry of a young Earth and the last universal common ancestor, which recent work dates to about 4.2 billion years ago and reconstructs as already a sophisticated cell with some 2,600 protein-coding genes, though other groups date it substantially later, there is a gap nobody has closed.[^1]

What is established: the building blocks form readily. Miller and Urey made amino acids from simple gases and a spark in 1953; nucleotide precursors have been synthesized under plausible conditions; and, decisively, the samples returned from asteroid Bennu in 2023 contain 14 of the 20 biological amino acids and all five nucleobases of DNA and RNA, so the chemistry happens in space and was delivered to Earth.[^2] The leading framework is the **RNA world**: RNA can both carry information and catalyze reactions, so it could have preceded the DNA-protein division of labor, and the ribosome's catalytic core is still RNA, a molecular fossil.

What is missing: how a self-replicating molecule arose without enzymes; how membranes, metabolism, and replication came together; and why life uses left-handed amino acids when the chemistry delivers both in equal measure, which the Bennu samples showed comes from Earth and not from space. The competing venues are alkaline hydrothermal vents, where proton gradients across mineral membranes resemble chapter 5's machinery, and warm little ponds with wet-dry cycles. Nobody has made a self-sustaining, evolving chemical system from scratch, and that is what would count as an answer.

## 2. Are we alone?

Biology's largest question is whether it has a second data point. What has changed: about 6,000 exoplanets are confirmed, several dozen in habitable zones; Mars had liquid water and organic molecules; Europa and Enceladus have subsurface oceans with the chemistry and energy life needs; and the James Webb telescope can now read the composition of some exoplanet atmospheres. What has not changed: no detection. Claims of a possible biosignature gas on the exoplanet K2-18b, first in 2023 and more strongly in 2025, were both heavily contested, and later analyses found the evidence weak.[^3] The physics guide's cosmology chapter covers the search; the biological contribution is to say what a biosignature would have to look like, which requires knowing which chemical imbalances, gases that should have reacted away but have not, only life can sustain, and that requires understanding question 1.

## 3. How does the brain produce experience?

Chapter 13 laid this out: the mechanism of neurons and memory is well understood, the systems level partly, and the relationship between neural activity and subjective experience not at all. The two leading theories were tested against each other in an adversarial collaboration, in which rival camps agree in advance on experiments whose results would count against their own view, and the 2025 results fit neither cleanly, and the field disagrees about what would count as an answer. This is the deepest unsolved problem in biology and possibly the deepest in science.[^4]

## 4. What is most of the genome doing?

Only about 1.5 percent of human DNA codes for protein, perhaps 8 percent is under evolutionary constraint, and the ENCODE project's claim that 80 percent is "functional" was contested on the grounds that biochemical activity is not function (chapter 6). Resolving this needs a test for function that does not beg the question, and large-scale deletion experiments, now feasible, are beginning to provide one.

## 5. Why do we age, and can it be slowed?

Twelve hallmarks are measurable, several interventions extend life in animals, and none is proven in humans (chapter 15). The open questions: whether aging is a program that can be switched, an accumulation of damage that must be repaired, or both; whether the hallmarks are causes or symptoms; and whether partial reprogramming, which reverses age markers in mice, is safe in people. The first human trial of a reprogramming therapy began dosing in 2026, in the eye, with no efficacy data yet. What would settle it is a randomized human trial with a mortality or morbidity endpoint, which nobody has run because it would take decades.[^5]

## 6. What is the microbiome actually causing?

Correlations abound, causal demonstrations are few (chapter 10). Fecal transplant for recurrent *C. difficile* works; nearly everything else is unproven. Settling it requires interventions in humans, not mice, with preregistered outcomes.

## 7. Can we predict a phenotype from a genome?

Given a genome, can you say what the organism will be like? For single-gene diseases, yes. For height, partially. For most traits and diseases, no: polygenic scores capture a modest fraction of variation and transfer poorly across ancestries (chapter 7). Genome language models trained on DNA, like the 2026 system trained on nine trillion bases across 100,000 species, can now predict which mutations are harmful without being told, which is a step; whether they can predict organism-level traits is untested.[^6]

## 8. How do we feed ten billion people without wrecking the biosphere?

Agriculture uses about half the habitable land, most of the fresh water, and produces roughly a quarter of greenhouse emissions (chapters 11 and 16). The candidate answers are all partial: improved photosynthesis (field trials have raised yields), nitrogen fixation in cereals (made to look less impossible by the 2024 discovery of a nitrogen-fixing organelle), gene-edited crops, precision agriculture, dietary shift, and reduced waste. This is the question with the largest number of lives attached.

## 9. What should not be built?

Not an empirical question, but the field's most pressing one. Germline editing has been done once, illegally, and the international consensus is against it for now. Mirror life, organisms built from mirror-image molecules, was the subject of an unusual 2024 call from thirty-eight scientists, several of whom had worked toward it, to stop before it becomes possible. Engineered pathogens are cheap enough that the governance gap is real. And AI systems that can design proteins and suggest genome edits are now part of the toolkit (chapter 18). The recurring pattern is that capability arrives before governance, and biology's response, Asilomar in 1975, the CRISPR moratorium statements, the mirror-life letter, has been self-organized, voluntary, and dependent on the field being small enough to convene.[^7]

## What would change everything

Three possible results would reorganize the subject.

**A second origin of life**, found in a Martian rock, an icy moon's plume, or a laboratory flask. It would turn a sample of one into a sample of two and tell us which features of life are necessary and which are accidents.

**A theory of consciousness that makes testable predictions** about which physical systems have experience. It would settle questions about anesthesia, disorders of consciousness, animals, and machines that are currently argued from intuition.

**Reliable prediction of organism from genome.** It would make biology a predictive science in the way physics is, and it would make the ethical questions of chapter 18 far sharper.

:::key
Biology's frontier is unusual among the sciences in that its largest questions are not extensions of existing theory but genuine blanks: how life started, whether it happened elsewhere, and how brains produce experience. Its next largest are practical and urgent: aging, prediction from genomes, feeding the world, and what not to build. The honest position on most of them is that we know what we would need to find out and have not found it.
:::

## Summary

- The origin of life is unsolved: building blocks form readily and were delivered from space, but no one has made a self-sustaining, evolving chemical system.
- No extraterrestrial life has been detected; the strongest biosignature claim to date was contested and weakened.
- How neural activity produces experience is unsolved and lacks an agreed method of solution; the two leading theories were tested head to head in 2025 and neither won.
- What most of the genome does, whether aging is modifiable in humans, what the microbiome causes, and whether phenotype can be predicted from genome are all open with clear tests available.
- Feeding ten billion people within planetary limits is the question with the most lives attached; what should not be built is the one with the least governance.
