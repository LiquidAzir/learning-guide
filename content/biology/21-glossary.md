## Terms

**Action potential** (ch. 13). The all-or-nothing electrical spike a neuron fires when its voltage passes threshold, propagating down the axon at 1 to 100 meters per second.

**Adaptive immunity** (ch. 14). The specific, remembering arm of defense, using B and T cells with randomly generated receptors; found only in vertebrates.

**Allele** (ch. 7). One of the alternative versions of a gene.

**Amino acid** (ch. 4). The building block of proteins; life uses twenty, each with a common backbone and a distinct side group.

**Antibody** (ch. 14). A protein made by B cells that binds a specific target molecule.

**Archaea** (ch. 9). One of the three domains of life; superficially like bacteria, with information machinery closer to ours, and the group from which eukaryotes appear to have arisen.

**ATP** (ch. 4, 5). Adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency; you recycle your body weight of it daily.

**Autoimmunity** (ch. 14). An immune attack on the body's own tissues.

**Axon** (ch. 13). The single output fiber of a neuron.

**Bacteria** (ch. 9, 10). One of the three domains; single-celled, no nucleus, about 10^30 cells on Earth.

**Base editing** (ch. 18). Changing a single DNA letter without cutting both strands.

**Biomass** (ch. 16). The total mass of living material; Earth's is about 550 gigatons of carbon, 80 percent of it plants.

**Calvin cycle** (ch. 5). The reactions that fix carbon dioxide into sugar using the ATP and NADPH made by the light reactions.

**Cancer** (ch. 17). Uncontrolled cell division arising from mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressors.

**Carbohydrate** (ch. 4). Sugars and their polymers: glucose, starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin.

**Cell** (ch. 3). The bounded unit of life: membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, ribosomes.

**Cell cycle** (ch. 3). The checkpointed sequence of growth, DNA copying, and division.

**Central dogma** (ch. 6). Information flows DNA to RNA to protein, and not back from protein.

**Checkpoint inhibitor** (ch. 14, 17). A drug that blocks the off-switches tumors use to disable T cells.

**Chloroplast** (ch. 3, 5). The photosynthetic organelle of plants and algae; a captured cyanobacterium.

**Chromosome** (ch. 6). One DNA molecule with its packaging proteins; humans have 46 in most cells.

**Clonal selection** (ch. 14). The expansion of the few lymphocytes whose receptors match an invader.

**Codon** (ch. 6). A three-base word in the genetic code, specifying one amino acid or stop.

**Connectome** (ch. 13). A complete map of the connections in a nervous system; completed for an adult fly brain in 2024.

**CRISPR** (ch. 18). A bacterial immune system adapted in 2012 into a programmable DNA-cutting tool.

**Cytoskeleton** (ch. 3). The protein scaffold that gives a cell shape, moves it, and carries cargo.

**Dendrite** (ch. 13). The branching input structure of a neuron.

**DNA** (ch. 6). Deoxyribonucleic acid; a double helix of four bases in which A pairs with T and G with C.

**Domain** (ch. 9). The highest rank of classification: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.

**Dominant allele.** An allele whose associated phenotype appears in a heterozygote under the inheritance model being discussed. See chapter 7.

**Endosymbiosis** (ch. 3). One cell living inside another; the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts.

**Enzyme** (ch. 4). A protein that catalyzes a specific reaction, speeding it up by factors up to 10^15.

**Epigenetics** (ch. 6). Chemical marks on DNA or histones that change gene use without changing sequence, and are copied when the cell divides.

**Eukaryote** (ch. 3, 9). A cell with a nucleus and organelles; animals, plants, fungi, protists.

**Evolution** (ch. 8). Change in the heritable characteristics of populations over generations.

**Fitness** (ch. 8). Relative reproductive success in a given environment; not strength or health.

**Fungi** (ch. 11). Organisms that digest externally and absorb; closer relatives of animals than of plants.

**Gene** (ch. 6). A stretch of DNA specifying a protein or functional RNA; the definition has become blurry.

**Genetic code** (ch. 6). The near-universal table mapping 64 codons to 20 amino acids and stop.

**Genetic drift** (ch. 8). Change in allele frequencies by chance, strongest in small populations.

**Genotype.** An organism's genetic constitution, or its alleles at a specified locus. See chapter 7.

**Glycolysis** (ch. 5). The oxygen-free first stage of sugar breakdown, yielding 2 ATP.

**Herd immunity** (ch. 14). The threshold of immune individuals above which transmission chains break; about 95 percent for measles.

**Heritability** (ch. 7). The fraction of variation in a trait, in a given population and environment, associated with genetic variation. Not the fraction of an individual's trait caused by genes.

**Homeostasis** (ch. 12). Maintenance of a constant internal environment by negative feedback.

**Homology** (ch. 8). Similarity due to common ancestry, as in the shared bones of a bat's wing and your hand.

**Hox genes** (ch. 15). The conserved genes, arranged in the same order as the body parts they specify, that lay out an animal.

**Induced pluripotent stem cell** (ch. 15). An adult cell reprogrammed to an embryonic-like state by four factors.

**Innate immunity** (ch. 14). The fast, general, inherited arm of defense.

**Keystone species** (ch. 16). A species whose effect on its community far exceeds its abundance.

**Kin selection** (ch. 8). The spread of traits that help relatives, who carry copies of the same genes.

**Krebs (citric acid) cycle** (ch. 5). The mitochondrial pathway that finishes breaking down fuel and loads electron carriers.

**Lipid** (ch. 4). Fats, oils, phospholipids, steroids; the membrane and storage molecules.

**Long-term potentiation** (ch. 13). The lasting strengthening of a synapse after repeated use; the physical basis of memory.

**Meiosis** (ch. 7). The double division that halves chromosome number and shuffles alleles to make gametes.

**Membrane** (ch. 3). The double layer of phospholipids that bounds every cell.

**Metabolism** (ch. 5). The whole network of a cell's chemical reactions; catabolism breaks down, anabolism builds up.

**Michaelis–Menten equation** (ch. 4). The relation between enzyme rate and substrate concentration.

**Microbiome** (ch. 10). The community of microbes living in or on a body; about 38 trillion cells in a human.

**Mitochondrion** (ch. 3, 5). The organelle that makes most of a cell's ATP; a captured bacterium.

**Mitosis** (ch. 3). Ordinary cell division, producing two identical daughter cells.

**Mutation** (ch. 6). A change in DNA sequence; about 60 to 70 new ones per child.

**Mycorrhiza** (ch. 11). The partnership between fungi and plant roots, present in 80 to 90 percent of land plants.

**Natural selection** (ch. 8). Differential survival and reproduction of heritable variants.

**Neuron** (ch. 13). A nerve cell; about 86 billion in a human brain.

**Neurotransmitter** (ch. 13). A chemical released at a synapse to signal the next cell.

**Neutral theory** (ch. 8). Most molecular evolution is by drift of changes that selection does not see.

**Nitrogen fixation** (ch. 10, 11). Converting nitrogen gas into usable compounds; done by some bacteria and, industrially, by the Haber-Bosch process.

**Oncogene.** A gene whose altered or excessive activity can promote cancer. See chapter 6.

**Organelle** (ch. 3). A membrane-bounded compartment inside a eukaryotic cell.

**Pangenome** (ch. 18). A reference made from many individuals' genomes rather than one.

**Phenotype.** An observable trait produced through genotype, environment, and development. See chapter 7.

**Phloem.** Plant vascular tissue that transports sugars and other organic substances between sources and sinks. See chapter 11.

**Photosynthesis** (ch. 5). Using light energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen.

**Pleiotropy** (ch. 7). One gene affecting several traits.

**Polygenic** (ch. 7). Influenced by many genes of small effect; most traits are.

**Polygenic score** (ch. 7). A sum of many small-effect variants used to estimate risk; weak and poorly transferable across ancestries.

**Prime editing** (ch. 18). Writing short new sequences at a chosen site without a double-strand break.

**Prokaryote** (ch. 3). A cell without a nucleus: bacteria and archaea.

**Protein** (ch. 4). A folded chain of amino acids; the working molecules of the cell.

**Recessive allele.** An allele whose associated phenotype is masked in that heterozygote. Dominance is not a measure of an allele's frequency, value, or strength. See chapter 7.

**Regulatory T cell** (ch. 14). A suppressor lymphocyte that holds back self-reactive cells; subject of the 2025 Nobel Prize.

**Replication** (ch. 6). Copying DNA, using each strand as a template.

**Ribosome** (ch. 3, 6). The machine that translates messenger RNA into protein.

**RNA** (ch. 6). Ribonucleic acid; messenger, transfer, ribosomal, and regulatory forms.

**RuBisCO** (ch. 5). The enzyme that fixes carbon dioxide; the most abundant protein on Earth and one of the slowest.

**Senescence, cellular** (ch. 15). The state of a cell that has stopped dividing but persists and secretes inflammatory signals.

**Species** (ch. 8). Populations that interbreed in nature and not with others; the definition has known exceptions.

**Stem cell** (ch. 15). A cell that can divide indefinitely and become other cell types.

**Synapse** (ch. 13). The junction where one neuron signals the next.

**Transcription factor** (ch. 6). A protein that binds DNA and switches genes on or off; about 1,600 in humans.

**Transcription.** Making RNA using a DNA template. See chapter 6.

**Translation.** Making an amino-acid chain from an mRNA sequence at a ribosome. See chapter 6.

**Transposable element** (ch. 6). A DNA sequence that copies itself around the genome; about 45 percent of ours.

**Tumor suppressor.** A gene whose normal activity helps restrain cancer; loss of its function can contribute to malignancy. See chapter 6.

**Vaccine** (ch. 14). A preparation that creates immune memory without causing the disease.

**Virus** (ch. 10). Genetic material in a protein coat, reproducing only inside a host cell.

**Xylem.** Plant vascular tissue that transports water and dissolved minerals, mainly from roots upward, and provides support. See chapter 11.

## The genetic code

Read the messenger RNA three bases at a time, from the start codon.

| First base ↓ / second base → | U | C | A | G |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **U** | UUU/UUC Phe; UUA/UUG Leu | UCN Ser | UAU/UAC Tyr; UAA/UAG **stop** | UGU/UGC Cys; UGA **stop**; UGG Trp |
| **C** | CUN Leu | CCN Pro | CAU/CAC His; CAA/CAG Gln | CGN Arg |
| **A** | AUU/AUC/AUA Ile; AUG **Met, start** | ACN Thr | AAU/AAC Asn; AAA/AAG Lys | AGU/AGC Ser; AGA/AGG Arg |
| **G** | GUN Val | GCN Ala | GAU/GAC Asp; GAA/GAG Glu | GGN Gly |

"N" means any of the four bases in the third position. The code is redundant (most amino acids have several codons), which is why many single-letter mutations are silent, and it is essentially universal across life.

## Classification, with the ranks

Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species. For humans:

| Rank | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | Eukarya | Cells with nuclei |
| Kingdom | Animalia | Multicellular, mobile, eat others |
| Phylum | Chordata | Nerve cord, notochord |
| Class | Mammalia | Milk, hair, warm-blooded |
| Order | Primates | Grasping hands, forward eyes |
| Family | Hominidae | Great apes |
| Genus | *Homo* | Large-brained members of the human line |
| Species | *Homo sapiens* | Us, ~300,000 years old |

Species names are always written *Genus species*, italicized, with the genus capitalized and the species not.

## The main model organisms

| Organism | Why it is used | Notable results |
|---|---|---|
| *Escherichia coli* | Grows in 20 minutes, easy genetics | Gene regulation, the genetic code, recombinant DNA |
| *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (yeast) | Simplest eukaryote, easy genetics | Cell cycle, synthetic chromosomes |
| *Caenorhabditis elegans* (worm) | 959 cells, transparent, fixed lineage | Programmed cell death, microRNA, aging genes |
| *Drosophila melanogaster* (fly) | Fast, cheap, powerful genetics | Genes on chromosomes, the body-plan toolkit, the first whole-brain connectome |
| *Danio rerio* (zebrafish) | Transparent embryos, vertebrate | Development, organ regeneration |
| *Mus musculus* (mouse) | Mammal, engineerable genome | Immunology, cancer, neuroscience, aging |
| *Arabidopsis thaliana* | Small genome, six-week generation | Plant development, hormones, immunity |
