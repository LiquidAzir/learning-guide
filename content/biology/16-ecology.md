## What changes when organisms become an ecosystem?

Everything so far has been about organisms. This chapter is about their interactions and their totals: how many there are, what they do to each other and to the chemistry of the planet, and what humans have done to all of it. It is the chapter with the most numbers and the most disputes about numbers.

## Energy through an ecosystem

Energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat, and on the way it passes up a food chain, losing most of itself at each step. Plants capture 1 to 2 percent of incoming sunlight (chapter 5); herbivores that eat plants convert about 10 percent of that into their own biomass, the rest being respired away or not eaten; carnivores do the same again. This roughly tenfold loss per level is why food chains are short (rarely more than four or five links), why top predators are rare and need large ranges, and why eating plants feeds more people per hectare than eating animals.[^1]

Some substances do the opposite of energy: they concentrate as they go up. A chemical that dissolves in fat and resists breakdown is not excreted, so a predator accumulates everything its prey ever ate. This is **biomagnification**, and the classic case is the pesticide DDT, which reached concentrations in fish-eating birds tens of thousands of times those in the surrounding water and thinned their eggshells until they broke under the parent. Rachel Carson assembled the evidence in *Silent Spring* (1962), a book usually credited with starting modern environmentalism; the United States banned DDT in 1972, and bald eagles, ospreys, and peregrine falcons recovered. The same logic governs mercury in tuna and persistent industrial chemicals today.[^1]

Matter, unlike energy, cycles. The **carbon cycle**: photosynthesis pulls carbon dioxide from air into living matter, respiration and decomposition return it, and geological processes bury and release it over millions of years. Human fossil-fuel burning has added carbon from the geological pool to the fast pool, raising atmospheric carbon dioxide from about 280 parts per million before industrialization to over 420 today. The **nitrogen cycle**: bacteria fix nitrogen gas into usable forms, and industrial fixation now roughly doubles the natural rate (chapter 11), with runoff creating oxygen-dead zones in coastal waters. The **water** and **phosphorus** cycles complete the set. These cycles are what "the biosphere" means as a working system, and biology's largest contribution to earth science is that living things run them.[^2]

## What holds a community together

**Competition** for a limited resource tends to exclude: two species with identical needs cannot coexist indefinitely, so coexisting species divide the resource in space, time, or kind. **Predation** limits prey populations and, by limiting the dominant competitor, can allow more species to coexist. **Mutualism** benefits both parties (pollination, mycorrhizas, gut microbes). **Parasitism** benefits one at the other's expense, and parasites are a large share of all species.

Some species matter far more than their abundance suggests. Robert Paine showed this in 1966 by removing a single starfish species from a rocky shore and watching the community collapse from fifteen species to eight as mussels took over; he coined **keystone species** for the pattern.[^3] Sea otters keep sea urchins from destroying kelp forests. Wolves returned to Yellowstone in 1995 changed elk behavior and, through it, vegetation and stream banks, though the size of that cascade has been debated and later analyses found it smaller and more complicated than the popular version.

**Succession**, the sequence by which a cleared area is recolonized, and **stability**, whether a diverse ecosystem resists disturbance better, are the field's classic questions. The current view on diversity and stability: more diverse communities are generally more productive and more stable in aggregate, while individual populations within them may fluctuate more, and the experimental evidence is good.[^4]

## The numbers of life

**Biomass.** All life on Earth totals about 550 gigatons of carbon, which is the mass of the carbon in it and roughly a fifth of its wet weight. Plants are 450 of that, 80 percent; bacteria 70; fungi 12; animals 2, of which arthropods are about half and fish 0.7. Humans are 0.06 gigatons, and livestock 0.1: together, humans and their domestic animals now outweigh all wild mammals by about 20 to 1, and domestic poultry outweigh all wild birds by about 3 to 1.[^5] Since the rise of civilization, plant biomass has roughly halved and wild mammal biomass has fallen by about 85 percent.

**Species.** About 2.1 million species have been described; the total is estimated between 3 and 100 million depending on method, with the most-cited estimate around 8.7 million eukaryotes, of which most are unnamed insects and fungi, and with microbial diversity essentially uncountable by current methods.

**Extinction.** The background rate from the fossil record is roughly 0.1 to 2 extinctions per million species-years: watch a million species for a year and you would expect between a tenth of one and two of them to vanish. Current documented rates are tens to hundreds of times higher, and about 28 percent of the 172,000-plus species assessed by the IUCN are threatened. Whether this constitutes a sixth mass extinction is a matter of definition and projection: the documented extinctions so far, about 900 species since 1500, are far short of the 75 percent losses of the big five, but the trajectory, if sustained for a few centuries, reaches them.[^6]

## The state of the planet in 2026

Four measured trends, stated with their disputes.

**Wildlife populations.** The Living Planet Index reports a 73 percent average decline in monitored vertebrate populations from 1970 to 2020. This is widely misread: it is the average change across populations, not the fraction of animals or species lost, and roughly half the monitored populations are stable or increasing. A 2024 analysis argued that the index's method biases it toward decline; its authors defend it. The direction is not in dispute; the headline number is.[^7]

**Insects.** A synthesis of 923 insect assemblages from 106 long-term studies found that the steepest declines are in the species that were formerly most abundant, which matters because abundant insects do the pollinating and the decomposing. Earlier estimates of 40 percent of species facing extinction were based on biased sampling and are not supported; a defensible figure for terrestrial insect abundance is around 9 percent decline per decade, with freshwater insects increasing.

**Coral.** The fourth global bleaching event, declared in April 2024 and confirmed by the US ocean agency in June 2026 to have ended in mid-2025, exposed 84 percent of the world's reef area to bleaching-level heat stress, the largest on record. That figure measures exposure to heat stress, not confirmed coral death, which varied widely between reefs. Reefs support about a quarter of marine species on less than 1 percent of the ocean floor, and the projection under continued warming is that most tropical reefs will be lost, which is one of the clearest ecological consequences of climate change.[^8]

**Carbon sinks.** Land and ocean together absorb roughly half of human carbon emissions each year. In 2023 the land sink nearly collapsed, to about a fifth of its usual size, under Amazon drought, record Canadian wildfires, and El Niño. One year is not a trend and it partially recovered, but it is a demonstration of what a warmer world does to the biosphere's ability to absorb what we emit. Modeling suggests that 10 to 47 percent of the Amazon could face compounding stresses by 2050 capable of pushing forest to degraded or savanna states.[^9]

## What is being done

The Kunming-Montreal framework, agreed in 2022, sets 23 targets for 2030, of which the best known is protecting 30 percent of land and sea ("30x30"). Progress: about 17 percent of land and 8 percent of ocean were protected at the last count, funding is far short of the target, and the first global review comes at the 2026 conference. Conservation does work when it is done: the green sea turtle moved from Endangered to Least Concern in 2025 after decades of nest protection, humpback whales and several raptors have recovered, and analyses find that protected areas and species-specific programs measurably reduce extinction risk. The gap is not knowledge but resources and political will.[^10]

:::key
Energy flows one way through ecosystems, losing about 90 percent per level, while matter cycles; living things run the planet's carbon and nitrogen chemistry, and human industry now rivals them in both. The living world's totals are dominated by plants and microbes, and humans plus livestock now outweigh wild mammals twentyfold. The declines are real, several of the headline numbers are more contested than they appear, and the interventions that have been tried have worked where they were tried.
:::

## Summary

- About 10 percent of energy passes to each next level of a food chain, which is why food chains are short and top predators rare; matter, unlike energy, cycles.
- Communities are structured by competition, predation, mutualism, and parasitism, and some keystone species matter far beyond their abundance.
- Life totals about 550 gigatons of carbon, 80 percent of it plants; humans and livestock now outweigh wild mammals about twentyfold, and plant biomass has roughly halved since civilization began.
- Extinction rates are tens to hundreds of times background and 28 percent of assessed species are threatened, though documented extinctions remain far short of a mass extinction so far.
- The 73 percent Living Planet figure is an average of population changes and is methodologically disputed; coral bleaching in 2023 to 2025 was the largest on record; the 2023 land carbon sink nearly failed; conservation demonstrably works where it is funded.
