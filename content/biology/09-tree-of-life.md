## Recap

Chapter 8 gave the mechanism. This chapter is the result: what has actually lived, in what order, and how the whole of it is related. It is biology's history chapter, and it ends with us.

## Three domains, not two

Until 1977 life was divided into prokaryotes (bacteria) and eukaryotes (everything with a nucleus). Then Carl Woese, comparing the sequences of ribosomal RNA, a molecule every organism has and which changes slowly enough to record deep history, found that the "bacteria" were two groups as different from each other as either was from us. Life has three **domains**: **Bacteria**, **Archaea**, and **Eukarya**.[^1] Archaea look like bacteria under a microscope but differ in their membranes, cell walls, and information-processing machinery, which resembles ours. Woese's proposal was resisted for a decade and is now standard, and it means that the visible world of plants and animals is one branch of one domain, a twig on a tree whose trunk and most of whose limbs are microbial.

Current evidence, from the Asgard archaea (chapter 3), suggests the tree has two primary domains rather than three: eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, so the picture is Bacteria on one side and Archaea, with Eukarya nested inside them, on the other. The argument is active and the resolution matters for how life's earliest history is drawn.[^2]

The two things to notice: life was single-celled for the first three billion years, five sixths of its history, and the interesting parts came fast and late.[^3]

## The Cambrian explosion

For three billion years life was microbial mats and, at the end, soft strange fronds. Then, beginning about 539 million years ago, within perhaps 20 million years, nearly every animal body plan that exists today, and some that do not, appears in the fossil record: arthropods with jointed legs and compound eyes, molluscs, worms with guts, and the first chordates, ancestors of everything with a backbone. The Burgess Shale in Canada and Chengjiang in China preserve them in extraordinary detail, including soft parts.[^4]

Explanations, none exclusive: oxygen rose past the level a large active animal needs; predation began, setting off an arms race of shells, eyes, and speed (the first eyes appear here); the genetic toolkit for building bodies (the **Hox** genes, chapter 15) had just been assembled; and mineralized skeletons made fossils possible, so part of the "explosion" is an artifact of what preserves. Molecular clocks suggest the lineages diverged somewhat earlier than the fossils show, so the explosion was probably a rapid diversification of forms rather than of lineages. It remains, for a process with no foresight, the most impressive twenty million years on record.

## Five extinctions, and a sixth

Life has nearly ended five times.[^5]

| Extinction | Age (Mya) | Marine species lost | Probable cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| End-Ordovician | 444 | ~85% | Glaciation and sea-level fall |
| Late Devonian | 372 | ~75% | Ocean anoxia, possibly land plants |
| End-Permian | 252 | ~81% | Siberian Traps volcanism, warming, anoxia |
| End-Triassic | 201 | ~80% | Volcanism, carbon dioxide, warming |
| End-Cretaceous | 66 | ~76% | Asteroid impact at Chicxulub |

The end-Permian, "the Great Dying," is the worst: over about 60,000 years, volcanism in Siberia released enough carbon dioxide to raise ocean temperatures by 8 to 10°C, acidify the water, and strip its oxygen, and it took ten million years for ecosystems to recover. The end-Cretaceous is the best understood: a 10-kilometer asteroid struck the Yucatán, and the iridium layer, shocked quartz, and the crater itself confirmed the 1980 hypothesis of Luis and Walter Alvarez that was ridiculed when proposed.[^6] Without it, mammals would probably still be small nocturnal insect-eaters.

Whether we are in a sixth is discussed in chapter 16. The short version: current extinction rates are estimated at tens to hundreds of times the background rate, which if sustained would produce a mass extinction on a geological timescale, though the total number of documented extinctions so far is far below the five events above.

## The branches

**Bacteria and Archaea** dominate by number (about 10^30 cells), by chemical diversity (they run metabolisms no eukaryote can: eating rock, breathing sulfate, fixing nitrogen), and by habitat range (boiling vents, acid mine drainage, kilometers underground, the inside of you). Most have never been grown in a laboratory and are known only from environmental DNA. Chapter 10 covers them.

**Eukaryotes** divide into several major groups, of which the familiar three are small parts. **Animals** (about 1.5 million described species, mostly insects; total estimates run from 3 to 30 million) are multicellular, mobile, and eat other organisms. **Plants** (about 380,000 species) make their own food from sunlight and built the land ecosystems. **Fungi** (about 150,000 described, with a current estimate of 2.2 to 3.8 million) digest externally and absorb, and they are more closely related to animals than to plants. The rest, the **protists**, are not a natural group but a grab-bag of everything else: amoebae, ciliates, diatoms, kelps, malaria parasites, slime molds, and the several independent lineages that separately invented multicellularity.[^7]

**Viruses** sit outside the tree, and whether they belong on it is unsettled. They are genetic material in a protein coat, with no metabolism and no ability to reproduce alone; they hijack cells. They are also the most numerous biological entities on Earth (about 10^31 particles), they kill about a fifth of the ocean's microbial biomass every day, and they have moved genes between lineages throughout history, including into our own genome. Chapter 10 treats them.

## Us

Humans are apes, and the evidence is not subtle: we share about 98.8 percent of our DNA with chimpanzees and bonobos, our closest living relatives, from whom our lineage split six to seven million years ago.[^8] The hominin fossil record now includes some two dozen species, of which the arc is: bipedal apes with small brains (*Australopithecus*, including "Lucy," 3.2 million years old); the first stone tools 3.3 million years ago; the genus *Homo* from about 2.8 million years ago, with brains that roughly doubled over two million years, and about tripled from the australopithecine starting point; *Homo erectus*, the first to leave Africa and to use fire; and, from about 300,000 years ago, our own species in Africa. We were not alone: Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, Denisovans in the east (known first from a finger bone, given a face in 2025 by proteins recovered from a Chinese skull), and small-bodied species on Flores and Luzon shared the planet with us until about 40,000 years ago, and living people outside Africa carry a percent or two of Neanderthal DNA and, in Oceania, several percent Denisovan.[^9]

What made the difference is argued. Bipedalism freed the hands; tool use and meat-eating supported a metabolically expensive brain; fire made food digestible; language, at an unknown date, made knowledge cumulative. The distinctive human trait is not intelligence in the individual sense but **cumulative culture**: we are the species that can build on what the previous generation figured out, so that no one person needs to invent the boat, and this is why our species alone has an archaeological record that changes over time. The history guide takes the story from there.

:::key
The tree of life is mostly microbial, mostly single-celled for most of its history, and mostly not visible. Complex life is a late, contingent development that depended on an oxygen atmosphere made by bacteria, a merger of two cells that happened once, and at least five near-annihilations that cleared the field for whatever survived. Humans are one twig on one branch, three hundred thousand years old on a four-billion-year tree, and the branch we sit on exists because an asteroid hit the Yucatán.
:::

## Summary

- Life has three domains, Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya, discovered by comparing ribosomal RNA in 1977; current evidence nests eukaryotes within the archaea.
- Life was single-celled for its first three billion years; the eukaryotic cell appeared about two billion years ago and animals only in the last 600 million.
- The Cambrian explosion produced nearly all animal body plans in about 20 million years, driven by oxygen, predation, and the developmental toolkit.
- Five mass extinctions removed 75 to 81 percent of marine species each; the end-Permian was the worst and the end-Cretaceous asteroid cleared the way for mammals.
- Humans are African apes that split from chimpanzees six to seven million years ago, shared the planet with Neanderthals and Denisovans until about 40,000 years ago, and are distinguished by cumulative culture.
