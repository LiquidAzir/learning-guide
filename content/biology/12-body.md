## Recap

Chapters 3 to 6 built the cell; this chapter builds the organism from it. The animal body is a solution to a logistics problem: a single cell can exchange everything it needs with its surroundings by diffusion, but diffusion is hopeless over more than about a millimeter, so any animal larger than that needs pumps, pipes, and pumps for the pipes. Everything below follows from that.

## Homeostasis, the organizing idea

Claude Bernard observed in the 1860s that the cells of a complex animal live not in the outside world but in an internal environment, the fluid around them, and that health consists in keeping that fluid constant. Walter Cannon named it **homeostasis** in 1926. The body holds core temperature at 37°C ± 0.5, blood pH between 7.35 and 7.45, blood glucose between about 4 and 7 millimoles per liter, and blood sodium, calcium, and oxygen within similarly tight bands, because the enzymes of chapter 4 work only in that narrow world.[^1]

The mechanism is nearly always **negative feedback**: a sensor detects a deviation, a controller compares it to a set point, and effectors push back. Body temperature falls; the hypothalamus detects it; blood vessels in the skin constrict, shivering begins, and metabolic rate rises. Blood glucose rises after a meal; the pancreas releases insulin; cells take up glucose and the liver stores it. The failures of these loops are much of medicine: diabetes is a failure of the glucose loop, hypertension of the blood-pressure loop, fever a deliberate resetting of the temperature set point by the immune system.

## The systems, with numbers

**Circulatory.** The heart is two pumps in one: the right sends blood to the lungs, the left to the body. It beats about 100,000 times a day, moving about 5 liters a minute at rest and up to 25 during hard exercise, through some 100,000 kilometers of vessels of which nearly all the length is capillaries, one cell thick, where the actual exchange happens. No cell in the body is more than about 100 micrometers from a capillary. Blood is about 45 percent cells by volume, nearly all red cells, of which you have about 25 trillion, each carrying about 270 million hemoglobin molecules and living about 120 days.[^2]

**Respiratory.** The lungs contain roughly 480 million alveoli with a combined surface area of about 130 square meters, genuinely about half a tennis court, and a barrier to the blood 0.5 micrometers thick. You breathe about 12 times a minute at rest, moving 6 to 8 liters of air a minute, and the driver of breathing is not low oxygen but rising carbon dioxide, which is why hyperventilating before a breath-hold dive is dangerous: it removes the urge to breathe without adding much oxygen.

**Digestive.** A tube nine meters long with specialized regions: the stomach's acid (pH 1.5 to 3.5) kills microbes and starts protein digestion; the small intestine, with a surface area of about 30 square meters after villi and microvilli are counted, does nearly all absorption; the large intestine reclaims water and houses the microbiome (chapter 10). The liver, the largest internal organ, is the body's chemical plant: it stores and releases glucose, makes plasma proteins and bile, and detoxifies drugs and alcohol.

**Excretory.** The kidneys filter about 180 liters of plasma a day and return more than 99 percent of it, excreting 1 to 2 liters of urine. They are the body's regulator of water, salt, pH, and blood pressure, and dialysis, which replaces only the filtering, is a poor substitute for the regulating.

**Musculoskeletal.** 206 bones in an adult, remodeled continuously so that the skeleton is replaced about every ten years; over 600 skeletal muscles, which shorten by sliding protein filaments past each other, powered by ATP, in the mechanism worked out in 1954 and confirmed since.[^3] Bone is living tissue that thickens under load and thins without it, which is why astronauts lose bone density and why weight-bearing exercise prevents osteoporosis.

**Endocrine.** Glands releasing **hormones** into the blood to act on distant tissues: insulin and glucagon from the pancreas (glucose), thyroid hormone (metabolic rate), cortisol (stress, immune suppression, glucose), adrenaline (fight or flight), growth hormone, and the sex hormones. The hypothalamus and pituitary at the base of the brain sit at the top of most of these loops, which is where the nervous and hormonal systems join.

**Nervous and immune.** Chapters 13 and 14. **Reproductive.** Meiosis makes the gametes (chapter 7); hormones from the pituitary drive the cycles that release them; fertilization joins one of each, and chapter 15 takes the resulting cell from there.

**Integumentary.** Skin: about 2 square meters, the largest organ, a barrier against water loss and infection, a thermostat, and a sensor.

## What the numbers are for

Two examples of what knowing them buys you.

**Why you cannot hold your breath long.** Blood carries about a liter of oxygen, and you use about 250 milliliters a minute at rest, so the supply is roughly four minutes, less with any exertion, and the brain fails first because it uses about 20 percent of the body's oxygen for 2 percent of its weight. Trained free divers extend this with lung packing, slowed metabolism, and tolerance of carbon dioxide, not by storing much more oxygen.

**Why a large animal is not just a scaled-up small one.** Volume grows as the cube of length and surface area as the square, so a larger animal has less surface per unit of volume; it loses heat more slowly (which is why small mammals must eat constantly and large ones can fast), needs proportionally thicker bones, and has a slower metabolic rate per gram. Across species, metabolic rate scales roughly as body mass to the 3/4 power (Kleiber's law), so an elephant, ten thousand times a mouse's mass, uses only about a thousand times as much energy; heart rate and lifespan scale in the opposite direction, so that most mammals get roughly a billion heartbeats, with humans a conspicuous outlier at about three billion.[^4]

:::math Scaling and surface area
Take an animal of characteristic length $L$. Its surface area scales as $L^2$ and its volume, and so its mass $M$, as $L^3$. Therefore

$$\text{surface area} \propto M^{2/3}$$

If heat loss were proportional to surface area and heat production to mass, a doubling of length would multiply mass by 8 and surface by 4, halving the heat lost per gram. That is why an elephant needs ears to radiate heat and a shrew needs to eat most of its waking hours.

Metabolic rate, though, does not scale as $M^{2/3}$. Measured across species from bacteria to whales it goes as

$$B = B_0 M^{3/4}$$

where $B$ is metabolic rate, $M$ is body mass, and $B_0$ is a constant that differs between groups. This is **Kleiber's law**, observed since 1932; explanations based on the geometry of branching supply networks (blood vessels, airways) predict the 3/4 exponent, and the debate over whether the exponent is exactly 3/4 or varies is ongoing.[^5]

*Using it.* Drug doses for animals of very different sizes are often scaled by $M^{3/4}$ rather than by mass, because clearance follows metabolic rate. A dose per kilogram derived in a mouse will overdose a human if scaled linearly.
:::

## Exercise, sleep, and the two things that reliably work

Two interventions have effects on human health so large and so well replicated that they are worth stating in a biology guide.

**Exercise.** Regular physical activity lowers all-cause mortality by roughly 20 to 35 percent in large prospective cohorts, improves insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, bone density, mood, and cognition, and most of the benefit comes from the first 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, with diminishing returns after.[^6] The mechanisms are many: muscles take up glucose without insulin during contraction, vessels dilate better, mitochondria multiply, and muscle releases signaling molecules that act on other organs.

**Sleep.** Adults need about 7 to 9 hours; chronic restriction impairs glucose tolerance, immune function, memory consolidation, and mood within days, and the effects on performance are larger than most people estimate for themselves. What sleep is for is still not settled; the leading candidates are memory consolidation (well supported), metabolic and synaptic housekeeping, and clearance of waste from brain tissue, the last of which was strengthened by a 2013 result and challenged by a 2024 one that found clearance slower, not faster, during sleep. That the need is real is not in doubt; the reason is.[^7]

:::story The stomach with a window
In 1822 a French-Canadian trapper named Alexis St. Martin was shot in the side at close range. He survived, but the wound healed into a permanent opening into his stomach. The army surgeon who treated him, William Beaumont, realized what he had and spent a decade running experiments through it: tying pieces of food on a string, lowering them in, and pulling them out at intervals to see what had dissolved; extracting gastric juice and testing whether it digested food outside the body (it did, settling an argument about whether digestion was chemical or mechanical); observing that the stomach lining reddened when St. Martin was angry.[^8] The 238 experiments Beaumont published in 1833 founded the physiology of digestion. The relationship was also exploitative: St. Martin was Beaumont's servant under contract, was paid little, tried repeatedly to leave, and outlived him by 27 years. The history of physiology has many episodes like this, and the modern rules on informed consent exist because of them.
:::

## Summary

- Bodies exist because diffusion fails beyond about a millimeter; every system is a delivery or removal solution.
- Homeostasis holds temperature, pH, glucose, and salts in narrow bands by negative feedback; most chronic disease is a broken feedback loop.
- The numbers matter: 100,000 km of vessels, 70 m² of lung, 30 m² of gut, 180 liters filtered daily by the kidneys, a skeleton replaced every decade.
- Surface area scales as mass to the 2/3 and metabolic rate as mass to the 3/4, which sets how fast animals of different sizes must eat, cool, and dose drugs.
- Exercise and sleep have unusually large, well-replicated effects on health; what sleep is actually for remains argued.
