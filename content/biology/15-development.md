## How do cells with similar DNA become different tissues?

Chapter 7 covered how genes pass between generations. This chapter covers what they do in one lifetime: build an organism from a single cell, run it, and lose the ability to maintain it. Development and aging are treated together because the second is increasingly understood as the failure of the maintenance programs the first installs.

## From one cell to a body

A fertilized egg divides into two, four, eight, and within days into a hollow ball that folds in on itself, producing three layers that become, respectively, the gut and its organs, the muscles and bones and blood, and the skin and nervous system. By nine weeks a human embryo has every organ in rough form. The question that puzzled biologists for two centuries is how, with no blueprint, no foreman, and every cell holding the same genome, cells come to differ and to be in the right places.

The answer has three parts.[^1]

**Gradients.** The egg is not uniform: molecules are unevenly distributed, and after fertilization more are made and diffuse from sources, producing concentration gradients across the embryo. A cell reads the local concentration and switches on a corresponding set of genes: high concentration means one fate, medium another, low a third. Lewis Wolpert's "French flag" model of 1969 stated this idea, and the first real example was found in the fruit fly, where a gradient of the Bicoid protein laid down by the mother tells cells how far from the head they are.

**Signals between neighbors.** Cells tell each other what to become. A cell that has committed to one fate often inhibits its neighbors from doing the same, which produces regular spacing (of hair follicles, of leaves, of the bristles on a fly). Whole organizing centers exist: transplant the dorsal lip of an amphibian embryo to another embryo's flank and it induces a second body axis, complete with a second head and spine. Hans Spemann and Hilde Mangold did this in 1924, in an experiment that is still the most striking demonstration in the field, and Spemann received the Nobel Prize; Mangold, who performed the experiments, died in a household accident at 26, before it was awarded.[^2]

**A shared toolkit.** In the late 1970s Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard and Eric Wieschaus set out to find every gene needed to build a fly, by mutating flies at random and screening tens of thousands of offspring for embryos that were malformed in specific ways. They found about 120 genes and grouped them into a logical hierarchy: genes that set the head-to-tail axis, then genes that divide the embryo into broad bands, then into segments, then genes that tell each segment what to become.[^3] The last group, the **Hox** genes, whose colinear arrangement Edward Lewis had worked out in the fly's bithorax complex and for which he shared the 1995 Nobel Prize with them, are laid out on the chromosome in the same order as the body parts they specify, and a mutation in one can turn an antenna into a leg.

The discovery that made this general came next: the same Hox genes, in the same order, doing the same job, are present in mice, in fish, and in humans. A mouse Hox gene inserted into a fly works. The genetic toolkit for building an animal body was assembled once, before the Cambrian, and every animal since has been a variation on its settings. This is the central insight of **evolutionary developmental biology**, and it explains how large changes in form can come from small changes in when and where a conserved gene is switched on, which is how a fin becomes a limb and a limb becomes a wing.[^4]

## Stem cells

A **stem cell** can divide indefinitely and can become other cell types. The most potent are the **embryonic stem cells** of the early embryo, which can become anything. Adults keep more limited ones: blood stem cells in bone marrow (the basis of bone-marrow transplants since 1956), stem cells in the gut lining, skin, and muscle.

In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka showed that a mature cell can be reversed. Adding four transcription factors, screened down from 24 candidates, turned an adult mouse skin cell into something functionally equivalent to an embryonic stem cell: an **induced pluripotent stem cell** (iPSC). It worked in human cells the next year, won the 2012 Nobel Prize, and removed both the ethical objection to embryonic cells and the rejection problem, since the cells can be made from the patient.[^5]

What iPSCs have delivered so far: disease models in a dish, from patients with genetic conditions; drug screening; and, since 2020, the first clinical trials in which iPSC-derived neurons, heart cells, and retinal cells are transplanted. In 2025 two trials reported transplanting stem-cell-derived dopamine neurons into the brains of people with Parkinson's disease; the grafts survived, made dopamine, caused no tumors, and several patients improved, in small open-label trials with no control group.[^6] Stem-cell-derived **organoids**, miniature guts, kidneys, livers, and brains grown in culture, and stem-cell-based **embryo models** that mimic the first two weeks of human development, are now standard research tools and raise ethical questions the field is working through: these models are not embryos, cannot become a fetus, and are governed by guidelines that forbid transferring them to a uterus.

## Aging

Bodies maintain themselves for decades and then, on a species-specific schedule, stop doing it well. Aging is the increase in the probability of death with time: in humans, mortality risk doubles roughly every eight years after 30, a regularity noticed by Benjamin Gompertz in 1825.[^7] Lifespans across species vary by four orders of magnitude, from mayflies to the 400-year Greenland shark and the bristlecone pines and clonal organisms that pass ten thousand years, and some animals show no measurable increase in mortality with age at all.

The field has converged on a list of **hallmarks of aging**, published in 2013 and extended to twelve in 2023: genomic instability, telomere shortening, epigenetic changes, loss of protein quality control, disabled autophagy, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem-cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and microbiome disturbance.[^8] Each is measurable, each worsens with age, and interventions on several extend life in animals.

What actually extends lifespan, in animals: **caloric restriction** (30 to 40 percent fewer calories without malnutrition) extends life in yeast, worms, flies, and rodents by up to 50 percent; in monkeys the results were mixed across two long studies, and in humans the evidence is limited to improved markers rather than demonstrated longevity. **Rapamycin**, which inhibits a nutrient-sensing pathway, extends mouse lifespan even when started late, the most robust drug result in the field. **Senolytics**, which kill senescent cells, extend mouse healthspan; the first randomized human trial, in postmenopausal women's bone metabolism in 2024, found no overall benefit but a signal in those with the highest senescent-cell burden. **Partial reprogramming** with Yamanaka factors reverses age markers in mice; the first human trial, of an eye therapy using three of the four factors, began dosing in 2026 and has no efficacy data.[^9]

What actually extends healthy human lifespan, on current evidence: not smoking, physical activity, sleep, a reasonable diet, blood-pressure and cholesterol control, vaccination, and not being poor. The gap between the animal work and this list is the state of the field, and it is worth being blunt about: no drug or supplement has been shown to extend human lifespan, and the longevity industry sells many that have not.

:::key
Development works without a blueprint because cells read local signals and interpret them with a genetic toolkit assembled before the Cambrian and shared by all animals. The same Hox genes lay out a fly and a human, which is why changes in the regulation of a few conserved genes can produce large changes in form. Aging is the failure of the maintenance systems that development installs, described by twelve measurable hallmarks; several interventions extend life in mice, none is proven to do so in people, and the boring list of behaviors remains the only evidence-based way to add healthy years.
:::

:::story The worm that lived twice as long
In 1988 Thomas Johnson reported that a mutation in a single gene, *age-1*, extended the life of the roundworm *C. elegans* by 65 percent. The result was hard to believe: aging was supposed to be the accumulation of a thousand kinds of wear, not something a single gene could govern. In 1993 Cynthia Kenyon's laboratory found that mutating another single gene, *daf-2*, doubled the worm's lifespan, and that the long-lived worms were not merely surviving but remained active and youthful.[^10] *daf-2* turned out to encode a receptor for insulin-like signals, and the pathway is present, doing related things, in flies, mice, and humans, where variants in it are enriched among centenarians. Aging, it emerged, is regulated rather than merely suffered, which is why the field became a field. What has not followed, thirty years on, is a human treatment, and the distance between "regulated in a worm" and "modifiable in a person" is the honest summary of longevity science.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
A neuron and a muscle cell largely share the same genome. What can make them so different?

:::answer Show the reasoning
They express different combinations of genes and maintain different regulatory states. Signals, transcription factors, and chromatin help establish those patterns during development. Having a gene in the genome is different from using it at a particular time and level.
:::

## Summary

- An embryo builds itself through concentration gradients that tell cells where they are, signals between neighboring cells, and organizing centers such as the one Spemann and Mangold transplanted in 1924.
- Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus found the genes that lay out a fly; the same Hox genes, in the same chromosomal order, lay out every animal, so form evolves largely by changing when and where conserved genes are used.
- Stem cells can be made from adult cells by adding four factors (Yamanaka, 2006); stem-cell-derived dopamine neurons, transplanted in Parkinson's trials since 2018, reported their first results in 2025, and organoids and embryo models are now standard tools.
- Human mortality risk doubles about every eight years; aging is described by twelve hallmarks, and caloric restriction, rapamycin, senolytics, and partial reprogramming extend life or healthspan in animals.
- No intervention is proven to extend human lifespan; the established ways to add healthy years are behavioral and medical, not pharmacological.
