## Recap

Chapter 5 covered photosynthesis, the chemistry that makes plants possible. This chapter is about the organisms: how they are built, the problems they solve differently from animals, the fungi that made land life possible and that most people misclassify, and the agriculture that both depends on them and is the largest thing humans do to the planet.

## The problem with standing still

An organism that cannot move has three problems: getting resources that are in two places, defending itself without fleeing, and finding a mate. Plants solve all three by growing rather than moving.

**Resources in two places.** Light and carbon dioxide are above; water and minerals are below. So a plant is built as two systems: **shoots** with leaves spread to catch photons, and **roots** that spread further underground than the shoots do above. Between them run two plumbing systems. **Xylem** carries water up, in dead hollow tubes, pulled by evaporation from the leaves: water molecules stick to each other (chapter 4), so as one evaporates at the top, it drags the column behind it, and the tension can lift water a hundred meters to the top of a redwood with no pump.[^1] **Phloem** carries sugar from leaves to roots and fruit, in living tubes, pushed by pressure. A large tree moves hundreds of liters of water a day this way, and about 97 percent of it evaporates: the price of opening pores (**stomata**) to let carbon dioxide in.

**Defense.** A plant that cannot run must be unappetizing, so plants are the great chemists of the living world, making an estimated hundreds of thousands of compounds to poison, repel, or sicken the things that eat them. Caffeine, nicotine, morphine, capsaicin, cyanide in almonds and cassava, the tannins in tea, and most of the world's drugs and spices are plant defenses that we have learned to enjoy in small doses.[^2] Some plants respond to attack within minutes, thickening cell walls, releasing volatile compounds that summon the predators of their attackers, and signaling to their own other leaves through electrical and chemical waves. Whether they signal to other plants through fungal networks is a live and contested question (below).

**Sex.** Plants send gametes by proxy. **Pollen** carries the male gamete on wind, water, or animal; **flowers** are advertisements that pay insects, birds, and bats in nectar to carry pollen to another flower of the same species, an arrangement that appeared about 130 million years ago and drove the diversification of both flowering plants (now some 300,000 species, the large majority of vascular plants) and insects. **Fruits** pay animals to carry seeds; the sugar is a bribe and the seed is the cargo.[^3]

## How a plant grows

Animals set their body plan in the embryo and then get bigger. Plants build themselves continuously from **meristems**, regions of dividing stem cells at every root and shoot tip, so a plant's form responds to where the light and water are. They also keep the ability to regenerate a whole organism from a fragment, which is why a cutting grows into a plant and no animal does this.

Growth is directed by hormones, of which the classic is **auxin**: it accumulates on the shaded side of a shoot, makes those cells elongate, and the shoot bends toward light. Darwin and his son Francis did the first experiments on this in 1880, covering the tips of grass seedlings and showing that the tip perceives light while the bending happens below.[^4] Others control seed dormancy, fruit ripening (ethylene, a gas, which is why one ripe banana ripens the others), the drop of leaves in autumn, and the response to drought.

## Fungi

Fungi are not plants. They do not photosynthesize; they secrete enzymes into their surroundings, digest food externally, and absorb it. Their cell walls are chitin, like insect shells, not cellulose. And on the molecular tree they sit closer to animals than to plants: the split between the fungal and animal lineages came after both had separated from plants, which is why antifungal drugs are hard to make (a target in a fungus is often present in us too) and why fungal infections in immunocompromised people are so dangerous.[^5]

Most of a fungus is an underground or in-substrate network of threads (**hyphae**) collectively called a **mycelium**; the mushroom is only the fruiting body, the part that makes spores. One honey fungus network in Oregon covers about 9 square kilometers and may be thousands of years old, among the largest organisms known.

Fungi do three things that matter enormously. They **decompose**: they are the main organisms able to break down lignin, the tough polymer in wood, and without them forests would not recycle. They form **mycorrhizas**, partnerships with plant roots in which the fungus extends the root system's reach for water and phosphorus and receives sugar in return; some 80 to 90 percent of land plants have them, and the fossil evidence suggests the partnership is as old as land plants themselves and may have made the colonization of land possible.[^6] And they cause **disease**: the Irish potato blight (an oomycete, a fungus-like organism), Dutch elm disease, wheat rusts, the chytrid fungus that has driven amphibian declines worldwide, and the fungal diseases of bats and snakes.

:::frontier
The "wood-wide web," the claim that trees in a forest are linked by fungal networks through which they share carbon and send warnings, and that "mother trees" preferentially feed their kin, has become a staple of popular science books and documentaries. In 2023 three researchers reviewed the field evidence and concluded that the claims outrun it: common mycorrhizal networks are less well documented in real forests than assumed; where resources do move between plants, the amounts are usually small and the direction and benefit unclear; and the "mother tree" claim about preferential kin support has no peer-reviewed support at all. They also found that positive results are cited far more often than negative ones, so the literature has drifted. The researchers whose work the popular accounts draw on dispute the reading. The underlying biology, that mycorrhizal fungi are essential to plants and that hyphae can connect roots, is not in doubt; what is in doubt is the forest-as-cooperative-network story built on top of it, and this is a good example of how a charming hypothesis can outrun its data.[^7]
:::

## Agriculture

Humans get about 80 percent of their calories from plants, and a startling share from very few of them: wheat, rice, maize, and potatoes together supply roughly half of the calories eaten by our species, and about 15 crops supply 90 percent.[^8] Every one of them was domesticated by prehistoric farmers (history guide, chapter 2) through selection for traits that suit us and not the plant: seeds that stay on the stalk, fruits without seeds, loss of bitter defensive chemicals, and gigantism.

The twentieth century transformed yields twice. The **Haber-Bosch process** (1909) fixes atmospheric nitrogen into fertilizer industrially, doing chemically what bacteria do biologically; it consumes about 1 to 2 percent of world energy and supports, by the standard estimate, roughly half the nitrogen in the human population's protein.[^9] The **Green Revolution** of the 1950s to 1970s bred short-stemmed, fertilizer-responsive, disease-resistant wheat and rice; Norman Borlaug's varieties are credited with averting famine in South Asia, and world grain production roughly tripled while land use rose by about 10 percent. The costs were real too: fertilizer runoff creating oxygen-dead zones in coastal waters, groundwater depletion, pesticide effects, and a narrowing of crop genetic diversity that makes the system fragile.

The current agenda: engineering better photosynthesis (RuBisCO is slow and wasteful, chapter 5, and engineered bypasses of its wasteful side reaction raised biomass by up to 40 percent in tobacco field trials), transferring nitrogen fixation to cereals (which the 2024 discovery of a nitrogen-fixing organelle in an alga makes look less impossible), drought and salt tolerance, gene-edited crops that avoid the regulatory burden of transgenic ones, and reducing the food system's roughly one-third share of greenhouse emissions, of which agriculture proper is about three quarters.[^10]

:::key
Plants solve the problems of a sessile life by growing rather than moving, by chemistry rather than flight, and by paying animals to carry their gametes and seeds. Fungi, which are closer relatives of animals than of plants, recycle the world's wood and partner with the roots of most plants. Between them they make every calorie humans eat, and the modern food supply rests on about fifteen domesticated species, industrial nitrogen fixation, and a few decades of plant breeding.
:::

## Summary

- Plants are built as shoots and roots joined by xylem (water up, pulled by evaporation) and phloem (sugar down, pushed by pressure); most water taken up is lost as the price of admitting carbon dioxide.
- Unable to flee, plants defend themselves chemically, which is the source of most drugs and spices; they grow from meristems throughout life and direct growth with hormones such as auxin.
- Fungi digest externally, are closer to animals than plants, decompose wood, and form mycorrhizal partnerships with 80 to 90 percent of land plants.
- The popular "wood-wide web" account of forests sharing resources and warnings through fungal networks outruns the field evidence, though mycorrhizas themselves are essential.
- Four crops supply about half of human calories; Haber-Bosch nitrogen and Green Revolution breeding tripled grain production, with real environmental costs, and the current agenda is better photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation in cereals, and lower emissions.
