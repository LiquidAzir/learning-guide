## Recap

Chapter 10 described the pathogens. This chapter describes the defense: a distributed system with no central control that generates receptors for molecules that have never existed, distinguishes them from the body's own, remembers, and gets it wrong in ways that produce allergy and autoimmune disease. It is also the system that the last decade's most successful new cancer drugs work on.

## Two systems

**Innate immunity** is fast, general, and ancient, shared in some form by plants and insects. Physical barriers (skin, mucus, stomach acid), chemical ones (antimicrobial peptides, complement proteins that punch holes in bacteria), and cells that eat what they find: macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells. They recognize broad patterns common to whole classes of pathogen, bacterial cell-wall components, viral double-stranded RNA, through a limited set of inherited receptors whose discovery (Beutler, Hoffmann, Steinman) won the 2011 Nobel Prize. The response is inflammation: vessels dilate and leak, cells are recruited, and the area becomes red, hot, swollen, and painful. Innate immunity handles most infections without your noticing.[^1]

**Adaptive immunity**, found only in vertebrates, is slow the first time, specific, and remembers. Its cells are **lymphocytes**: **B cells**, which make **antibodies**, and **T cells**, which kill infected cells (cytotoxic T cells) or direct other cells (helper T cells). Its defining trick is that each lymphocyte carries a different, randomly generated receptor, and the ones that happen to match the invader are selected and multiplied.

## Making a receptor for anything

The immune system must be able to bind molecules that no ancestor ever met, including ones invented last year in a laboratory. It cannot have a gene for each, because there are more possible targets than genes. The solution, found by Susumu Tonegawa in 1976, is that antibody genes are assembled, not inherited whole: each developing B cell cuts and pastes together one segment from each of several pools, adds random letters at the joins, and so produces a unique receptor. The combinatorics give an estimated repertoire of more than 10^13 possible antibodies from a few hundred gene segments, and T cells do the same.[^2]

The problem this creates is obvious: a randomly generated receptor may bind *you*. So developing lymphocytes are screened. In the thymus, T cells that bind the body's own molecules too strongly are killed (**central tolerance**). Only about 2 percent of developing thymocytes leave the thymus at all: most of the rest die because their randomly built receptor binds nothing useful, and a smaller fraction are actively deleted for binding self too strongly. Because the screening cannot present every self molecule, a second layer works in the body: **regulatory T cells**, a suppressor population that actively holds back self-reactive cells that escaped. Their existence was proposed by Shimon Sakaguchi in 1995 against a skeptical field, and the gene behind a fatal autoimmune syndrome in mice and people, FOXP3, was identified by Mary Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell in 2001; Sakaguchi's group then showed in 2003 that FOXP3 is what makes a regulatory T cell. The three shared the 2025 Nobel Prize for this work on **peripheral tolerance**.[^3]

## Clonal selection and memory

When a pathogen arrives, dendritic cells carry pieces of it to a lymph node and display them. The few lymphocytes whose receptors happen to fit are activated and divide, producing an army of clones over about a week: this is **clonal selection**, Frank Macfarlane Burnet's 1957 idea, and it is the reason a first infection takes days to control.[^4] B cells then improve their antibodies in real time: their receptor genes mutate at a high rate and the cells that bind best are selected to divide again, so the antibodies produced late in an infection bind far better than the first ones. It is evolution by natural selection, running inside you, over days.

Afterward, a subset persists as **memory cells**, so that a second encounter produces a response in hours rather than days, often before symptoms. Memory can last decades: antibody responses to smallpox vaccination remain measurable more than 60 years later, and measles immunity is effectively lifelong.[^5]

## Vaccines

A vaccine presents the immune system with something recognizable from a pathogen without the disease, so that memory exists before exposure. The forms:

| Type | How | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Live attenuated | Weakened live pathogen | Measles, mumps, rubella, yellow fever, oral polio |
| Inactivated | Killed pathogen | Polio (injected), hepatitis A, rabies |
| Subunit / conjugate | A purified protein or sugar, sometimes linked to a carrier | Hepatitis B, HPV, pneumococcus, Hib |
| Viral vector | A harmless virus carrying a gene for the target protein | Ebola, some COVID-19 vaccines |
| mRNA | Genetic instructions in a lipid bubble; your cells make the protein briefly | COVID-19 vaccines |

The mRNA platform, built over three decades by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman among others and deployed in 2020, cut the design step from years to days once a sequence is known, because the manufacturing does not change with the target, though the trials still take the better part of a year.[^6] The 2023 Nobel Prize went to Karikó and Weissman for the nucleoside modification that made it work in humans.

Vaccination's record is the largest in medicine: smallpox eradicated, polio nearly, measles deaths cut by over 90 percent from their peak, and an estimated 154 million deaths averted in the 50 years of the WHO's Expanded Programme on Immunization, most of them infants.[^7] **Herd immunity** is why the unvaccinated benefit too: above a threshold that depends on how contagious the disease is (about 95 percent for measles, the most contagious), chains of transmission break. Below it they resume, which is why measles returned to the United States in 2025 with 2,289 cases, the most since 1991, and 2026 has been worse still, passing 3,100 cases by September, in populations where vaccination had fallen below that threshold. A review that may strip the United States of the elimination status it gained in 2000 is due in November 2026.

The fall in coverage has a traceable cause. In 1998 a paper in *The Lancet* claimed a link between the MMR vaccine and autism; it was based on twelve children, its author held an undisclosed financial interest, the data were later found to have been altered, the journal retracted it in 2010, and the author was struck off the UK medical register. Studies since have looked for the association in millions of children and not found it, including a Danish cohort of 657,461 that found no increased risk even in the subgroups most often claimed to be at risk.[^12] The honest case for vaccination has to include the real risks, which are small and specific rather than absent: myocarditis after mRNA vaccines, concentrated in young men and usually mild; febrile seizures at slightly higher rates with the combined MMRV shot than with MMR and varicella given separately; and anaphylaxis at roughly one case per million doses. These are the numbers that belong next to the measles figures above, and they are why the comparison favors vaccination by orders of magnitude.

## When it goes wrong

**Allergy** is an immune response to something harmless: pollen, peanut, cat dander. The mechanism is antibodies of the IgE class, made to fight parasites, binding the allergen and triggering mast cells to dump histamine, producing itching, swelling, mucus, and, in the worst case, anaphylaxis. Allergies have risen sharply in wealthy countries over decades, and the leading explanation is the **hygiene hypothesis** in its modern form: early exposure to a diverse microbial environment trains the immune system, and its absence leaves regulation poorly calibrated. Evidence includes lower allergy rates in children raised on farms and with older siblings; the mechanism is not settled. One thing that is settled is the reversal on peanut: early introduction of peanut to infants at risk cut peanut allergy by about 80 percent in a randomized trial, overturning a decade of advice to avoid it.[^8]

**Autoimmunity** is an immune attack on the body: type 1 diabetes (insulin-producing cells), multiple sclerosis (myelin), rheumatoid arthritis (joints), lupus (many tissues), celiac disease (gut lining, triggered by gluten). Around one person in ten is affected, and disproportionately women (13 percent against 7 percent in the largest population study), for reasons that are only partly understood and involve the X chromosome and sex hormones. Treatment has moved from broad immunosuppression to targeted biologics, and, most recently, to using CAR-T cells, engineered to kill the B cells driving the disease: a 2024 case series of 15 patients with severe lupus and related diseases reported drug-free remission lasting up to two years, which if it holds in randomized trials would be a step change.[^9]

**Transplant rejection** is the immune system doing its job on a foreign organ; it is managed with lifelong immunosuppression, which brings infection and cancer risk. Pig organs edited to remove the molecules humans reject are in early human trials; the longest-surviving recipient lived 271 days free of dialysis before being bridged to a human kidney transplant in 2026.

**Cancer and immunity.** Tumor cells are your cells with mutations, and the immune system does detect and destroy many of them; tumors that grow have evolved ways to hide, often by displaying molecules that switch T cells off. **Checkpoint inhibitors**, antibodies that block those off-switches, release the brakes and have produced durable remissions in melanoma and lung cancers that were previously fatal within months; James Allison and Tasuku Honjo won the 2018 Nobel Prize for the discovery. They work in a minority of patients, for reasons still being worked out, and can cause autoimmune side effects, which is the mechanism working as designed in the wrong place.[^10]

:::story The variolation gamble
Long before vaccines, people in China, India, and the Ottoman Empire practiced **variolation**: deliberately infecting a healthy person with material from a mild smallpox case, which produced a usually milder illness and lifelong immunity. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, whose face had been scarred by smallpox and whose brother had died of it, saw it in Constantinople and had her son inoculated there in 1718, then her daughter in London in 1721, and campaigned for it against considerable medical resistance. It killed about 1 to 2 percent of those treated, against 20 to 30 percent for natural smallpox: a bad risk by modern standards and an excellent one at the time. In 1796 Edward Jenner tested the folk observation that milkmaids who caught cowpox did not get smallpox, inoculated a boy with cowpox, and then challenged him with smallpox; the boy did not get sick.[^11] Vaccination is named for the cow (*vacca*). The sequence, an empirical folk practice, a risky improvement, and then a mechanism understood only two centuries later, is how much of medicine actually happened.
:::

## Summary

- Innate immunity is fast and general; adaptive immunity is specific, slow at first, and remembers, using randomly generated receptors on B and T cells.
- Receptor diversity comes from cutting and pasting gene segments, giving more than 10^13 possible antibodies; self-reactive cells are deleted in the thymus and suppressed by regulatory T cells, the subject of the 2025 Nobel Prize.
- Clonal selection expands the matching cells over about a week and leaves memory cells that can last a lifetime.
- Vaccines create memory without disease; they have averted an estimated 154 million deaths in 50 years, and falling coverage returned measles to the United States in 2025 and 2026.
- Allergy is a misdirected response, best prevented in the peanut case by early introduction; autoimmunity affects 4 to 8 percent of people; checkpoint inhibitors release the immune brakes on cancer and won the 2018 Nobel Prize.
