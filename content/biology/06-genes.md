## How does a DNA sequence become a working molecule?

Chapter 2 told how DNA was found to be the genetic material and its structure solved in 1953. This chapter explains what that structure does: how information is stored, copied, read, and occasionally miscopied. It is the most mechanistic chapter in the guide, and the one everything in Parts III and VI depends on.

## The molecule

**DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a chain of **nucleotides**, each made of a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate, and one of four **bases**: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C). The sugar and phosphate form the backbone; the bases stick out from it like teeth on a zipper. Two chains run side by side in opposite directions and twist into a **double helix**, held together by the bases pairing across the middle: A always with T, G always with C, by hydrogen bonds. The pairing is the whole point. Because each base can pair with only one other, either strand specifies the other completely, so the molecule carries its own copying instructions.[^1]

The information is the sequence of bases along one strand, read like letters. One copy of the human nuclear genome holds about 3.1 billion base pairs in 23 **chromosomes**. A typical body cell has two copies, about 6.2 billion base pairs in 23 pairs of chromosomes. Before DNA replication, each chromosome is a single enormous DNA molecule (the largest, chromosome 1, is about 250 million base pairs and 8 centimeters long) wound around protein spools called **histones** and coiled again and again to fit in the nucleus.[^2] Printed as letters at normal size, your genome would fill about a thousand thousand-page books.

## Copying

Before a cell divides it copies all of it, and the copying (**replication**) works because of the pairing. An enzyme unzips the helix; each separated strand serves as a template; **DNA polymerase** moves along it adding the complementary base at each position (A opposite T, G opposite C), producing two identical double helices, each with one old strand and one new. The polymerase adds about 50 bases per second in humans and 1,000 in bacteria, and, because thousands of polymerases work at once at different points, a human genome is copied in about eight hours.

The polymerase picks the wrong base about once in a hundred thousand; its own proofreading catches almost all of those, and a separate mismatch-repair system catches most of what is left, so the final rate is roughly one error per billion bases. Across a genome of three billion that is a few new **mutations** per cell division, and about 60 to 70 new mutations in each child that neither parent carried.[^3] That error rate is not an accident of sloppiness. It is the raw material of evolution (chapter 8), and organisms with much lower rates would not have changed enough to be here.

## From gene to protein

A **gene**, in the classical sense, is a stretch of DNA that specifies a protein. Getting from one to the other takes two steps, and the flow of information, DNA to RNA to protein, is what Crick called the **central dogma**.

**Transcription.** An enzyme, RNA polymerase, unzips the DNA at the gene, reads one strand, and builds a matching single strand of **RNA** (ribonucleic acid, like DNA but with a slightly different sugar and uracil, U, in place of thymine). This **messenger RNA** (mRNA) is a working copy of the gene, which leaves the nucleus while the DNA stays safe. In eukaryotes the copy is edited first: genes are interrupted by stretches called **introns** that are cut out, and the remaining pieces (**exons**) spliced together, often in more than one way, so one gene can yield several proteins.

**Translation.** A **ribosome** grabs the mRNA and reads it three letters at a time. Each three-letter word, a **codon**, specifies one amino acid (or a stop). Small adaptor molecules, **transfer RNAs**, each carrying a specific amino acid and bearing the matching three-letter anticodon, line up on the message, and the ribosome links their amino acids into a chain, at about 20 amino acids per second in a bacterium and about 5 in a human cell. The chain folds (chapter 4) and the protein is made.[^4]

## The code

The **genetic code** is the table that maps the 64 possible three-letter codons to the 20 amino acids and the stop signal. It was cracked between 1961 and 1966 and it is, with a handful of minor variations, the same in every living thing: a bacterium, a mushroom, a whale, and you all read GCA as alanine and UAA as stop. That universality is the strongest single piece of evidence that all life shares an ancestor, and it is what makes genetic engineering possible: a human gene put into a bacterium is read correctly, and the bacterium makes human insulin, which is where most insulin now comes from.[^5]

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Letters | 4 bases (A, C, G, T in DNA; U replaces T in RNA) |
| Word length | 3 bases (a codon) |
| Possible words | 64 |
| Meanings | 20 amino acids plus stop; most amino acids have several codons |
| Start signal | AUG, which also codes methionine, so every chain starts with one, usually removed later |
| Stop signals | UAA, UAG, UGA |
| Reading | From a fixed start, three at a time, no punctuation, no overlaps |

Because most amino acids have several codons, many single-letter mutations change nothing (**silent** mutations). Others swap one amino acid for another (**missense**), which may or may not matter, as in the alcohol enzyme of chapter 4 or the sickle-cell mutation, a single A to T in the hemoglobin gene that changes one glutamate to valine and makes red cells collapse into sickles. Others create a stop signal early (**nonsense**) and truncate the protein. And the insertion or deletion of a single base shifts the reading frame so that every codon after it is misread, which is almost always disastrous.[^6]

## Switches

Every cell in your body has the same genome, and a liver cell and a neuron are different because they use different parts of it. **Gene regulation**, deciding which genes are transcribed, how much, and when, is most of what a genome does, and most of the genome is devoted to it.

The basic switch was found in bacteria by Jacob and Monod in 1961: a **repressor** protein sits on the DNA just before a gene and blocks transcription until a signal (lactose, in their case) pulls it off. Eukaryotes elaborate this enormously. **Transcription factors**, some 1,600 of them in humans, bind to specific short sequences near or far from genes and recruit or block the polymerase; combinations of them give each cell type its program. **Enhancers**, regulatory sequences that can sit hundreds of thousands of bases from the gene they control, loop the DNA around to reach it. And **epigenetic** marks, chemical tags on the DNA (methylation) or its histone spools, make regions of the genome open or closed for business and are copied when the cell divides, so that a liver cell's daughters are liver cells. Some epigenetic marks respond to the environment, which is the small kernel of truth in Lamarck, though whether and how much they pass between generations in humans is disputed.[^7]

## The other 98 percent

Only about 1.5 percent of the human genome codes for protein: some 20,000 genes, fewer than a rice plant's and about the same as a roundworm's. What is the rest? About 8 percent shows signs of being preserved by selection, and most of that non-coding fraction is regulatory sequence of the kind just described. Some is transcribed into RNAs that are never translated but do jobs of their own: the RNA parts of the ribosome, the transfer RNAs, and thousands of **non-coding RNAs**, including the **microRNAs** that fine-tune gene expression and whose discovery won the 2024 Nobel Prize. Nearly half is the remains of **transposable elements**, stretches of DNA that copy themselves around the genome, parasites that have mostly been silenced but occasionally still jump. Some is the fossil of ancient viruses, some is junk in any sense, and how much of the rest does anything is one of biology's live arguments, with estimates of the functional fraction running from about 10 percent to most of it depending on what "functional" means.[^8]

:::history How we got here
Two ideas about genes had to be given up. The first was that a gene was a bead on a string, one gene one protein, with a fixed address. Genes turned out to be interrupted by introns, spliced in alternative ways, overlapping, nested inside other genes, and sometimes read from both strands; the count of human genes fell from the 100,000 predicted in 1990 to about 20,000, and the definition of a gene has been under revision since. The second was "junk DNA." When the human genome came back 98 percent non-coding, the phrase, coined in 1972, was taken to mean that most of the genome did nothing. The ENCODE project's 2012 claim that 80 percent of it was "functional" (meaning biochemically active in some assay) swung too far the other way and was sharply criticized; the current view is that a substantial minority of non-coding DNA is regulatory or otherwise useful, that much of the rest is transposon debris drifting along because deleting it costs nothing, and that the question is not settled because there is no agreed test for function. The genome is not a blueprint. It is more like a very old codebase: working parts, control logic, commented-out sections, dead code, and a great deal of copied boilerplate, with no documentation.
:::

:::key
DNA stores information as a sequence of four letters, copies itself because each letter pairs with one partner, and is read in three-letter words by a code that is the same in all life. A gene is transcribed into messenger RNA and translated by the ribosome into a protein. Copying errors, a few per division, are the source of all genetic variation. Most of the genome does not code for protein; a large part of it decides which genes are used where, and that regulation, not the gene count, is what makes a human different from a worm.
:::

## Summary

- DNA is a double helix of four bases in which A pairs with T and G with C, so either strand specifies the other; a human genome is 3.1 billion pairs, present in two copies on 46 chromosomes.
- Replication copies both strands with about one uncorrected error per billion bases, giving each child some 60 to 70 new mutations: the raw material of evolution.
- Genes are transcribed into mRNA and translated by ribosomes three bases at a time; the 64-codon code is universal, which is why bacteria can make human insulin.
- Mutations are silent, missense, nonsense, or frameshift; sickle-cell disease is one letter.
- Regulation by transcription factors, enhancers, and epigenetic marks makes cell types from one genome; only 1.5 percent of the genome codes for protein, and how much of the rest matters is argued.
