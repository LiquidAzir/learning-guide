## How can selection produce adaptation without a plan?

Chapter 7 gave the mechanism of inheritance: genes passed on with reshuffling and occasional mistakes. Evolution is what happens to that inheritance across a population over generations. This chapter states the mechanism carefully, lays out the evidence, and answers the standard objections, most of which are good questions with good answers.

## The mechanism, stated exactly

Natural selection follows from four facts, none of which is controversial.

1. Individuals in a population **vary**, and some of the variation is **heritable** (chapters 6 and 7).
2. More offspring are produced than can survive and reproduce, because resources are finite.
3. Some heritable variants make their carriers better at surviving and reproducing **in that environment**.
4. Therefore those variants become more common in the next generation.

That is all. There is no force, no striving, no goal. **Fitness**, in this technical sense, is not strength or health but the number of surviving offspring relative to others, and it is always relative to a particular environment: a variant that is fit in a drought is unfit in a flood. Herbert Spencer's phrase "survival of the fittest," which Darwin adopted reluctantly, is misleading twice over: it suggests a fixed quality of fitness, and it suggests survival is the point when reproduction is.[^1]

Selection is not the only way populations change. **Genetic drift** is change by chance: in a small population, an allele can spread or vanish simply through who happens to breed, regardless of its effects. **Gene flow** is the movement of alleles between populations by migration. **Mutation** supplies new variants at a rate of a few dozen per person per generation (chapter 6). And most mutations are **neutral**, neither helping nor harming, so much of the genetic difference between species is the accumulated drift of changes that selection never saw, an insight (the neutral theory, Motoo Kimura, 1968) that is essential to reading molecular data and was fiercely resisted.[^2]

## How new species form

A **species**, in the most used definition, is a group of populations that interbreed in nature and do not interbreed with others. New species form when populations stop exchanging genes long enough to diverge, usually because they are physically separated: a river changes course, an island is colonized, a mountain range rises. Given time, their genomes drift and adapt apart until they can no longer interbreed even if reunited, at which point they are two species.[^3]

We can watch it happening. The seventeen or so species of Darwin's finches in the Galápagos, the count depending on the taxonomist, all descended from one South American ancestor, differ mainly in beak shape, and Peter and Rosemary Grant, measuring every finch on one small island for forty years, documented beak size shifting measurably within a few generations as droughts changed which seeds were available, and even the origin of a new breeding lineage from a hybridization event.[^4] Bacteria in continuous culture, apple maggot flies switching to a new host plant, and mosquitoes adapting to the London Underground are all cases of speciation caught in the act; the classic "ring species" of the textbooks have turned out to be less tidy than they looked.

## The evidence

Evolution is one of the best-supported claims in science, and the support comes from independent directions that all agree.

**The fossil record** shows a consistent order: no mammals before the reptiles, no reptiles before the fish, no animals at all in the first three billion years. Transitional forms, once said to be missing, are now abundant: *Tiktaalik* (a fish with a neck, wrist bones, and lungs), *Archaeopteryx* and the feathered dinosaurs, the whale series from four-legged land animals through *Ambulocetus* to modern whales with vestigial hip bones, and the hominin series (chapter 9). The fossil record is patchy, because fossilization is rare, and every gap that has been filled has been filled in the predicted direction.[^5]

**Anatomy.** The same bones, rearranged, appear in the flipper of a whale, the wing of a bat, and your hand: **homology**, the signature of common descent rather than of design for purpose. And the bad design is more telling than the good: the wisdom teeth that no longer fit the jaw, the vestigial hip bones of whales and pythons, the muscles that let some people wiggle their ears, the tailbone, the nerve that runs from the brain down the neck, around the aorta, and back up to the larynx, a detour of a few centimeters in you and about four meters in a giraffe, because it evolved in a fish where the path was direct.[^6]

**Molecules.** Every organism uses the same genetic code, and the differences between species' genomes fall into exactly the nested pattern that common descent predicts: humans and chimpanzees differ at about 1.2 percent of the bases they share, and by about 4 percent once insertions and deletions are counted, humans and mice about 15 percent of the alignable portion, humans and yeast far more, and the tree drawn from DNA matches the tree drawn from anatomy and the tree drawn from fossils. We also carry the wreckage: a broken gene for making vitamin C (which is why we need it in food and most mammals do not), broken genes for smell receptors, and about 8 percent of our genome consisting of the remains of ancient viruses.[^7]

**Direct observation.** Antibiotic resistance, pesticide resistance, herbicide-resistant weeds, the peppered moth's color shift with industrial soot and back, the rapid evolution of the COVID-19 virus through variants, lactose tolerance spreading through dairying populations in the last 8,000 years. Evolution is not only a historical inference; it is a daily engineering problem in medicine and agriculture.

:::key
The common objections to evolution are good questions with clear answers. *"It's only a theory."* In science a theory is a well-tested explanatory framework, not a guess (see chapter 1 of the physics guide). *"How can chance produce complexity?"* Mutation is chance; selection is not, and the two together are a search algorithm that accumulates improvements. *"What use is half an eye?"* Half the visual acuity, which beats none: eyes exist in every intermediate form among living animals, from light-sensitive patches through pinholes to lensed cameras, and each stage is useful. *"The second law forbids increasing order."* Not in a system fed energy by the Sun (chapter 5). *"There are gaps in the fossil record."* Yes, and each one filled has been filled where predicted. *"It cannot explain morality or consciousness."* It explains how brains capable of them arose; whether it fully explains them is a live question and does not bear on whether species share ancestors.
:::

## What evolution is not

**Not progress.** There is no ladder from simple to complex with humans at the top. Most of life's history and most of its present biomass is microbial; parasites evolve toward simplicity; the most successful body plan by number of species is the beetle's. Evolution has no direction except better fit to current conditions.

**Not optimization.** Selection works on what is available, not on what would be best. The vertebrate eye has its wiring in front of its photoreceptors, producing a blind spot, because that is how it happened to start; the octopus eye, evolved separately, does not. Bodies are full of compromises, historical accidents, and jury-rigging.

**Not only competition.** Cooperation evolves readily whenever it pays: cells in a body, ants in a colony, bacteria in a biofilm, lichens, mitochondria in your cells (chapter 3). **Kin selection**, worked out by William Hamilton in 1964, explains why an individual might sacrifice for relatives: a gene that causes altruism toward kin can spread because relatives carry copies of it, and the arithmetic (help if the benefit to the relative, times your relatedness, exceeds your cost) predicts observed behavior from sterile worker bees to birds that help raise siblings.[^8] Reciprocity, mutualism, and symbiosis account for much of what life does. E. O. Wilson, whose life's work was ants, pushed this reasoning to its limit in *Sociobiology* (1975), arguing that social behavior in every species including ours is shaped by selection; the biology was largely accepted and the extension to humans provoked a bitter argument that ran for decades.

**Not "the survival of the species."** Selection acts on individuals and, through them, on genes; a trait that helps the species but harms its bearer will not spread. Whether selection can act on groups is an old argument, mostly resolved in favor of gene- and individual-level explanations with a minority defending group and multilevel selection in specific cases.[^9]

## The modern additions

The 1940s synthesis (chapter 2) has been extended, not overturned. **Neutral theory**: most molecular change is not adaptive. **Punctuated equilibrium** (Eldredge and Gould, 1972): the fossil record shows long stasis broken by rapid change, which is compatible with the standard mechanism at population time scales and was, in its strong form, overstated. **Developmental genetics** ("evo-devo"): large changes in form can come from small changes in the regulation of a few conserved developmental genes, which explains how new body shapes appear quickly. **Horizontal gene transfer**: bacteria swap genes across species freely, so their history is a network more than a tree, and this is how antibiotic resistance spreads. **Endosymbiosis**: whole organisms merging (chapter 3). And **epigenetic and cultural inheritance**: non-genetic transmission across generations, real but of contested importance for long-term evolution.[^10]

:::history How we got here
Darwin's theory had a serious problem for fifty years and it was not religion; it was inheritance. If offspring were a blend of their parents, as everyone assumed, any new favorable variant would be diluted by half in each generation and vanish before selection could act. Critics pointed this out immediately, and Darwin, unable to answer, gave increasing weight in later editions to Lamarckian mechanisms he had originally rejected. Mendel's particulate inheritance, published in 1866 and unread, was the answer: alleles are not diluted, they are shuffled and reappear intact. When Mendel was rediscovered in 1900 the two camps first fought (the "biometricians" who studied continuous variation against the "Mendelians" who studied discrete traits) and then, in the 1920s, were shown by Fisher, Haldane, and Wright to be describing the same thing at different scales. The eclipse of Darwinism between 1880 and 1920, when most biologists accepted evolution but doubted natural selection, is the standard case of a correct theory stalling for want of a missing piece rather than for want of evidence.[^11]
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
Bacteria become resistant after an antibiotic is used. Why is “they mutated because they needed resistance” a misleading explanation?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Variation can arise before or during exposure without being directed toward the needed solution. The antibiotic changes which variants survive and reproduce; resistance genes can also spread between bacteria. Selection changes the frequencies of variants. It does not require organisms to foresee the useful mutation.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Natural selection follows from heritable variation, limited resources, and differential reproduction; fitness means relative reproductive success in a particular environment, not strength.
- Drift, gene flow, mutation, and neutral change also alter populations; most molecular evolution is neutral.
- Species form when populations stop exchanging genes and diverge; the process has been observed in finches, flies, bacteria, and mosquitoes.
- The evidence comes from fossils in consistent order with transitional forms, homologous and vestigial anatomy, the nested pattern and shared code of genomes, and direct observation of resistance and adaptation.
- Evolution is not progress, not optimization, and not only competition; kin selection explains altruism, and the synthesis has been extended by neutral theory, evo-devo, horizontal transfer, and endosymbiosis.
