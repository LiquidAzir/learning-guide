## How do you tell whether an apparent health improvement was caused by a treatment?

The preceding chapters gave the machinery. This one applies it to the thing most people want biology for: their own health. It covers what disease is, the largest causes of death, cancer in detail because it is the clearest application of everything in Part II, how medical evidence is produced, and how to read it.

## What kills people

Global deaths, roughly 62 million a year, break down as follows.[^1]

| Cause | Share of global deaths |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular disease (heart attack, stroke) | ~28% |
| Cancers | ~17% |
| Chronic respiratory disease | ~7% |
| Infectious and parasitic disease, in a non-pandemic year | ~12% |
| Dementias | ~3% |
| Diabetes and kidney disease | ~5% |
| Injuries, including road traffic and suicide | ~8% |
| Everything else | remainder |

The pattern has inverted within a century. In 1900 the leading causes in industrial countries were pneumonia, tuberculosis, and diarrheal disease; today they are chronic conditions of long life. This **epidemiological transition** was produced mainly by clean water, sanitation, nutrition, and vaccination rather than by clinical medicine, and it is why global life expectancy rose from about 32 in 1900 to 73 today.[^2] The remaining large infectious burden, tuberculosis, malaria, HIV, diarrheal disease, pneumonia, falls overwhelmingly on poor countries and poor people, and is mostly preventable with tools that exist.

The biggest modifiable risk factors, in no strict order: high blood pressure, air pollution, tobacco, dietary risks, high blood sugar, obesity, and alcohol. Tobacco alone kills about 8 million people a year, and the single most effective health decision available to a smoker is to stop.

## Cancer

**Cancer** is what happens when the controls on cell division fail. A cell acquires mutations that make it divide without the usual signals, ignore signals to stop, evade the self-destruct program, become immortal (by switching the telomere-maintaining enzyme back on), recruit blood vessels, invade neighboring tissue, and finally spread to other organs. Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg codified these as the **hallmarks of cancer** in 2000, extended in 2011 and 2022.[^3]

It takes several mutations, usually five to ten in the right genes, which is why cancer is mostly a disease of age: mutations accumulate. The genes involved come in two kinds. **Oncogenes** are normal growth genes stuck on (RAS, MYC, HER2). **Tumor suppressors** are brakes that have been broken (TP53, broken in about half of all cancers; RB; BRCA1 and BRCA2, which repair DNA). Inherited mutations in the second kind explain familial cancers: a BRCA1 carrier starts life one step down the path in every cell.

Causes, by attributable fraction: tobacco (about 20 percent of cancer deaths worldwide), infections (about 13 percent of new cancer cases, including HPV for cervical cancer, hepatitis B and C for liver, *H. pylori* for stomach), obesity, alcohol, radiation including ultraviolet, and specific chemicals. A large share of the remainder is unavoidable: mutations that arise from the ordinary errors of cell division, which is why some cancers strike people who did everything right, a finding sometimes misreported as "cancer is mostly bad luck," and itself disputed by researchers who put more weight on environmental causes.[^4]

Treatment has four modes and a fifth arriving. **Surgery** removes it, and remains the most curative when the cancer is local. **Radiation** damages DNA in a targeted volume. **Chemotherapy** poisons dividing cells, which is why it also attacks hair, gut lining, and bone marrow. **Targeted therapy** blocks a specific mutated protein: imatinib for chronic myeloid leukemia, in 2001, turned a fatal disease into a manageable one and is the field's proof of concept. **Immunotherapy** (chapter 14) releases the immune system's brakes and has produced durable remissions in cancers that were rapidly fatal. Overall, age-adjusted cancer mortality in the United States has fallen by roughly a third since its 1991 peak, with about half of that from reduced smoking and much of the rest from screening and treatment; other high-income countries show similar declines from differently timed peaks.[^5]

## How a drug gets approved

The path from molecule to medicine, and why it takes a decade and fails 90 percent of the time.

1. **Discovery and preclinical.** Find a molecule that hits a target; test in cells and in at least two animal species for effect and toxicity. Years 0 to 6.
2. **Phase 1.** Tens of healthy volunteers (or patients, for cancer drugs). Question: is it safe, and what does the body do to it? Not designed to show benefit.
3. **Phase 2.** Hundreds of patients. Question: does it appear to work, and at what dose?
4. **Phase 3.** Hundreds to thousands of patients, randomized against placebo or the current best treatment, ideally double-blind. Question: does it work, and what are the harms? This is the evidence regulators require.
5. **Regulatory review**, then **Phase 4**: post-marketing surveillance, which finds the rare harms that trials of a few thousand people cannot.

About 90 percent of drugs entering human trials fail, most often for lack of efficacy in Phase 2 or 3, and the average cost per approved drug, counting the failures, is estimated at roughly one to three billion dollars depending on the method.[^6] The reasons matter for reading news: a promising Phase 1 result means almost nothing about whether the drug works, and a mouse result means less.

:::howto Reading a medical study
1. **What kind of study is it?** The hierarchy, weakest to strongest: anecdote, case series, observational study (cohort, case-control), randomized controlled trial, systematic review of trials. Only randomization reliably separates cause from correlation, because it balances everything you did not measure.
2. **How many people, and for how long?** Small and short studies find fluke effects. A trial of 40 people for 6 weeks does not establish that something prevents heart attacks.
3. **Compared to what?** Placebo, nothing, or the current standard? Improvement against no treatment can be regression to the mean, natural recovery, or placebo effect.
4. **What was actually measured?** A **surrogate endpoint** (cholesterol, tumor shrinkage, bone density) is not the outcome you care about (heart attacks, survival, fractures). Drugs that improved surrogates and worsened survival exist.
5. **Absolute or relative?** "Cuts risk by 50 percent" could be 2 percent to 1 percent. Ask for the absolute difference and the **number needed to treat**: how many people must take the drug for one to benefit. For statins in primary prevention it is roughly 100 over 5 years; for antibiotics in bacterial pneumonia it is a handful.
6. **Preregistered?** A trial that declared its main outcome before starting cannot switch to whichever result came out best. Unregistered or switched outcomes are a major source of false findings.
7. **Who paid, and has it been replicated?** Industry funding is not disqualifying and is associated with more favorable results. One study is a hypothesis; a systematic review of several is evidence.[^7]
:::

## Why so much published research is wrong

In 2005 John Ioannidis argued, with a formal model, that most published research findings are false, because studies are small, effects are sought in many places, analytic flexibility is large, and only positive results get published.[^8] The prediction has been borne out: large replication efforts in psychology reproduced under half of published findings; in preclinical cancer biology, industry laboratories reported reproducing well under half. The causes are structural, not fraud: publication bias, small samples, flexible analysis, and career incentives that reward novelty over verification.

The fixes are being adopted: preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans, registered reports where journals accept a study before results are known, larger collaborations, data sharing, and requirements to publish negative results. Medicine has moved fastest, because trial registration was mandated; the effect was visible almost at once, with the share of large cardiovascular trials reporting a benefit falling sharply after registration became mandatory in 2000.[^9]

For a reader, the practical rule is that a single new study, however striking, is a weak signal, and the strongest available evidence is usually a recent systematic review from a body like Cochrane, which pools trials and grades their quality.

:::story The scurvy trial
In 1747 James Lind, a naval surgeon aboard HMS *Salisbury*, took twelve sailors sick with scurvy, put them all on the same diet, and divided them into six pairs, each given a different treatment: cider, sulfuric acid elixir, vinegar, seawater, a paste of garlic and mustard, or two oranges and a lemon. Within six days the pair on citrus were nearly fit for duty; nobody else improved.[^10] It is often called the first controlled clinical trial, and it has the shape of one: same starting condition, same diet, simultaneous comparison groups. It also shows how slowly evidence moves: the Royal Navy did not require citrus for another 48 years, Lind himself hedged his conclusions among other theories, and the causal agent, vitamin C, was not isolated until 1932. Scurvy killed more sailors in the age of sail than all combat combined, and the cure was demonstrated on twelve men in a ship's cabin in 1747.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
A treated group improves over a month. Why is its own starting condition not enough of a comparison to show that the treatment worked?

:::answer Show the reasoning
People can improve naturally, measurements fluctuate, and expectations or other changes may matter. A suitable comparison group helps estimate what would have happened without the treatment. Randomization reduces systematic differences between groups, while uncertainty and the size of the effect still need reporting.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Cardiovascular disease and cancer cause nearly half of global deaths; the shift from infectious to chronic causes came mainly from sanitation, nutrition, and vaccination.
- The largest modifiable risks are blood pressure, tobacco, diet, air pollution, blood sugar, obesity, and alcohol.
- Cancer is the failure of the controls on cell division, requiring several mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressors; treatment is surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted drugs, and immunotherapy, and mortality has fallen about a third since 1991 in rich countries.
- Drug development runs from preclinical work through three trial phases; about 90 percent of drugs entering human trials fail.
- Read studies for design, size, comparator, endpoint, absolute effect, preregistration, and replication; a single study is a hypothesis, and much published research does not replicate.
