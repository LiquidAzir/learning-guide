## Recap

Chapter 9 showed that most of life's diversity is microbial. This chapter is about those organisms in their own right: what they do, how they cause disease, how we fight them, and how they live inside us. It is also the chapter where biology meets medicine most directly.

## What bacteria do

There are about 10^30 bacterial and archaeal cells on Earth, with a combined mass roughly 35 times that of all animals.[^1] They run the planet's chemistry. **Nitrogen fixation**: the air is 78 percent nitrogen gas, which no plant or animal can use; only certain bacteria can break its triple bond and convert it to ammonia, and every protein in your body contains nitrogen that passed through a bacterium or through the industrial process (chapter 11) that copies them. **Decomposition**: bacteria and fungi return the carbon and nutrients in dead things to circulation; without them the planet would be buried in corpses and the carbon cycle would stop. **Photosynthesis**: marine cyanobacteria, notably *Prochlorococcus*, the most abundant photosynthetic organism on Earth at about 3 × 10^27 cells, produce a large share of the world's oxygen.[^2] They also make and eat methane, oxidize iron and sulfur, live in boiling vents and Antarctic ice, and survive kilometers underground on a metabolism so slow that some cells may divide once a century.

## What viruses are

A virus is genetic material, DNA or RNA, wrapped in protein, with no metabolism, no ribosomes, and no way to reproduce except by entering a cell and hijacking its machinery. Sizes run from about 20 to 300 nanometers, small enough that they were defined before they could be seen: in 1892 Dmitri Ivanovsky found an infectious agent in tobacco that passed through filters fine enough to stop bacteria.

The life cycle: attach to a specific receptor on a cell surface (which determines what it can infect), enter, release its genome, force the cell to make viral proteins and copies of the genome, assemble, and leave, often killing the cell. **Retroviruses** like HIV do something extra: they copy their RNA genome into DNA and insert it into the host's chromosomes, where it stays for the life of the cell, which is why HIV cannot be cleared by current drugs and must be suppressed indefinitely.[^3]

Viruses are the most numerous biological entities on Earth, about 10^31 particles. In the ocean they kill roughly 20 percent of microbial biomass every day, releasing nutrients and driving the carbon cycle. They move genes between hosts, and about 8 percent of the human genome is the remains of ancient retroviral insertions, some of which have been put to use: the protein that lets the placenta fuse to the uterus comes from a virus.[^4]

## How infections work, and how they are stopped

A pathogen must get in (through the airway, gut, skin break, or a bite), attach, multiply faster than the immune system removes it, and get out to a new host. What makes a disease dangerous is a combination of how easily it transmits, how much damage it does, and how long a person is infectious before symptoms appear, which is why COVID-19 spread as it did and Ebola, which incapacitates quickly, has not.

The countermeasures, in order of impact on human life: **sanitation** (clean water and sewers, which cut infectious mortality more than any medicine), **vaccination**, **antibiotics**, and **antivirals**. Smallpox, which killed some 300 million people in the twentieth century alone, was declared eradicated in 1980 through vaccination, the only human disease so far eliminated. Polio is close.[^5]

**Antibiotics** kill bacteria by attacking structures bacteria have and we do not: cell walls (penicillins), bacterial ribosomes (tetracyclines, macrolides), bacterial DNA-copying enzymes (quinolones). They do nothing at all to viruses, which is why an antibiotic for a cold is useless and harmful. Penicillin, found by Alexander Fleming in 1928 on a contaminated plate and made into a drug by Florey and Chain in 1940, changed medicine: before it, a scratch could kill and pneumonia killed a third of those who caught it.

## Resistance

Every antibiotic use selects for the bacteria that survive it, so resistance is not a risk but a certainty; the question is how fast. Bacteria acquire it by mutation and, faster, by **horizontal gene transfer**, swapping resistance genes on small DNA circles (plasmids) across species, so that a resistance gene arising in a harmless gut bacterium can end up in a pathogen.

The scale is now well measured. In 2021 drug-resistant bacterial infections directly caused an estimated 1.14 million deaths worldwide and were associated with 4.71 million; on current trends the projection is 1.91 million direct deaths a year by 2050.[^6] The drivers are overuse in medicine (a large fraction of prescriptions are for viral illnesses) and, in most countries, larger use in agriculture, where antibiotics are given to healthy livestock to promote growth. The pipeline of new drugs nearly dried up because antibiotics are poor business: a drug used briefly and then held in reserve earns little. In 2024 a genuinely new class, zosurabalpin, was reported against a resistant hospital pathogen, which would be the first new class against Gram-negative bacteria in about fifty years if it clears trials; it entered phase 3 in 2025. It was found by screening 45,000 compounds rather than by artificial intelligence, though AI-guided searches are producing candidates too.[^7] Phage therapy, using viruses that kill bacteria, is being revived; a 100-patient series in 2024 reported improvement in 77 percent, best when combined with antibiotics, but it was uncontrolled.

:::howto When antibiotics help, and when they do not
1. **Bacterial or viral?** Antibiotics work only on bacteria. Colds, flu, COVID-19, most sore throats, most coughs, most sinus congestion, and most ear infections in older children are viral or self-resolving; in children under two, with severe pain, or with a bulging eardrum, treatment is usually recommended, and that is a clinician's decision rather than a rule of thumb. Strep throat, bacterial pneumonia, urinary tract infections, cellulitis, and sepsis need antibiotics promptly.
2. **Green mucus is not evidence.** The color comes from your own immune cells, not from bacteria.
3. **A course exists to treat you, not to prevent resistance.** The old advice to always finish the course is being revised: for several infections, shorter courses work as well and select less for resistance. Follow the specific advice you were given rather than a general rule.[^8]
4. **Do not keep or share leftovers**, and do not use an antibiotic prescribed for something else; the wrong drug at the wrong dose is the ideal resistance trainer.
5. **The real levers are elsewhere**: vaccination (which prevents infections that would need antibiotics), hand washing, and, at the policy level, ending routine agricultural use.
:::

## Your microbiome

You carry about 38 trillion bacterial cells, most in the large intestine, weighing about 0.2 kilograms in total, far less than the one-to-two-kilogram figure often quoted; they include several hundred species per person and collectively carry far more genes than you do.[^9] They digest fiber you cannot, producing short-chain fatty acids that feed your gut lining; they make some vitamins; they occupy space that pathogens would otherwise take; and they train the immune system, which is why animals raised germ-free have poorly developed immunity.

Then there is the rest, which requires care. The microbiome has been reported to correlate with obesity, depression, autism, Parkinson's, allergies, cancer response, and much else, and a large industry sells probiotics and testing on the strength of it. What is solidly established: fecal transplant cures recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection, dramatically, in randomized trials, and is now standard care. What is plausible but unproven: most of the rest. The difficulties are that the microbiome shifts with diet, illness, and drugs, so correlation with a disease may be a consequence rather than a cause; that mouse results transfer poorly; and that most commercial probiotics do not colonize the gut at all. A field that produced spectacular mouse experiments has produced few confirmed human interventions, and the honest summary is that the microbiome matters and the specific claims are mostly not yet demonstrated.[^10]

:::story The stomach ulcer bet
Until the 1980s, everyone knew that stomach ulcers were caused by stress and acid and were treated by antacids for life. Two Australians, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, found a spiral bacterium, *Helicobacter pylori*, in the stomachs of ulcer patients and proposed that it was the cause. This was dismissed: the stomach was known to be too acidic for bacteria. Unable to infect animals and unable to get a trial approved, Marshall in 1984 drank a broth of the bacteria himself, developed gastritis within days, showed the organism in his own biopsy, and cured himself with antibiotics.[^11] It still took a decade for practice to change. They shared the Nobel Prize in 2005, ulcers are now cured in two weeks with antibiotics, and stomach cancer, which *H. pylori* also causes, has fallen with it. The story is told as a triumph of the maverick, and the more useful lesson is about what "everyone knows" is worth when nobody has tested it.
:::

## Summary

- Bacteria and archaea run the planet's nitrogen, carbon, and sulfur cycles and produce much of its oxygen; their combined mass exceeds all animals' many times over.
- Viruses are genetic material in a coat with no metabolism; they are the most numerous entities on Earth, kill a fifth of ocean microbes daily, and left 8 percent of the human genome behind.
- Sanitation, vaccination, and antibiotics transformed human mortality; smallpox is the one human disease eradicated.
- Antibiotic resistance is inevitable and now causes over a million deaths a year directly, driven by medical overuse and agricultural use; the drug pipeline is thin but not empty.
- The microbiome is real and important, fecal transplant for *C. difficile* is proven, and most other clinical claims for it remain unconfirmed.
