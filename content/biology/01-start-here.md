## A drop of pond water

In 1674 a Dutch cloth merchant named Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, who ground glass lenses as a hobby, put a drop of lake water under one of them and saw, in his words, "very many little animalcules," some spinning, some darting, some a thousand times smaller than anything visible to the eye. He wrote to the Royal Society in London, which did not believe him; Robert Hooke was asked to repeat the observations and, on his third attempt, succeeded, while Leeuwenhoek sent testimonials from eight respectable witnesses in Delft. He was right. In a single drop of water there were more living things than there were people in Holland, and nobody had known they existed.[^1]

Three and a half centuries later we know what he was looking at: bacteria, algae, and single-celled predators, each one a bag of a few thousand kinds of molecule running a chemistry so intricate that we still cannot build one from scratch. We also know that every one of them, and every cell in your body, and every tree, mushroom, and whale, runs on the same code, uses the same twenty building blocks for its proteins, and descends from a single population of cells that lived about 4.2 billion years ago (chapter 9). That is the largest fact in biology, and it was not known until the 1960s. This guide is about how it was found out, what it means, and what has been done with it since.

## What life is

Biologists cannot agree on a definition of life, and it is worth knowing why. Every proposed definition either leaves something out or lets something in. "Reproduces" excludes mules and includes fire. "Made of cells" excludes viruses, which many biologists think are alive, and would exclude any life not built the way Earth's is. NASA's working definition, "a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution," is the most used and still argued.[^2]

What is not argued is the list of things living systems on Earth do. They are made of **cells**, bounded compartments with a controlled inside. They **metabolize**: take in energy and matter, transform them, and excrete waste. They maintain **homeostasis**, a stable internal state against a changing outside. They **respond** to their environment. They **grow and develop**. They **reproduce**, passing on **heredity** in the form of **DNA**. And, as populations over generations, they **evolve**. A rock does none of these. A crystal does one or two. A virus does several but only inside a host. Life is not a substance; it is a set of processes running together, and this guide takes them one at a time.

## The scale of it

Biology spans about sixteen orders of magnitude in size, from a molecule to the planet, and the reader should have the ladder in mind.

| Scale | Size | What lives here |
|---|---|---|
| Small molecule | 1 nanometer (a billionth of a meter) | Water, sugar, ATP |
| Protein | 5 to 10 nm | Enzymes, antibodies, the machines of the cell |
| Virus | 20 to 300 nm | The smallest things that reproduce |
| Bacterium | 1 to 5 micrometers (millionths of a meter) | Most life on Earth, by number |
| Human cell | 10 to 20 μm (egg cells and neurons far larger) | Some 30 to 37 trillion in you |
| Small animal | millimeters | Insects, the most diverse animals |
| Human | ~2 meters | You |
| Blue whale | 30 m | The largest animal ever |
| Giant sequoia | 80 m | The largest single tree by volume; clonal aspens and fungal networks are larger |
| Ecosystem | kilometers | Forests, reefs, the soil under a field |
| Biosphere | 12,700 km | The whole living planet, a film a few kilometers thick |

Most of this guide happens at the small end, because that is where the mechanisms are. But it ends at the large end, because that is where the consequences are.

## How biologists find things out

Biology is an experimental science with a historical twist: it studies things that were built by four billion years of trial and error rather than designed, so they are messy, redundant, and full of parts whose purpose is unknown. Its methods:

**Looking.** From Leeuwenhoek's lens to the electron microscope (which resolves individual molecules) to cryo-electron microscopy (which shows their shapes) to live-cell imaging with fluorescent proteins that glow where a gene is active. Much of biology is still natural history: finding out what exists and what it does.

**Breaking things.** The most powerful tool in biology is to remove a part and see what fails. Knock out a gene; if the animal cannot make red blood cells, the gene is involved in making red blood cells. Most of what we know about what genes do was learned this way, and it has the limits of the method: what you learn is what breaks, not what the part does when everything is working.

**Comparing.** Because all life is related, a mechanism found in yeast is usually present in humans, in modified form. Biology's **model organisms**, the bacterium *E. coli*, baker's yeast, the roundworm *C. elegans*, the fruit fly *Drosophila*, the zebrafish, the mouse, and the plant *Arabidopsis*, were chosen because they are easy to grow and breed, and a large share of Nobel Prizes in physiology or medicine were won with them.[^3] Animal research is regulated and contested, and the standard framework, the "three Rs" (replace animals where possible, reduce the numbers used, refine procedures to limit suffering), is written into law in most countries and enforced by institutional review committees. Several results in this guide, from the nerve impulse to the effects of caloric restriction, exist because animals were used, and that is worth stating rather than passing over.

**Counting.** Biology became quantitative in the twentieth century, and a great deal of it now is statistics: does this drug work better than placebo in 10,000 people? Is this gene variant more common in people with the disease? The randomized controlled trial, whose modern form dates from the streptomycin trial of 1948, is biology's version of the physics experiment, and chapter 17 explains how to read one.

**Sequencing.** Since 2001 the cost of reading a megabase of DNA has fallen about a million-fold, and a human genome has gone from roughly $95 million to a few hundred dollars, and biology has become, in large part, the analysis of sequences: genomes of every species, the DNA of every cell in a tissue, the microbes in a gram of soil. Chapter 18 covers the tools.

:::howto Reading a biology claim
Biology produces more headlines than any other science and more of them evaporate. Six questions:
1. **In what?** A result in mice, cells in a dish, or worms is a result in mice, cells, or worms. Most drugs that cure cancer in mice do not work in people; the ones that make it to human trials fail about 90 percent of the time.
2. **How many?** A study of 12 people can find a large effect by chance. Look for the number of subjects and whether the effect was found again by someone else.
3. **Compared to what?** An improvement without a control group is not evidence. A control group that knew it was the control is a weak one.
4. **Correlation or cause?** People who drink coffee live longer; people who drink coffee are also richer, healthier, and less depressed. Only a trial that assigns the coffee can tell.
5. **How big?** "Doubles your risk" of something that happens to one person in a million is one more person in a million. Look for the absolute numbers.
6. **Who paid, and who is quoted?** A study funded by the maker of the thing being tested is not automatically wrong, but the finding should be checked against independent work.

*Example.* "Red wine compound extends lifespan." Resveratrol extended the lifespan of yeast (2003), worms and flies (2004), and obese mice (2006), and the company that made it was bought for $720 million. In healthy mice, and in every human trial since, it has done nothing measurable for lifespan.[^4] Every step in that story was real, and the headline was still wrong.
:::

## What biologists argue about

Biology has fewer settled facts than physics and more live arguments, and the reader should know where they are. Some are old: how much of behavior is genetic, whether natural selection acts mostly on genes or also on groups, how life began. Some are new: whether the microbiome causes the diseases it is correlated with, how much of aging is programmed and how much is wear, whether the brain can be understood by mapping every connection. Each chapter marks these with a **Where it stands today** box and says which way the evidence leans. Where the evidence does not lean, it says that too.

The history matters more in biology than in most sciences, because so many of the wrong ideas were reasonable. Spontaneous generation (life from rot), vitalism (a life force beyond chemistry), the inheritance of acquired characteristics (giraffes stretching their necks), preformation (a tiny person in every sperm), and the fixity of species were all held by intelligent people on the evidence they had, and each chapter's **How we got here** box explains what the evidence was and what changed it.

:::key
Everything alive on Earth shares one chemistry and one ancestor. Every cell uses DNA to store instructions, RNA to carry them, proteins built from the same twenty amino acids to do the work, and ATP to pay for it. This unity is why an experiment in yeast tells you about people, why a gene from a jellyfish works in a mouse, and why a virus can hijack your cells: they are all speaking the same language. The diversity of life, from bacteria to blue whales, is variation on that one theme, produced by evolution over about four billion years. Hold onto the unity; the rest is detail.
:::
