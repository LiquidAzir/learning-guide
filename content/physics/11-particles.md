## What are particles made of, and how do they interact?

Quantum mechanics describes electrons in atoms. But what is the atom made of, what holds the nucleus together, and what is the electron itself? This chapter descends through the layers of matter to the current floor, the **Standard Model**, the most precisely tested theory in science and one everybody agrees is incomplete.

## Inside the atom

By 1900 atoms were accepted and known to contain electrons, discovered by J. J. Thomson in 1897 as tiny negatively charged particles far lighter than any atom.[^1] Where was the positive charge? Thomson pictured a "plum pudding": electrons embedded in a diffuse positive cloud.

## Radioactivity: the atom gives itself away

The tool that opened the atom was found by accident. In 1896 Henri Becquerel left uranium salts on a wrapped photographic plate in a drawer and found the plate fogged: the uranium was emitting something penetrating, all by itself, with no sunlight or any other input.[^19] Marie Curie, then a doctoral student in Paris, showed the emission came from the atoms themselves regardless of their chemical state, named it **radioactivity**, and with Pierre Curie isolated two new, intensely radioactive elements from tons of ore, polonium and radium.[^20] She won Nobel Prizes in both physics (1903) and chemistry (1911), the only person to do so.

Ernest Rutherford sorted the emissions by how far they penetrate. **Alpha particles** are heavy, positively charged, and stopped by paper; they turned out to be helium nuclei. **Beta particles** are fast electrons. **Gamma rays** are very high-energy light. He also found that every radioactive substance decays at its own fixed rate, losing half its atoms in a characteristic **half-life**, the exponential law from chapter 2, with no way to speed it up or slow it down.[^21] This was the first hint that atomic events are governed by probability rather than mechanism: each atom has a fixed chance per second of decaying, and nothing about its history changes that. Quantum mechanics later explained why. In 1928 George Gamow showed that alpha decay is tunneling (chapter 9): the alpha particle is trapped behind an energy wall it cannot climb and simply leaks through, and the leak rate reproduces the observed half-lives across a range of a trillion trillion.[^22]

Radioactivity also handed physicists their first probe. Alpha particles from radium were the bullets in the experiment that found the nucleus.

## Inside the nucleus

In 1909 Ernest Rutherford's students Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired alpha particles at a sheet of gold foil a couple of thousand atoms thick. Most passed straight through. About one in eight thousand bounced back. Rutherford later said it was "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."[^2] The only explanation: nearly all of an atom's mass and all of its positive charge are concentrated in a **nucleus** about $10^{-15}$ meters across, one hundred-thousandth the size of the atom. Atoms are almost entirely empty space. If the nucleus were a marble on the pitcher's mound, the electrons would be gnats in the outfield seats.

The nucleus itself has parts. Rutherford identified the **proton**, the hydrogen nucleus, as a component of all nuclei in 1919. James Chadwick found the **neutron**, a neutral particle of almost the same mass, in 1932.[^3] An element is defined by how many protons its nucleus has; **isotopes** are versions with different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons; carbon-14 has six and eight, and is radioactive.

## Two new forces

Protons repel each other electrically, ferociously at nuclear distances. Something far stronger must hold them together, but only at short range, since we do not feel it in everyday life. This is the **strong nuclear force**. It is roughly a hundred times stronger than electromagnetism but dies away completely beyond about $10^{-15}$ meters.

A second nuclear force is needed to explain **beta decay**, in which a neutron turns into a proton, spitting out an electron and (Pauli's) neutrino. This **weak nuclear force** is feeble and extremely short-ranged, but it is the only force that can change one kind of particle into another, which makes it responsible for beta radioactivity and for the first step of the fusion chain that powers the Sun. Without the weak force there would be no sunlight. In 1935 Hideki Yukawa proposed that short-ranged forces are carried by particles with mass, the heavier the carrier the shorter the reach, an idea that turned out to be exactly right for both nuclear forces.[^23]

The weak force is also strange in a way no other force is. In 1956 Chien-Shiung Wu tested whether beta decay looks the same in a mirror, by lining up the spins of cobalt-60 nuclei in a magnetic field and counting the electrons emitted up versus down. They came out mostly one way.[^24] The weak force distinguishes left from right, a property called **parity violation**, which every other force respects perfectly. Eight years later James Cronin and Val Fitch found that the weak force also treats matter and antimatter slightly differently, **CP violation**, an asymmetry that chapter 14 will show is essential to explaining why the universe contains any matter at all.[^25]

With gravity and electromagnetism, that makes **four fundamental forces**. Everything that happens, from a supernova to a thought, is some combination of the four.

## Splitting and fusing

The nucleus stores energy because of $E = mc^2$. A nucleus weighs slightly *less* than its separated protons and neutrons; the missing mass, the **binding energy**, was released when it formed. Nuclei near iron and nickel are the most tightly bound (nickel-62 wins by a hair; iron-56 is where fusion in stars stops). Nuclei heavier than iron release energy when split (**fission**); nuclei lighter than iron release energy when joined (**fusion**).

Nuclear energies are measured in **electron-volts** (eV): the energy an electron gains crossing a one-volt battery, about $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ joules. Chemical bonds are a few eV. Nuclear reactions are millions of eV (MeV). The Large Hadron Collider works in trillions (TeV). This unit will appear in every remaining chapter.

Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann observed uranium splitting in 1938, and Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained it weeks later.[^4] Each fission releases neutrons that can trigger further fissions, a **chain reaction**, and about 200 MeV of energy per nucleus, tens of millions of times more than a chemical reaction. Fermi built the first reactor in a Chicago squash court in 1942. The bomb followed in 1945. Fusion, which powers stars, was reproduced destructively in 1952 and has been pursued as a power source ever since, by two routes: holding a **plasma** (a gas so hot its atoms have been stripped into bare nuclei and free electrons) in a magnetic bottle, the approach of the international ITER reactor under construction in France, or crushing a fuel pellet with lasers. In December 2022 the laser route reached a milestone: the National Ignition Facility became the first laboratory to get more fusion energy out of a fuel pellet than the laser energy put in, and yields have since grown severalfold.[^5] Neither route yet produces net electricity.

## The particle zoo

From the 1930s to the 1960s, physicists studying cosmic rays and then building **particle accelerators** (machines that smash particles together at nearly light speed, converting kinetic energy into new particles via $E = mc^2$) discovered dozens of new particles: muons, pions, kaons, lambdas, and more. Most lived for less than a microsecond. Nobody had asked for them. Willis Lamb joked in his Nobel lecture that the discoverer of a new particle used to be rewarded with a prize, but "such a discovery now ought to be punished by a $10,000 fine."[^6]

Order came in 1964. Murray Gell-Mann and, independently, George Zweig proposed that protons, neutrons, and the whole zoo of strongly interacting particles are built from smaller constituents. Gell-Mann named them **quarks**, after a line in *Finnegans Wake*.[^7] A proton is two "up" quarks and one "down"; a neutron is one up and two down. Experiments at Stanford in 1968 firing electrons into protons found hard, point-like scatterers inside, just as Rutherford had found the nucleus inside the atom.[^8] Quarks are real. They are also never seen alone: the strong force between them grows *stronger* with distance, so pulling two apart pumps in enough energy to create new quark pairs from the vacuum. This is **confinement**, and it is why every quark is locked inside a composite particle. The flip side, discovered in 1973 by David Gross, Frank Wilczek, and David Politzer, is that at very short distances the force becomes *weak*, so quarks inside a proton rattle around almost freely.[^26] This **asymptotic freedom** is what let the Stanford experiments see quarks as hard, point-like scatterers, and it is the property that makes the theory of the strong force, **quantum chromodynamics**, calculable at all.

## Quantum field theory: what a particle is

To understand the Standard Model you need one conceptual upgrade. In the 1930s and 40s, quantum mechanics was combined with special relativity into **quantum field theory** (QFT). Its central claim: the basic objects in nature are not particles but **fields**, one for each type of particle, filling all of space. A particle is a localized vibration in its field, a quantum of excitation. Every electron in the universe is a ripple in the single electron field, which is why all electrons are perfectly identical.

Forces, too, are fields, and their quanta are particles. The electromagnetic force is carried by the photon: two electrons repel by exchanging photons. This picture, **quantum electrodynamics** (QED), was made to give finite, sensible answers by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger, and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga in the late 1940s, using a technique called **renormalization** to tame infinities that had plagued it for twenty years.[^9] Feynman introduced diagrams, now **Feynman diagrams**, that turn each term in the calculation into a picture of particles meeting and exchanging force carriers. QED predicts the electron's magnetic strength to about one part in a trillion, in agreement with measurement: roughly the precision of measuring the distance from the Earth to the Moon to within the width of a human hair.[^10] Nothing else in science is checked to that precision.

:::key
In quantum field theory, the vacuum is the lowest-energy state of the fields, not an absence of fields. Quantum fluctuations contribute to measurable effects, including shifts in atomic energy levels (the Lamb shift) and forces between conducting plates (the Casimir effect). This does not mean a tunneling particle borrows energy from the vacuum: as chapter 9 explains, tunneling follows from a wavefunction extending through a barrier, with energy conserved.
:::

## The Standard Model

Between roughly 1961 and 1975, theorists assembled a single quantum field theory describing all known particles and three of the four forces. It is called, with deliberate modesty, the **Standard Model**.[^11] Its ingredients:

| Kind | Particles | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Quarks** (6) | up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom | Build protons, neutrons, and all other strongly interacting matter |
| **Leptons** (6) | electron, muon, tau, and three neutrinos | Electron makes atoms; neutrinos barely interact |
| **Force carriers** | photon (electromagnetism), W⁺, W⁻, Z (weak force), 8 gluons (strong force) | Transmit the three non-gravitational forces |
| **Higgs boson** (1) | | Gives mass to the other particles |

The quarks and leptons come in three "generations" of increasing mass; ordinary matter uses only the first (up, down, electron, electron-neutrino). Nobody knows why there are three generations. Each particle also has an antiparticle. The heaviest quark, the top, was the last piece of matter to be found, at Fermilab in 1995; it weighs about as much as a gold atom.[^27]

The structure is governed by **gauge symmetry**, a mathematical requirement that the laws look the same when you make certain adjustments to the fields independently at every point in space. An analogy: insist that a map of altitudes be correct no matter how each town independently chooses its own "sea level." To make that work you are forced to add a new field that records, between every pair of neighboring towns, how their zero points differ. In the Standard Model, that connecting field *is* the force. Demanding the symmetry *forces* the existence of force-carrying particles with exactly the right properties. This is Noether's theorem (chapter 4) at its most powerful: the forces are not added in; they are the price of the symmetry. Electromagnetism, the weak force, and the strong force each correspond to one such symmetry, and the weak and electromagnetic forces turn out to be two aspects of a single **electroweak** force, unified by Sheldon Glashow, Steven Weinberg, and Abdus Salam.[^12] The W and Z particles that theory demanded were found at CERN in 1983, with the predicted masses.[^28]

:::key
Everything in the table above follows from three symmetries plus one field that breaks them. That is the whole content of the Standard Model, and it is why physicists find it beautiful despite its nineteen loose ends.
:::

## The Higgs problem and its solution

Gauge symmetry has an awkward consequence: it requires force carriers to be massless, like the photon. But the weak force is short-ranged, which means its carriers, the W and Z, must be heavy (about 85 and 97 times a proton's mass, as measured in 1983). And the symmetry also forbids quarks and electrons from having mass. Something must break the symmetry without destroying it.

In 1964 several theorists, including Peter Higgs, François Englert, and Robert Brout, found the mechanism.[^13] Add a new field that fills all of space and, uniquely, has a nonzero value even in the vacuum. Particles that interact with this **Higgs field** are slowed by it, and that resistance *is* their mass. The photon does not interact with it and stays massless; the W and Z interact strongly and are heavy; the electron interacts weakly and is light. Mass is not an intrinsic property; it is the strength of a particle's coupling to a background field. Like any quantum field, the Higgs field has a particle, the **Higgs boson**, and finding it was the crucial test.

It took 48 years. On 4 July 2012 the ATLAS and CMS experiments at CERN's Large Hadron Collider announced a new particle with a mass of about 125 GeV, roughly 133 times the proton's, with exactly the expected properties.[^14] The Standard Model was complete. Higgs and Englert received the 2013 Nobel Prize; Brout had died in 2011.

## Why the Standard Model is incomplete

Everyone who works on the Standard Model agrees it is not the final theory. The reasons:

1. **Gravity is missing.** The model does not include it, and attempts to add it as a quantum field theory produce uncontrollable infinities.
2. **Dark matter is missing.** Astronomers observe about five times more matter than the Standard Model's particles can account for (chapter 13). None of the model's particles fits.
3. **Neutrinos have mass.** The original model assumed massless neutrinos. Experiments since 1998 show neutrinos change type as they travel, which requires mass.[^15] The masses are tiny (less than a millionth of the electron's) and their origin is unknown.
4. **Matter outnumbers antimatter.** The Big Bang should have made equal amounts; the universe is almost all matter. The Standard Model's CP violation provides a mechanism for a slight asymmetry, but it is about a billion times too weak.
5. **Too many unexplained numbers.** The model has about 19 free parameters, masses and coupling strengths that must be measured rather than predicted. Why is the electron 1836 times lighter than the proton? Why three generations? The model shrugs.
6. **The hierarchy problem.** Quantum corrections should make the Higgs mass enormous, roughly $10^{17}$ times heavier than observed, unless an unexplained cancellation occurs. Proposed solutions such as **supersymmetry** (a partner particle for every known one) predicted new particles at LHC energies. None have been found.

:::frontier
The LHC has been running since 2010 and has found the Higgs and nothing else new, which is itself a profound result: many favored extensions of the Standard Model are now ruled out. The long-standing hint of new physics in the muon's magnetism, the "muon g-2 anomaly," largely dissolved in 2025 when improved supercomputer calculations of the Standard Model prediction moved to agree with Fermilab's final measurement.[^16] A 2022 claim that the W boson is heavier than predicted was contradicted by a more precise LHC measurement.[^17] Neutrino experiments (KATRIN, JUNO, and the forthcoming DUNE) are pinning down neutrino masses and ordering.[^18] The Standard Model, infuriatingly, keeps winning. See Latest Research for the running scorecard.
:::

## Summary

- Radioactivity (Becquerel, the Curies, Rutherford) revealed that atoms change spontaneously and probabilistically, and supplied the alpha particles that found the nucleus.
- Atoms are mostly empty: a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons.
- Two nuclear forces join gravity and electromagnetism: the strong force binds nuclei; the weak force changes particle types, drives beta decay and solar fusion, and alone among the forces tells left from right and matter from antimatter.
- Protons and neutrons are made of quarks, permanently confined.
- Quantum field theory: particles are ripples in fields; forces are exchanges of field quanta; the vacuum fluctuates.
- The Standard Model: 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 12 force carriers, and the Higgs, whose field gives particles mass. Completed by the Higgs discovery in 2012.
- Known to be incomplete: no gravity, no dark matter, unexplained neutrino masses, matter–antimatter imbalance, and about 19 unexplained numbers.
