## How to use this chapter

Physics is made by people, and knowing the cast makes the plot easier to follow. This roster is organized by era. Each entry gives the person's central contribution and points to the chapter where it appears. It is deliberately selective: these are the figures whose ideas you need in order to hold the whole subject in your head. Where a discovery was shared, the entry says so.

## The founders (before 1700)

**Aristotle** (384–322 BCE). Built the first systematic physics: natural motion, natural place, heavier falls faster. Wrong in ways that took two thousand years to fix, largely because his system matched everyday experience. Chapter 3.

**Nicolaus Copernicus** (1473–1543). Put the Sun at the center in *De revolutionibus* (1543), published the year he died. Raised the question of why we don't feel the Earth move. Chapter 3.

**Tycho Brahe** (1546–1601). Made the most precise pre-telescope measurements of planetary positions. His data made Kepler's laws possible. Chapter 3.

**Johannes Kepler** (1571–1630). Found that planets move in ellipses and obey three exact laws. Described the solar system without explaining it. Chapter 3.

**Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642). Measured motion for the first time, found constant acceleration, established inertia and the relativity of motion, and turned a telescope to the sky. Tried for heresy. Chapter 3.

**Christiaan Huygens** (1629–1695). Wave theory of light, the pendulum clock, and the correct formula for centripetal force. Lost the light argument to Newton for a century, then won. Chapter 6.

**Isaac Newton** (1643–1727). Three laws of motion, universal gravitation, calculus, and the *Principia* (1687). Also the prism experiments and the particle theory of light. Chapters 2, 3, 6.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646–1716). Independently invented calculus, in the notation we still use, and argued for $mv^2$ as the true measure of motion, an early form of kinetic energy. Chapters 2, 4.

## The classical builders (1700–1900)

**Émilie du Châtelet** (1706–1749). Translated Newton into French with commentary and championed the concept of energy conservation using 's Gravesande's experiments. Chapter 4.

**Daniel Bernoulli** (1700–1782). Founded fluid mechanics and the kinetic theory of gases. Chapter 3.

**Charles-Augustin de Coulomb** (1736–1806). Measured the force between electric charges: an inverse-square law like gravity. Chapter 6.

**Alessandro Volta** (1745–1827). The battery (1800), which turned electricity into a steady, usable current. Chapter 6.

**Thomas Young** (1773–1829). The double-slit experiment, which showed light to be a wave. Chapter 6.

**Hans Christian Ørsted** (1777–1851) and **André-Marie Ampère** (1775–1836). Found that electric currents make magnetic fields, and wrote the mathematics of it. Chapter 6.

**Augustin Fresnel** (1788–1827). The mathematical wave theory of light, polarization, and the predicted bright spot that convinced the doubters. Chapter 6.

**Joseph-Louis Lagrange** (1736–1813) and **William Rowan Hamilton** (1805–1865). Rewrote mechanics as the principle of least action. Their formulation survives into quantum theory when Newton's does not. Chapter 3.

**Pierre-Simon Laplace** (1749–1827). Articulated determinism ("Laplace's demon") and did the mathematics of celestial mechanics. Chapter 3.

**Sadi Carnot** (1796–1832). Found the maximum efficiency of any heat engine, founding thermodynamics. Died of cholera at 36. Chapter 5.

**Michael Faraday** (1791–1867). Discovered electromagnetic induction, invented the field concept, and connected light to magnetism. The greatest experimentalist of his century, with no formal mathematics. Chapter 6.

**James Prescott Joule** (1818–1889). Measured the exchange rate between work and heat, establishing that heat is energy. Chapter 4.

**Rudolf Clausius** (1822–1888). Defined entropy and stated the second law in its modern form. Chapter 5.

**James Clerk Maxwell** (1831–1879). Unified electricity, magnetism, and light in four equations, predicted radio waves, founded statistical mechanics, and invented the demon. Died at 48. Chapters 5, 6.

**William Thomson, Lord Kelvin** (1824–1907). The absolute temperature scale, much of thermodynamics, and the 1900 "two clouds" lecture. Chapters 5, 14.

**Ludwig Boltzmann** (1844–1906). Explained entropy as a count of microstates, $S = k \ln W$, and fought for the reality of atoms. Chapter 5.

**J. Willard Gibbs** (1839–1903). Built the general machinery of statistical mechanics and chemical thermodynamics, largely unnoticed in his lifetime. Chapter 5.

**Henri Becquerel** (1852–1908). Discovered radioactivity in 1896, by accident, in a drawer. Chapter 11.

**J. J. Thomson** (1856–1940). Discovered the electron (1897), the first subatomic particle. Chapters 9, 11.

**Henri Poincaré** (1854–1912). Found chaos in the three-body problem and much of the mathematics of relativity. Chapters 3, 7.

**Albert Michelson** (1852–1931) and **Edward Morley** (1838–1923). Failed to find the ether in 1887, the most important null result in physics. Chapter 6.

**Heinrich Hertz** (1857–1894). Produced and detected radio waves, confirming Maxwell. Chapter 6.

**Hendrik Lorentz** (1853–1928). Found the transformation equations of special relativity before Einstein, without understanding what they meant. Chapter 7.

## The revolutionaries (1900–1935)

**Max Planck** (1858–1947). Introduced the quantum of energy in 1900 to explain blackbody radiation, and spent years trying to take it back. Chapter 9.

**Albert Einstein** (1879–1955). Special relativity, $E = mc^2$, the photon, Brownian motion (all 1905), and general relativity (1915). Then thirty years of principled resistance to quantum mechanics that produced, via the EPR paper, the question Bell answered. Chapters 7, 8, 9, 10.

**Marie Curie** (1867–1934). Discovered polonium and radium, coined "radioactivity," and won Nobel Prizes in both physics and chemistry. Her work made nuclear physics possible. Chapter 11.

**Ernest Rutherford** (1871–1937). Discovered the atomic nucleus (1911), the proton, and the concept of radioactive half-life. Trained a generation of Nobel laureates. Chapter 11.

**Niels Bohr** (1885–1962). Quantized the atom (1913), formulated the correspondence principle, and led the Copenhagen interpretation. Einstein's chief debating opponent for thirty years. Chapters 9, 10.

**Hermann Minkowski** (1864–1909). Recast special relativity as the geometry of spacetime. Chapter 7.

**Emmy Noether** (1882–1935). Proved that symmetries imply conservation laws, the deepest structural result in physics. Never held a paid professorship. Chapters 2, 4.

**Arthur Eddington** (1882–1944). Led the 1919 eclipse expedition that confirmed general relativity and made Einstein famous. Chapter 8.

**Karl Schwarzschild** (1873–1916). Solved Einstein's equations for a point mass while serving in World War I, finding what became the black hole. Died months later. Chapter 8.

**Louis de Broglie** (1892–1987). Proposed in his 1924 thesis that matter has wavelength $h/p$. Chapter 9.

**Wolfgang Pauli** (1900–1958). Exclusion principle (1925), which structures the periodic table, and the neutrino (1930). Chapters 4, 9.

**Werner Heisenberg** (1901–1976). Matrix mechanics (1925) and the uncertainty principle (1927). Chapter 9.

**Erwin Schrödinger** (1887–1961). The wave equation (1926), the word "entanglement," and the cat (1935). Chapters 9, 10.

**Max Born** (1882–1970). The probability interpretation of the wavefunction. Chapter 9.

**Paul Dirac** (1902–1984). United quantum mechanics with special relativity, predicting spin and antimatter. Chapter 9.

**Henrietta Leavitt** (1868–1921). The Cepheid period–brightness law (1912), the first rung of the cosmic distance ladder. Chapter 13.

**Vesto Slipher** (1875–1969). Measured the redshifts of galaxies through the 1910s, the data behind Hubble's law. Chapter 13.

**Edwin Hubble** (1889–1953). Showed galaxies are receding, and faster with distance: the expanding universe. Chapter 13.

**Arthur Eddington** also belongs here for stellar physics: he argued in 1920 that stars are powered by fusion. Chapter 13.

**James Chadwick** (1891–1974). Discovered the neutron (1932). Chapter 11.

**George Gamow** (1904–1968). Explained alpha decay as quantum tunneling (1928) and co-predicted the elements made in the Big Bang. Chapters 11, 13.

**Fritz Zwicky** (1898–1974). Proposed dark matter (1933) and coined "supernova." Chapter 13.

**Georges Lemaître** (1894–1966). Derived the expanding universe from Einstein's equations before Hubble's data and proposed the "primeval atom," the Big Bang. Chapters 8, 13.

**Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar** (1910–1995). Found the maximum mass of a white dwarf, implying that heavier stars must collapse further. Chapter 12.

## The mid-century (1935–1980)

**Lise Meitner** (1878–1968) and **Otto Frisch** (1904–1979). Explained nuclear fission in 1939. Meitner, excluded from the Nobel given to Hahn, has element 109 named for her. Chapter 11.

**Enrico Fermi** (1901–1954). Theory of beta decay and the weak force, the first nuclear reactor (1942), and the art of estimation. Chapters 2, 11.

**Hideki Yukawa** (1907–1981). Predicted that nuclear forces are carried by massive particles (1935); Japan's first Nobel laureate. Chapter 11.

**Hans Bethe** (1906–2005). Worked out how stars burn hydrogen (1939); later central to nuclear physics and quantum electrodynamics. Chapter 13.

**J. Robert Oppenheimer** (1904–1967). Showed that massive stars must collapse into black holes (1939); led the Manhattan Project. Chapters 8, 11.

**Chien-Shiung Wu** (1912–1997). Proved the weak force violates mirror symmetry (1956), one of the great experiments of the century, unrewarded by the Nobel committee. Chapter 11.

**John Wheeler** (1911–2008). Popularized "black hole," summarized general relativity in a sentence, and taught Feynman, Thorne, and Everett. Chapter 8.

**Roger Penrose** (b. 1931). Proved singularities are unavoidable inside black holes (1965); Nobel Prize 2020. Chapter 8.

**Jocelyn Bell Burnell** (b. 1943). Discovered pulsars (1967) as a graduate student; the Nobel went to her supervisor. Chapter 13.

**Russell Hulse** (b. 1950) and **Joseph Taylor** (b. 1941). The binary pulsar (1974), the first evidence for gravitational waves. Chapter 8.

**Richard Feynman** (1918–1988), **Julian Schwinger** (1918–1994), **Sin-Itiro Tomonaga** (1906–1979). Made quantum electrodynamics work; Feynman's diagrams became the language of particle physics. Chapter 11.

**John Bardeen** (1908–1991). The transistor (1947, with Brattain and Shockley) and the theory of superconductivity (1957, with Cooper and Schrieffer). The only person with two Nobel Prizes in physics. Chapter 12.

**Lev Landau** (1908–1968). Theory of phase transitions and symmetry breaking, superfluidity, and much of Soviet theoretical physics. Chapter 12.

**Fred Hoyle** (1915–2001). Worked out how stars make the elements; coined "Big Bang" as an insult and never accepted it. Chapter 13.

**Hugh Everett** (1930–1982). The many-worlds interpretation (1957). Left physics after it was ignored. Chapter 10.

**Murray Gell-Mann** (1929–2019). Quarks (1964, with Zweig independently) and the classification of the particle zoo. Chapter 11.

**Peter Higgs** (1929–2024), **François Englert** (b. 1932), **Robert Brout** (1928–2011). The mechanism by which particles get mass (1964). Confirmed 48 years later. Chapter 11.

**John Stewart Bell** (1928–1990). Bell's theorem (1964), which made the reality of quantum mechanics an experimental question. Chapter 10.

**Sheldon Glashow** (b. 1932), **Steven Weinberg** (1933–2021), **Abdus Salam** (1926–1996). Unified the electromagnetic and weak forces. Chapter 11.

**Arno Penzias** (1933–2024) and **Robert Wilson** (b. 1936). Found the cosmic microwave background by accident in 1965. Chapter 13.

**Vera Rubin** (1928–2016). Measured galaxy rotation curves that established dark matter. Chapter 13.

**Philip Anderson** (1923–2020). "More is different"; the mechanism later called Higgs, found first in superconductors; localization. Chapter 12.

**Stephen Hawking** (1942–2018) and **Jacob Bekenstein** (1947–2015). Black hole entropy and Hawking radiation, the origin of the black hole information problem. Chapters 5, 14.

**Kenneth Wilson** (1936–2013). The renormalization group, which explained both phase transitions and what "fundamental" means in quantum field theory. Chapter 12.

## The contemporary era (1980–present)

**Alan Guth** (b. 1947). Cosmic inflation (1981). Chapter 13.

**Alain Aspect** (b. 1947), **John Clauser** (b. 1942), **Anton Zeilinger** (b. 1945). Experimental tests of Bell's inequality, 1972–2015; Nobel Prize 2022. Chapter 10.

**Klaus von Klitzing** (b. 1943), **Robert Laughlin** (b. 1950), **Horst Störmer** (b. 1949), **Daniel Tsui** (b. 1939). The integer and fractional quantum Hall effects, the beginning of topological matter. Chapter 12.

**John Clarke** (b. 1942), **Michel Devoret** (b. 1953), **John Martinis** (b. 1958). Showed quantum tunneling and quantized energy in a macroscopic circuit (1984–85); Nobel Prize 2025. The foundation of superconducting quantum computing. Chapters 10, 12.

**Saul Perlmutter** (b. 1959), **Brian Schmidt** (b. 1967), **Adam Riess** (b. 1969). The accelerating universe and dark energy (1998). Riess also leads the SH0ES team at the center of the Hubble tension. Chapter 13.

**Juan Maldacena** (b. 1968). The AdS/CFT correspondence (1997), the exact example of holography that dominates quantum gravity research. Chapter 14.

**Peter Shor** (b. 1959). The factoring algorithm (1994) that made quantum computing a serious goal. Chapter 10.

**Rainer Weiss** (1932–2025), **Kip Thorne** (b. 1940), **Barry Barish** (b. 1936). Built LIGO and detected gravitational waves (2015); Nobel Prize 2017. Chapter 8.

**Edward Witten** (b. 1951). The leading figure in string theory and the only physicist to win the Fields Medal in mathematics. Chapter 14.

## The collaborations

Since the 1970s the largest discoveries have been made by teams too large to name. Know these by their initials.

- **CERN / ATLAS / CMS**: the Large Hadron Collider near Geneva; discovered the Higgs boson in 2012.
- **LIGO / Virgo / KAGRA**: gravitational-wave detectors in the US, Italy, and Japan.
- **Planck / WMAP / COBE**: satellites that mapped the cosmic microwave background.
- **Event Horizon Telescope**: imaged black holes in 2019 and 2022.
- **DESI / Euclid / Rubin**: the current generation of galaxy surveys testing dark energy.
- **Fermilab**: US particle laboratory; the muon g-2 experiment and the coming DUNE neutrino experiment.

:::key
A pattern worth noticing across this list: the crucial idea often came from someone young (Einstein at 26, Heisenberg at 23, Carnot at 28, Bell working in his spare time), was resisted by the establishment (Boltzmann, Everett, Meitner, Noether), and was confirmed by experiment only decades later (the Higgs, gravitational waves, Bell's theorem). Physics is conservative in the short run and radical in the long run. That is roughly the right way for it to be.
:::
