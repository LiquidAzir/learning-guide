## How do we reconstruct a universe we cannot watch from the beginning?

General relativity said the universe could not sit still. Einstein did not believe it. This chapter is about the century in which astronomers found out he was wrong to doubt, and then found two more things nobody was ready for.

## The universe is expanding

In the 1920s Edwin Hubble, using the 100-inch telescope on Mount Wilson, established that the fuzzy "spiral nebulae" were other galaxies, vastly distant, each containing billions of stars. He could measure their distances thanks to Henrietta Leavitt, who had found in 1912 that a class of pulsing stars, the **Cepheids**, brighten and dim on a schedule set by their true brightness, so that timing one tells you how far away it is.[^18] Meanwhile Vesto Slipher had spent the 1910s finding that the light of most nebulae is stretched toward the red.[^19] In 1929 Hubble put Slipher's redshifts together with his own distances and found the pattern: the farther away a galaxy is, the more its light is stretched.[^1] **Redshift**, the stretching of light to longer wavelengths, indicates that the source is moving away. Every galaxy is receding from us, and the recession speed $v$ is proportional to distance $d$:

$$v = H_0 \, d$$

$H_0$ is the **Hubble constant**, today's rate of expansion. Its modern value is about 70 kilometers per second for every megaparsec (3.26 million light-years) of distance.

This does not mean we are at the center. Draw dots on a balloon and inflate it; every dot sees every other dot receding, faster the farther away. Space itself is stretching, carrying the galaxies with it. Georges Lemaître had predicted exactly this from Einstein's equations two years earlier, and drew the obvious conclusion: run the film backwards and everything was once together.[^2] The universe had a beginning. (Since 2018 the relation has officially been the Hubble–Lemaître law.)

The name **Big Bang** was coined by Fred Hoyle in a 1949 radio broadcast; he preferred a "steady state" universe with no beginning, though whether he meant the phrase as a sneer is disputed.[^20] The name stuck, and so did the theory, for reasons that arrived in 1965.

## The afterglow

If the universe was once compressed, it was hot, hot enough to be an opaque glowing **plasma**, a gas so hot that electrons are stripped from their nuclei and light cannot travel far without scattering. As it expanded and cooled, it would have become transparent, releasing a flash of light that should still be traveling, stretched by the expansion from visible light into microwaves, coming from every direction at once.

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs found it by accident in 1965 while trying to eliminate noise from a radio antenna.[^3] The noise was the same in every direction, day and night, all year. It was the **cosmic microwave background** (CMB): light from when the universe was 380,000 years old, now cooled to 2.725 kelvin. It is the oldest thing anyone has ever seen. A small part of the static on an untuned analog television was this signal.

The CMB is astonishingly uniform, the same temperature everywhere to one part in 100,000. The tiny variations that do exist, mapped in exquisite detail by the COBE, WMAP, and Planck satellites, are the seeds of all later structure: slightly denser regions that gravity amplified over billions of years into galaxies and clusters.[^4] The pattern of these ripples encodes the age, composition, and geometry of the universe, and reading it is the central technique of modern cosmology.

## What the Big Bang made

The theory makes a quantitative prediction: in its first three minutes, the universe was hot enough for nuclear fusion, and should have converted about 25% of its hydrogen into helium by mass, with traces of deuterium and lithium.[^5] That is what we observe in the oldest gas clouds, to good precision. Almost all the helium in the universe, including what fills party balloons, was made before any star existed.

Everything heavier, the carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe, the iron in your blood, was made later inside stars and scattered by their deaths. Fred Hoyle, who never accepted the Big Bang, worked out this stellar alchemy in 1957.[^6]

## Stars: where the elements come from

Stars deserve their own section, because the rest of this chapter keeps using them as tools. A star is a ball of plasma held together by gravity and held up by the pressure of the fusion in its core. Arthur Eddington argued in 1920 that fusing hydrogen into helium, with the mass difference released as energy by $E = mc^2$, was the only thing that could power the Sun for billions of years.[^21] Hans Bethe worked out the exact reaction chains in 1939.[^22] The Sun converts about four million tons of mass into light every second and has been doing so for 4.6 billion years, with about five billion to go.

Plotting stars' brightness against their color (the **Hertzsprung–Russell diagram**, from 1910) revealed that most lie along one band, the **main sequence**, where they spend their hydrogen-burning lives; the massive ones are hot, blue, and short-lived, the small ones cool, red, and long-lived.[^23] What happens at the end depends on mass. A star like the Sun swells into a red giant, sheds its outer layers, and leaves a **white dwarf**, an Earth-sized ember held up by the quantum degeneracy pressure of chapter 12. A star above about eight solar masses fuses successively heavier elements up to iron, at which point fusion stops paying, the core collapses in under a second, and the star explodes as a **supernova**, briefly outshining its galaxy and scattering newly made elements into space. The collapsed core becomes a **neutron star**, a city-sized object as dense as a nucleus, or, above roughly three solar masses, a black hole. In 1967 Jocelyn Bell Burnell noticed a radio signal pulsing every 1.3 seconds with clockwork regularity; it was a spinning neutron star, the first **pulsar**, and proof that these objects exist.[^24]

Stars are also the rungs of the **cosmic distance ladder**. Nearby distances come from parallax, the tiny shift in a star's position as Earth orbits the Sun. Cepheids extend the ladder to nearby galaxies. **Type Ia supernovae**, exploding white dwarfs that reach a predictable peak brightness, extend it across the visible universe. Every measurement of the expansion of the cosmos rests on this ladder, which is why arguments about its rungs (see the Hubble tension below) matter so much.

## Dark matter

In 1933 Fritz Zwicky measured the speeds of galaxies in the Coma Cluster and found they were moving far too fast to be held together by the gravity of the visible stars; the cluster should fly apart.[^7] He proposed *dunkle Materie*, dark matter. Nobody paid much attention for forty years.

In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars orbit within galaxies.[^8] Newton and Kepler say stars far from the center should orbit slowly, as outer planets do. Instead the orbital speeds stay flat all the way out, as though each galaxy sits inside a vast invisible halo of mass extending far beyond its stars. Every spiral galaxy measured since shows the same thing.

{{fig:rotation-curve|A galaxy's rotation curve. If the visible stars and gas were all the mass, orbital speeds would fall off with distance from the center, as the planets' do. Measured speeds stay flat, which requires several times more mass than can be seen.}}

Independent lines of evidence agree. **Gravitational lensing**, the bending of light by mass predicted by Einstein, shows clusters bending light far more than their visible matter allows. The CMB ripple pattern requires about five times more matter than the Standard Model's particles can supply. And computer simulations cannot form galaxies on the observed timescale without it.

The conclusion: about 85% of the matter in the universe is something that has mass and gravity but does not emit, absorb, or reflect light, and is not made of any known particle. It has been searched for directly in underground detectors for four decades. The leading candidate, a heavy weakly interacting particle called a **WIMP**, has been excluded across most of its expected range by experiments such as LZ and XENONnT.[^9] Alternatives include **axions**, ultralight particles proposed for other reasons, and **primordial black holes**. Some physicists argue instead that gravity itself behaves differently at low accelerations (**modified gravity**), but this has trouble matching the CMB and cluster data.[^10] The identity of dark matter is the largest open question in astrophysics.

## Dark energy

In 1998 two teams measured the distances to Type Ia supernovae in remote galaxies, intending to measure how much the expansion was slowing under gravity's pull. It was not slowing. It was speeding up.[^11] Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize.

An accelerating expansion needs something that pushes. The simplest candidate is Einstein's cosmological constant $\Lambda$, the term he added to his equations in 1917 and later discarded: an energy that belongs to empty space itself, constant everywhere and for all time, with a repulsive gravitational effect. It is called **dark energy**, and it makes up about 68% of the energy content of the universe.

The trouble is that nobody knows what it is. Quantum field theory predicts that the vacuum should have energy, but the naive calculation gives a value about $10^{120}$ times larger than observed, often called the worst prediction in the history of physics.[^12] Why the cosmological constant is small but not zero is unsolved.

## The standard model of cosmology

Putting this together gives the **ΛCDM model** (Lambda plus Cold Dark Matter): a universe 13.8 billion years old, spatially flat (parallel lines stay parallel over cosmic distances; space as a whole is not curved), containing about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, and 68% dark energy, that began hot and dense and has been expanding ever since.[^4] Six numbers fit thousands of independent data points. It is the cosmological equivalent of the Standard Model: spectacularly successful and obviously missing something, since 95% of its contents are unidentified.

One more ingredient is usually included. The CMB's extreme uniformity is a puzzle: regions on opposite sides of the sky have never been in contact, yet have the same temperature. **Cosmic inflation**, proposed by Alan Guth in 1981, posits that in its first $10^{-32}$ seconds the universe expanded by a factor of at least $10^{26}$, stretching a tiny causally connected patch to encompass everything we see and flattening its geometry.[^13] Inflation also explains the statistical pattern of the CMB ripples as quantum fluctuations blown up to cosmic size. It is widely accepted but not proven; its distinctive prediction of primordial gravitational waves has not been detected, and some physicists regard the idea as too flexible to test.

:::math How we know the age of the universe
The simplest estimate is Hubble's law run backward. If galaxies at distance $d$ recede at $v = H_0 d$, they were all together a time $t = d/v = 1/H_0$ ago. With $H_0 \approx 70$ km/s/Mpc, converting units gives $1/H_0 \approx 14$ billion years. The real calculation, using general relativity with the measured mix of matter and dark energy, gives $13.8$ billion years, with an uncertainty of about 0.02 billion. The naive estimate lands so close only by a coincidence of history: dark energy's recent acceleration almost exactly cancels the deceleration that matter caused earlier.
:::

## The cracks

ΛCDM has tensions, and they are where cosmology is most alive.

**The Hubble tension.** Measuring $H_0$ from the CMB (early universe) gives $67.4 \pm 0.5$. Measuring it from supernovae and pulsating stars in nearby galaxies (late universe) gives about $73 \pm 1$.[^14] The two disagree by about five standard deviations, which is discovery-level significance if neither has a hidden error. Either a measurement is wrong or the universe did something between then and now that ΛCDM does not include. James Webb Space Telescope observations have confirmed that the Hubble Space Telescope's distance measurements were not the problem, though Wendy Freedman's team, using different kinds of stars as distance markers, gets about 70.4 and argues the discrepancy is smaller than claimed.[^15]

**Is dark energy constant?** The DESI survey, mapping tens of millions of galaxies, reported in 2024 and 2025 that its data fit slightly better if dark energy is *weakening* over time rather than constant.[^16] The evidence is around three sigma: suggestive, not conclusive. If it holds, the cosmological constant is not a constant, and the future of the universe changes.

**Early galaxies.** JWST has found galaxies that appear surprisingly massive and mature within the first few hundred million years, earlier than models expected.[^17] Some candidates have been revised down; the general puzzle remains.

:::frontier
This is a golden age for cosmology data. DESI continues; the Euclid space telescope began its survey in 2024; the Vera Rubin Observatory released its first images in 2025 and began its ten-year survey of the whole southern sky in June 2026. Within this decade these should decide whether the Hubble tension is real and whether dark energy evolves. The Latest Research section tracks each result as it arrives.
:::

## Summary

- The universe is expanding, so it had a hot dense beginning 13.8 billion years ago. The cosmic microwave background is the afterglow; the first three minutes made the helium.
- Stars fuse light elements into heavy ones and scatter them when they die; their life cycle, from main sequence to white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole, also supplies the distance markers cosmology depends on.
- Dark matter, 85% of all matter, is detected only by its gravity and is not made of known particles.
- Dark energy, 68% of everything, makes the expansion accelerate; it may be Einstein's cosmological constant, and its small size is unexplained.
- ΛCDM fits nearly everything with six numbers but leaves 95% of the universe unidentified.
- Live disputes: the Hubble tension, possibly evolving dark energy, and surprisingly early galaxies.
