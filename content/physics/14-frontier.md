## How do you judge a claim at the edge of physics?

You now have the whole map: mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, relativity, quantum mechanics, the Standard Model, condensed matter, and cosmology. This chapter walks the edge of that map. Each section states a problem precisely, explains why it is hard, and describes the leading attempts. None of these problems has an accepted solution. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

## Quantum gravity

**The problem.** General relativity describes gravity as smooth, definite spacetime geometry. Quantum mechanics says nothing at small scales is smooth or definite. Try to treat gravity as a quantum field theory like the others and the calculations produce infinities that cannot be tamed by renormalization.[^1] The two pillars of modern physics contradict each other, and both are right in their domains.

**Where it matters.** Almost nowhere accessible. Gravity is so weak that quantum gravitational effects only become important at the **Planck scale**, around $10^{-35}$ meters and $10^{19}$ GeV of energy, about $10^{15}$ times beyond the Large Hadron Collider. The only places nature reaches this regime are the singularities inside black holes and the first $10^{-43}$ seconds after the Big Bang. This is why the problem has resisted solution for a century: there are no experiments.

**Leading approaches.**

- **String theory** proposes that the fundamental objects are not point particles but tiny vibrating strings, with different vibrations appearing as different particles.[^2] One vibration mode has exactly the properties of the **graviton**, the hypothetical quantum of gravity, so gravity is included automatically and the infinities vanish. The costs: the theory requires ten spacetime dimensions (six curled up too small to see), predicts a vast landscape of possible universes with no way to pick ours, and has made no testable prediction in 40 years. It has, however, produced deep mathematics and the holographic principle below.
- **Loop quantum gravity** keeps spacetime as the fundamental object and quantizes it directly, predicting that space comes in discrete chunks of Planck size.[^3] It avoids extra dimensions but has struggled to reproduce ordinary smooth spacetime as a limit, and also lacks experimental tests.
- **Holography** is the most striking idea to emerge. Black hole entropy scales with surface *area*, not volume (chapter 5), suggesting that all the information in a region can be encoded on its boundary. In 1997 Juan Maldacena found an exact example: a certain gravitational theory in a curved space is mathematically identical to a quantum field theory without gravity on that space's boundary.[^4] The paper introducing this **AdS/CFT correspondence** is the most cited in high-energy physics. It suggests that spacetime and gravity may *emerge* from quantum entanglement, and calculations since 2019 using it appear to resolve the black hole information paradox: information that falls into a black hole comes back out, encoded in the radiation.[^5] The catch is that the exact example is for a universe unlike ours; extending it to real spacetime is ongoing work.

**Honest status.** No approach is confirmed. The field has produced beautiful mathematics and real conceptual progress on black holes, but no prediction that experiment has checked.

## The measurement problem

Covered in chapter 10: quantum mechanics does not say when or how a superposition becomes a definite outcome. The interpretations agree on all experiments. Progress, if it comes, will come from objective-collapse theories being ruled out or confirmed by ever-larger superposition experiments, or from a theoretical insight that makes one interpretation preferable on grounds other than taste.[^6]

## Dark matter

Covered in chapter 13. Five times more matter than the known particles account for, seen only through gravity. The direct-detection program for WIMPs has excluded most of the theoretically favored range without a confirmed signal; in 2026 the LZ experiment reported a single unexplained event at 2.6 sigma, far below discovery.[^7] Axion searches are scanning their range. The possibility that dark matter interacts with nothing but gravity, and can never be detected in a lab, is taken seriously.

## Dark energy and the cosmological constant

Why does empty space have energy, and why is it $10^{120}$ times smaller than quantum field theory naively predicts, but not zero? No accepted answer. Some argue the value is set by **anthropic selection**: in a multiverse of regions with different constants, only regions with small $\Lambda$ form galaxies and observers.[^8] Many physicists find this unsatisfying or unscientific. The DESI hints that dark energy may weaken over time, if confirmed, would rule out a pure cosmological constant and open the question wider.[^9]

## Why the Standard Model has the numbers it has

About 19 parameters, unexplained. Why three generations of matter? Why is the Higgs so light (the **hierarchy problem**)? Why is the strong force perfectly symmetric under time reversal when nothing requires it (the **strong CP problem**)? Supersymmetry, the most popular proposed answer to the hierarchy problem, predicted new particles at LHC energies and none appeared.[^10] The absence of new physics at the LHC is itself a major result: it means the answers, if they exist, lie at energies we cannot currently reach, or in directions no one has thought of.

## Matter versus antimatter

The Big Bang should have produced equal amounts. The universe is nearly all matter; if it were not, we would see the gamma rays from matter and antimatter annihilating at the boundaries. Andrei Sakharov showed in 1967 what is needed: processes that treat matter and antimatter slightly differently (the CP violation of chapter 11), happening while the universe was out of thermal equilibrium.[^11] The Standard Model has such processes, but they are about a billion times too weak. Neutrinos are the prime suspect: if they violate matter-antimatter symmetry strongly enough, they could have tipped the balance. DUNE in the United States and Hyper-Kamiokande in Japan are being built to find out.[^12]

## Neutrino masses

Neutrinos have mass but the Standard Model says they should not. The masses are at least a million times smaller than the electron's, and we do not know the actual values, only differences between them, or even which is heaviest. The **seesaw mechanism** would explain the smallness by postulating very heavy partner neutrinos, but these have not been found. KATRIN has set the upper limit at 0.45 eV; JUNO, which began taking data in 2025, aims to determine the mass ordering.[^13]

## Is the universe fine-tuned, and is there more than one?

Several constants of nature seem set to values that permit complex structures: slightly different values for the strong force and no carbon forms; slightly different values for $\Lambda$ and no galaxies form. Whether this requires explanation, and whether a **multiverse** of regions with different constants is that explanation, is contested. Inflation's mathematics tends to produce such a multiverse; string theory supplies the landscape of possible constants. Critics argue that a theory predicting everything predicts nothing.[^14] This is a live debate about what counts as science.

## The arrow of time

Why was the early universe in a state of such extraordinarily low entropy? Chapter 5 explained that the second law follows from this initial condition, but the initial condition itself has no accepted explanation. Proposals range from inflation to cyclic universes to time-symmetric cosmologies in which the arrow reverses on the other side of the Big Bang.[^15]

## Emergence and complexity

Not all frontiers are at the extremes of size. How does life's complexity arise from physics? How does turbulence work, the problem Feynman called the central one classical physics never solved (chapter 3)? Is there a general theory of how many simple interacting parts produce collective behavior, one that would cover neurons, economies, and superconductors alike? Condensed matter physics and the study of complex systems are attacking these, with the 2021 Nobel Prize recognizing work on complex systems and climate.[^16]

## Quantum technology

Not a mystery but a frontier of capability. Whether large-scale, error-corrected quantum computers can be built is now an engineering question with a lot of money behind it. Google's 2024 demonstration of below-threshold error correction, and neutral-atom and trapped-ion machines running logical qubits in 2025 and 2026, suggest yes.[^17] Their arrival would transform cryptography, chemistry simulation, and materials design, and would be the largest practical payoff of the quantum revolution since the transistor.

## How to read a physics headline

A field guide, from the material above:

1. **Check the sigma.** Three sigma is "evidence" and usually goes away. Five is a discovery. The muon anomaly never got past 4.2; the LK-99 announcement reported no statistics at all.
2. **Ask who else has seen it.** One lab is a claim; independent replication is a result. Room-temperature superconductivity died here, and so did the 7-sigma W-mass anomaly once a second experiment measured the Standard Model value.
3. **Distinguish "consistent with" from "detected."** Most theoretical results are the former.
4. **Ask what it would take to be wrong.** A theory that cannot fail is not yet physics. This is the serious objection to some multiverse and string-landscape ideas.
5. **Check the Latest Research list** in this guide, where each result carries a status: reported, preliminary, disputed, or retracted.

:::key
The open questions are not signs of a field in trouble. They are the normal condition of a science that is honest about its edges. In 1900 Lord Kelvin gave a lecture on the "two clouds" hanging over physics: the failure to detect the Earth's motion through the ether (the Michelson–Morley result) and the failure of Maxwell and Boltzmann's statistics to predict the heat capacities of gases.[^18] Those two clouds became relativity and quantum mechanics. Today's clouds are bigger, and it is reasonable to expect that whatever resolves them will look as strange to us as quantum mechanics did to Kelvin.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
Two independent instruments report a similar anomaly. What should you ask before calling it a new law of nature?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Ask whether they share calibration assumptions, analysis methods, or a background model that could produce the same error. Check the uncertainty, the number of comparisons made, and whether a new theory predicts other testable effects. Agreement strengthens a claim only to the extent that the evidence is independent.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Quantum gravity: relativity and quantum mechanics conflict at the Planck scale; string theory, loop quantum gravity, and holography are candidates, none tested.
- Dark matter, dark energy, the Standard Model's unexplained numbers, the matter-antimatter imbalance, neutrino masses, and the low entropy of the early universe are all unsolved.
- The LHC's failure to find anything beyond the Higgs, and the direct-detection experiments' failure to find WIMPs, are results in their own right that have reshaped which ideas are taken seriously.
- Quantum computing is crossing from science to engineering.
- Read every headline with the five-sigma rule and the replication rule in mind.
