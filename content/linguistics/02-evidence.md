## “Nobody says that” is a testable claim

Suppose someone says a particular phrase never occurs. You find it in three online posts. Have you settled the question?

You have challenged a literal claim about occurrence. But you have not shown that the phrase is common, acceptable to every speaker, or part of the same construction in each post. One might quote an error; another might use a different meaning. Linguistic evidence becomes useful when the question is specific enough for the observation to answer it.

## Four views of the same system

A **corpus** is a collected body of language data: transcripts, recordings, texts, or some combination. It can show what people actually produced in the sampled situations. A corpus of newspaper editorials, however, is a poor stand-in for conversations between toddlers and caregivers. CHILDES makes child-language recordings and transcripts available with documentation, allowing researchers to inspect development rather than rely only on memorable anecdotes.[^1]

**Elicitation** asks speakers for forms, interpretations, or judgments in a designed setting. Presenting a short story before a sentence can reveal a reading that sounds odd in isolation. The researcher's wording can also lead the participant, so alternatives and controls matter.

Experiments vary a factor while measuring a response: reading time, choice of picture, recall, or an eye movement. A slower response could reflect a difficult grammatical dependency, but also an unfamiliar word or an awkward picture. Match competing conditions carefully.

Computational models make an explanation explicit enough to run. If a model reproduces a pattern, that demonstrates one possible route to the result. Showing that humans use that route requires further evidence. A model with access to future words cannot by itself explain how a listener predicts the next word before hearing it.

## Grammaticality is not a popularity contest

**Acceptability** is a person's judgment of how an expression sounds in a setting. **Grammaticality** concerns whether it fits an analysis of the language's structural system. They are related, but memory load, meaning, context, familiarity, and prescriptive schooling can affect judgments.

Compare “The curious violin repaired the sandwich” with “Violin the repaired curious sandwich the.” The first is structurally ordinary English but semantically unusual. The second disrupts ordinary phrase structure. Give the first a cartoon context and its meaning becomes easier; that is a different change from repairing the second's word order.

Collect judgments from multiple relevant speakers and use several examples of each construction. Fifty sentences from one person are not fifty independent speakers. Likewise, many closely related languages can inherit a feature from one ancestor. Sample size must match the claim's unit of generalization.

### A worked corpus comparison

In an invented dataset, an informal corpus contains 120 instances of a construction in 60,000 words. A formal corpus contains 200 in 400,000 words. Raw counts suggest more formal use. Per 10,000 words, the rates are:

$$r_{informal}=\frac{120}{60000}\times10000=20,$$

$$r_{formal}=\frac{200}{400000}\times10000=5.$$

The informal rate is four times the formal rate. This is descriptive evidence, not proof that formality caused the difference. Genre, author, topic, and transcription choices might differ too.

Even words may be the wrong denominator. To study whether speakers omit a particular pronoun, count contexts in which that pronoun could occur. A cooking transcript and a weather report create different opportunities. Preserve the surrounding examples so another analyst can check your coding.
:::

## Evidence includes the people who provide it

Recordings can identify speakers even when names are removed. Agree on consent, access, attribution, and reuse before collecting material. A community may welcome teaching resources while restricting sacred narratives or public voice recordings. Technical availability is not a substitute for that agreement.

When a study spans languages, inspect the actual examples. An **interlinear gloss** aligns words or word parts with analytical labels and then provides an idiomatic translation. Glossing conventions help readers inspect the analysis; they do not make the labels theoretically neutral.[^2]

:::try Put the idea to work
An automatic search finds no examples of a construction in a million-word collection of product reviews. A researcher concludes that speakers cannot produce it. What should be checked before accepting that conclusion?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Check whether the genre supplies opportunities for the construction, whether the search recognizes its variants, and whether the collection represents the relevant speakers. Then test carefully designed examples and contexts with speakers. Zero matches establish absence from those search results, not impossibility in the language.
:::
:::

## Summary

Match the evidence to the question. Corpora reveal sampled use, elicitation probes possibilities, experiments compare conditions, and models expose assumptions. Denominators, sampling, and alternative explanations determine how far the result travels.
