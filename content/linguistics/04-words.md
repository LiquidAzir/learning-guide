## You can understand an invention

Imagine a machine called a *daxer*, designed to dax a surface. Someone reports that it has *redaxed* the floor. Even without knowing what daxing does, you can infer a repeated action and a past-tense form. Familiar word-building patterns have done part of the work.

**Morphology** studies the internal structure of words. A **morpheme** is a minimal unit of grammatical or lexical meaning in an analysis. In *repainted*, *re-*, *paint*, and *-ed* contribute repeat, paint, and past tense. Morphemes are not syllables: *paint* has one syllable, *elephant* has several, and neither syllable count tells you how many meaningful pieces a speaker uses.

## Roots, affixes, and different kinds of change

A **root** contributes a word's lexical core. An **affix** attaches to a base: a prefix before it, a suffix after it, and in some languages an infix within it. The base can itself already contain affixes.

**Derivation** forms a new lexical item, as in *kind* to *kindness*. It can change word class, but does not have to: *paint* and *repaint* are both verbs. **Inflection** supplies a grammatical form of an item, as in *paint* to *painted*. The boundary can be debated for particular constructions; the distinction is useful because word formation and grammatical agreement do different jobs.

Not every change is an added segment. English *sing/sang* changes a vowel. *Sheep* can be singular or plural without an audible suffix. *Go/went* uses a very different stem, a case of **suppletion**. Treating every grammatical meaning as a detachable string would miss these facts.

An **allomorph** is one of the forms used to realize a morpheme. The regular plural endings from [Sounds](#/linguistics/sounds) illustrate predictable sound-conditioned variation. Other alternations require lexical or grammatical information.

## The order of assembly can change the meaning

*Unlockable* can describe a cabinet that cannot be locked or one that can be unlocked. One reading assembles *lockable* first, then negates it: [un-[lock-able]]. The other assembles *unlock* first, then adds ability: [[un-lock]-able]. Context favors one reading, but the repeated letters alone do not display the different structures.

Compounds create another kind of combination. A *coffee cup* is a kind of cup associated with coffee; a *cupboard* is not straightforwardly a kind of board in its current use. Words can become established with meanings that speakers learn as wholes. Their history does not force their present interpretation.

**Productivity** is the extent to which a pattern extends to new forms. A familiar ending can be recognizable without being freely available on every possible base. To investigate productivity, use unfamiliar but plausible forms, compare speakers, and inspect which meanings and contexts support an extension.

### Keep the analysis open to correction

Suppose these forms come from a deliberately invented system:

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| dom | house |
| dom-ka | houses |
| dom-li | in the house |
| dom-ka-li | in the houses |
| sen | garden |
| sen-ka | gardens |

The best simple analysis is *-ka* for plural and *-li* for location inside something. Predict *sen-li* for “in the garden” and *sen-ka-li* for “in the gardens.” The observed order puts plural before location.

That is a hypothesis about these data, not proof that the system has no exceptions. Ask for another noun, a form meaning “from the houses,” and contexts involving several houses. If *-li* also appears for “at the garden,” revise the meaning from a narrow English translation to the range actually supported.

Interlinear glosses would abbreviate these proposed functions as PL and a suitable locative label. Real-language glossing uses conventions for aligning parts and marking boundaries, but segmentation is itself an analytical choice.[^1]
:::

## Languages distribute work differently

One language may express a relationship through an ending where another uses a separate word or word order. That does not establish that one is more logical, more expressive, or easier in every respect. Difficulty depends partly on what the learner already knows and which task is being attempted.

For a practical vocabulary notebook, record the base, its useful forms, and one sentence showing each function. A list of suffix translations without examples makes it too easy to attach a familiar piece where the language does not permit it.

:::try Put the idea to work
In the invented table, can you conclude that *ka-dom* is impossible, or that *sen-ka-li* must occur in actual usage?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Neither follows with certainty. The data support a suffix-order hypothesis and a predicted new form. They do not exhaust the language. Elicit the alternative order and the predicted form in suitable contexts; rejection, a different meaning, or an exception would refine the analysis.
:::
:::

## Summary

Words have reusable structure, but form and meaning are not always in a one-to-one relationship. Compare multiple forms, distinguish inflection from derivation, and test productivity beyond memorized examples.
