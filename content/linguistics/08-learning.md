## Where are the spaces in speech?

Printed words have convenient gaps. Ordinary speech usually does not pause between every word. A child must work out recurring units while also learning sound contrasts, meanings, sentence patterns, and when people take turns.

No single cue solves every case. Repeated sound sequences, stress, familiar names, visible events, and the intentions of other people can all constrain an interpretation. The challenge is to explain how these sources interact over development, rather than replace the problem with the word “absorption.”

## Learning patterns and learning exceptions

Children sometimes extend a regular pattern to an irregular word, producing forms like *goed*. Such a form shows organization beyond direct repetition of the adult target. It does not, by itself, decide whether the learner used a symbolic rule, an analogy, a learned statistical mapping, or some combination.

Researchers can follow spontaneous language over time, ask children to interpret sentences, or test extensions to unfamiliar words. Each method samples a different part of learning. A child who understands a distinction may not yet reliably produce it; a familiar phrase can be produced without full command of its internal structure.

Longitudinal corpora such as those in CHILDES make it possible to revisit actual interactions and compare analyses. Researchers still need to inspect the recording context, transcription, sampling intervals, and what the child had an opportunity to say.[^1]

## The system must also run in real time

The **mental lexicon** is the stored knowledge associated with words and word parts, including forms and meanings. It is a theoretical description of organized knowledge, not necessarily a dictionary-shaped location in the brain.

During comprehension, incoming information changes which interpretations are plausible. A reader can initially assign a phrase one role and then need to revise it. Consider “While Lena photographed the bird escaped.” At *the bird*, a reader may take the bird as the object of *photographed*. At *escaped*, the bird needs to become the subject of the main clause. A comma after *photographed* makes that boundary easier to see.

These **garden-path** effects show that a complete grammatical analysis and the path taken to reach it can differ. Reading times and eye movements help locate difficulty, but a slower region does not uniquely identify the mechanism that caused it.

## Bilingual knowledge is distributed across use

A multilingual person may know workplace vocabulary best in one language and family expressions best in another. Vocabulary in one language alone can therefore give an incomplete picture of what the person knows. Age of acquisition, proficiency, use, schooling, and the task need separate measurement.

**Code-switching** is alternation between languages or varieties within interaction. It can be patterned and purposeful, marking a quotation, a topic shift, or a relationship. It does not automatically demonstrate confusion or a lack of vocabulary. Likewise, being bilingual does not establish a universal advantage on every attention or memory task.

Learning an additional language after an accessible first language differs from having limited access to any language early in life. Signed-language research makes this distinction particularly important. A study of delayed first-language access cannot simply be used to predict an adult's progress in an evening language class.[^2]

### Design a useful personal experiment

Suppose you learn 20 unfamiliar words with pictures. Immediately afterward you recognize 18 from four options. That is evidence of immediate recognition under those conditions, not evidence that you can retrieve all 18 during a conversation next month.

Use three checks: recognition, production without the answer visible, and use in a new sentence or situation. Repeat a subset after a delay. Record which words you can retrieve, not just how familiar they feel when displayed.

To compare two practice methods, give them similar material and time, alternate which word sets receive each method, and use the same delayed test. With one learner and a small list, any difference is a local observation with substantial uncertainty. It can still guide a next attempt without becoming a universal claim about how everyone should study.

The reason to test transfer is concrete: repeating “Where is the station?” perfectly does not show that you can understand an unexpected answer about a replacement bus.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
A bilingual child scores lower on a single-language naming test than a monolingual comparison group. What additional information would help interpret the result?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Measure knowledge in both languages, exposure and use, the familiarity of the items, and the comparison groups' ages and educational contexts. Check comprehension as well as naming. The result describes performance on that test; it does not alone establish a general learning deficit.
:::
:::

## Summary

Acquisition, comprehension, and production are connected but distinguishable. Look for generalization and delayed use, measure language experience explicitly, and avoid treating one task as a complete assessment of a person's language abilities.
