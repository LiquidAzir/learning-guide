## Put a hand in front of your mouth

Say *pin*, then *spin*. In many English accents, the first word begins with a stronger puff of air. The spelling uses the same letter, and speakers usually treat both sounds as instances of the same category. Yet the physical events differ.

**Phonetics** studies how speech is produced, transmitted, and perceived. **Phonology** studies how a language organizes contrasts and patterns. The distinction lets us ask two questions separately: what changed in the signal, and did that change distinguish words?

## Describe the movement before naming the sound

For a consonant, ask where the airflow is shaped, how it is shaped, and what the vocal folds are doing. In a typical English /m/, the lips close, air escapes through the nose, and the vocal folds vibrate. In /s/, a narrow constriction creates turbulent airflow. The International Phonetic Alphabet provides symbols organized around such properties, rather than around the spelling conventions of one language.[^1]

Vowels involve a relatively open vocal tract. Tongue height, tongue position, and lip rounding change its resonances. Pitch and vowel identity are different: you can sing the same vowel on several notes. A **formant** is a resonance-related concentration of acoustic energy, useful in describing vowels; its measured value also depends on the speaker's vocal tract and the recording.

A spectrogram displays how energy at different frequencies changes over time. It is not a picture with a neat boundary around every letter. Articulatory movements overlap: the tongue can begin preparing for the next sound while the current sound is still being produced. This **coarticulation** is part of ordinary fluent speech.

## Which differences do words depend on?

A **minimal pair** contains different words that differ in one sound in the same position. In many English varieties, *sip* and *zip* demonstrate a contrast between /s/ and /z/. Slashes mark a phonemic analysis; square brackets mark phonetic detail. A **phoneme** is a contrastive category within a language, not a universal smallest noise.

The aspirated [pʰ] of *pin* and the less aspirated [p] of *spin* are commonly analyzed as **allophones** of English /p/: context-conditioned realizations of one phoneme. Other languages can use aspiration contrastively. Learning pronunciation therefore involves learning which distinctions listeners use, as well as learning unfamiliar movements.

Sound systems constrain combinations too. English readily permits an initial sequence like /st/ in *stay*, but not every possible combination of its consonants starts an ordinary word. These **phonotactic** patterns help a listener divide the speech stream and help explain why a borrowed word's pronunciation can change.

## Speech has structure above the segment

**Prosody** includes rhythm, stress, phrasing, and intonation. Stress can change which part of an utterance is contrasted: “MAYA borrowed the bicycle” corrects the borrower's identity; “Maya BORROWED the bicycle” can correct the idea that she bought it. Interpretation still depends on context.

In a tone language, pitch patterns can distinguish lexical or grammatical meanings. Intonation also operates across phrases. Having tone does not mean lacking intonation, and speaking without lexical tone does not mean speaking without meaningful pitch.

### One ending, three realizations

For regular English plurals, compare *cats*, *dogs*, and *buses*. A common analysis has [s] after voiceless nonsibilants, [z] after voiced nonsibilants and vowels, and an extra syllable after sibilants such as [s], [z], [ʃ], and [tʃ]. The syllabic ending's vowel varies by accent.

The conditioning sound belongs to the singular stem. *Key* ends in a vowel despite its final letter, so *keys* takes [z]. *Bus* ends in a sibilant, so simply attaching another [s] would not express the ordinary plural pronunciation. The pattern is sensitive to sound, not the letter name.

This connects phonology with the next chapter: plural meaning is one grammatical contribution, but its spoken form varies. Irregular forms such as *children* require additional analysis; the regular pattern never claimed to generate every noun.

*Niffs* takes [s] after voiceless nonsibilant /f/. *Nazzes* takes an additional syllable because /z/ is a sibilant. Both words are invented; the prediction tests a productive English pattern rather than recall of their spelling.

## Summary

Physical differences, contrastive categories, and spelling are separate levels. Describe articulation, test contrasts, and inspect context before declaring two sounds “the same.” Accent differences reveal systematic alternatives in those patterns.
