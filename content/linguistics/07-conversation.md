## “Can you reach the window?”

In one setting, this asks about someone's reach. In another, it politely requests that they open the window. In a third, a climbing instructor is assessing the next move. The sentence's form constrains interpretation, but the situation determines what action is being attempted.

A **speech act** is an action performed through an utterance: requesting, promising, warning, inviting, or asserting. Its success depends on more than grammar. Saying “I promise” in a rehearsal, a quotation, and an actual commitment need not perform the same act.

## Shared information is something people manage

**Common ground** is information participants treat as shared for the conversation. It need not include everything they separately know. You may know the station's exit numbers, but if your friend does not, “meet at the usual exit” is a poor instruction.

Speakers design references around what they believe listeners can identify. A first mention might be “a cyclist wearing a yellow coat”; later, “the cyclist” may be enough. If another cyclist arrives, the shorter description can stop working. Reference is therefore an ongoing coordination problem, not merely a lookup from a noun to an object.

Shared assumptions can also be wrong. Two people can agree to meet “next Friday” while silently selecting different dates. Effective communication often requires making an apparently redundant detail explicit: the calendar date, time zone, address, or version of a document.

## Taking a turn requires prediction

Ordinary conversation often has brief gaps even though planning an answer takes time. Listeners use syntax, prosody, meaning, gaze, and the activity underway to anticipate when a turn could finish. Prediction can begin before every word has arrived, so a late qualification can force a revision.

Overlap is not always interruption. A short *mm-hm* can encourage someone to continue; a simultaneous completion can display agreement; competing talk can attempt to take the floor. The interpretation depends on timing and what happens next. Counting overlapping milliseconds alone does not measure respect or cooperation.

Research using intracranial recordings has compared natural conversation with controlled listening and repetition, finding context-sensitive activity and changes associated with switching between speaking and listening. The clinical sample and differences between tasks limit the conclusions.[^1]

## Repair is part of fluency

**Repair** addresses a problem in speaking, hearing, or understanding. “Tuesday—sorry, Thursday” is self-repair. “Which station?” asks the speaker to repair a reference. “Did you say fifteen or fifty?” offers a narrow choice that can resolve the uncertainty efficiently.

An apparently smooth conversation can conceal a misunderstanding; a repair sequence can prevent one. This matters when judging second-language fluency or a voice assistant. A system that asks one precise clarification may serve the user better than a system that confidently chooses the wrong interpretation.

### Separate the missing pieces

Imagine this exchange:

**A:** Put it beside the other one after lunch.

**B:** The blue case, beside the printer, after today's lunch?

**A:** Yes, but beside the spare printer upstairs.

The first instruction leaves an object, a comparison target, a location, and a time dependent on context. B's response checks three of them. A's repair reveals that *the printer* was still insufficient.

A clearer final instruction is “After lunch today, put the blue case beside the spare printer upstairs.” It is longer, but it removes uncertainty that matters for the task. In an established routine, the short version could be perfectly efficient. Clarity is relative to the information participants actually share.

For a digital assistant, a reasonable policy is to ask about an ambiguity when the likely cost of acting incorrectly exceeds the cost of clarification. That is a design principle, not a formula that reveals a speaker's intention with certainty.
:::

## Directness is not a universal politeness scale

Communities and situations differ in how they use silence, address terms, indirect requests, and disagreement. A direct request can be considerate in an emergency; an elaborate hint can be confusing. Individual preferences and neurodiversity matter alongside community patterns.

Treat a mismatch as a hypothesis to explore. “I understood that as a request—was that what you meant?” is more informative than deciding that a whole population is evasive or rude. Cross-community communication improves when people can negotiate expectations instead of assuming them.

:::try Put the idea to work
A colleague says, “I can review the report on Thursday.” You reply, “Great, I'll send it to the client Thursday morning.” What inference needs checking?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Availability to review on Thursday does not entail completion before Thursday morning. Ask when the review can be finished and agree on the delivery deadline. The misunderstanding comes from coordinating an implied schedule, not from a difficult word.
:::
:::

## Summary

Conversation combines language with shared assumptions, timing, and joint action. Clarification and repair are mechanisms of success. Judge an utterance by what it helps its actual listener understand and do.
