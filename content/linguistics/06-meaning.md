## Some of the lights are on

You hear “Some of the lights are on” and picture a room with other lights off. But the speaker continues, “In fact, all of them are.” That sounds like a correction to an expectation rather than a flat contradiction.

**Semantics** studies conventional linguistic meaning. **Pragmatics** studies how context and communicative reasoning contribute to interpretation. The boundary is debated in places, but the distinction helps explain why a sentence can suggest more than it strictly commits the speaker to.

## Words do not arrive with one universal box

*Bank* can refer to a financial institution or a river edge. Related senses can also grow around a word: the *head* of a person, a table, or an organization. Linguists distinguish **homonymy**, involving separate lexical meanings with the same form, from **polysemy**, involving related senses; the boundary can be difficult to draw.

Reference is another task. *The next train* can pick out different trains depending on the station, direction, and moment. A dictionary entry does not supply those facts. Nor does knowing what *I* means tell you who a particular occurrence refers to without identifying the speaker.

**Compositionality** is the idea that a complex expression's meaning depends systematically on its parts and how they combine. It explains why switching *every* to *some* matters even when all the nouns stay the same. Idioms, context dependence, and ambiguity complicate the analysis; they do not remove the need to explain productive combinations.

## Three kinds of inference

An **entailment** holds when the truth of one claim requires another to be true under the relevant interpretation. “Nora bought a red bicycle” entails “Nora bought a bicycle.” You cannot keep the first true while making the second false in the same ordinary interpretation.

A **conversational implicature** is an inference encouraged by context and expectations about communication. The “some, perhaps not all” reading often arises because a speaker could have made a stronger claim. It can be cancelled: “Some are on; I haven't checked the others.”

A **presupposition** is information an expression tends to treat as background. “Nora stopped cycling to work” ordinarily takes earlier cycling for granted. “Nora didn't stop cycling to work” often retains that background. How such assumptions persist or disappear in larger sentences is a research question, not a rule that every speaker always accepts them.[^1]

## Scope: the same words, different commitments

“Every reviewer read a paper” has a reading under which each reviewer may have read a different paper. It can also be used with a particular paper in mind that everyone read. The relative **scope** of *every* and *a* changes which possibilities satisfy the sentence.

Negation creates similar pitfalls. “Not every door is locked” says that at least one door is not locked. It does not say that every door is unlocked. A useful editing technique is to write down a small situation in which the sentence is true. If it is also true in a situation you intended to exclude, revise the wording.

### Use sets without losing the language

Suppose a building has four doors: A, B, C, and D. Only A and B are locked. Then:

| Statement | True in this situation? |
|---|---|
| Some doors are locked. | Yes |
| Every door is locked. | No |
| Not every door is locked. | Yes |
| No doors are locked. | No |

Under the ordinary inclusive semantic reading, *some* means at least one; its truth does not require an unlocked door. If all four become locked, “some doors are locked” remains true on that reading even though choosing that wording can be misleading in conversation.

This separates two useful questions: “Is the statement true?” and “Does it answer what the listener needed to know?” A warning about building security should usually state which doors remain unlocked. Logical accuracy alone does not guarantee useful communication.
:::

## Translation is an interpretation problem

Languages do not always divide meaning into matching words. A translation may need to express a distinction that the source leaves unspecified, or explain a distinction the target does not normally mark. Researchers compare semantic patterns across languages using several kinds of evidence; patterns in text are only one window on what speakers know.[^2]

When interpreting a claim about language and thought, ask what changed: vocabulary, a task response, a habitual inference, or the ability to entertain an idea at all. Those are substantially different claims.

:::try Put the idea to work
A report says “Not all participants improved.” Can you conclude that most failed to improve, or that nobody improved?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Neither conclusion follows. At least one did not improve, but the sentence does not specify how many. It is compatible with one non-improver or with none improving. Ask for the counts and the definition of improvement before drawing a stronger conclusion.
:::
:::

## Summary

Separate what words conventionally contribute from reference, scope, and contextual inference. Test entailments with possible situations; test implicatures by asking whether they can be cancelled. Clear writing needs both defensible claims and attention to what readers will infer.
