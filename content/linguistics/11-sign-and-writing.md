## A language does not need a voice

Imagine receiving a sentence entirely through visible movement. You still need to identify reusable units, their relationships, what they refer to, and what the signer intends. Changing the channel does not remove grammar.

Natural **signed languages** develop and are used in communities, especially Deaf communities. American Sign Language and British Sign Language are distinct languages; sharing a surrounding spoken language does not make their signs or grammars identical. There is no single universal signed language used by everyone.

## Hands, face, body, and space

Handshape, location, orientation, and movement can contribute to contrasts. The face and body can contribute grammatical and discourse information as well as expression. A video crop that shows only the hands may therefore omit information a signer needs.

The term **phonology** is also used for the organization of signed forms. Here it refers to patterned sublexical structure, not literal sound. The general question is how a language organizes smaller contrastive units into meaningful forms. Its physical realization can be visual and manual.[^1]

Signing space can establish locations associated with referents, then use movements or pointing to track them. The conventions differ by language and construction. It is misleading to describe this either as unrestricted mime or as spoken-language word order performed with the hands.

Some signs have an **iconic** relationship between form and meaning. A motivated resemblance does not make a sign universally understandable: viewers need conventions and context to identify which property is represented. Spoken languages also contain sound symbolism and imitation, while signed languages also contain forms whose meanings must be learned.

## Writing represents language through another system

An **orthography** is a conventional writing system, including spelling practices. An alphabet primarily represents consonant and vowel segments. An abjad primarily represents consonants, with varying ways of indicating vowels. A syllabary uses signs for syllables. These are useful descriptions, not perfectly exclusive boxes for every script.

Chinese writing is often described as morphosyllabic: characters generally correspond to a morpheme and a syllable, with important qualifications. Calling it a collection of pictures for pure ideas obscures its connections to particular languages and pronunciations. Japanese writing combines several systems, showing why a single category may not describe a whole orthography.

Writing can preserve distinctions that pronunciation has lost, or fail to record distinctions that speakers readily hear. Standard spelling therefore cannot be treated as a transparent transcript. **Transliteration** maps between writing systems; **translation** conveys meaning between languages. Neither guarantees the other.

### Follow a message without confusing its layers

Take the English message “Meet me at eight.” Its ordinary spelling is an orthographic representation. A phonetic transcription would instead describe a chosen pronunciation, including accent-specific details. A translation into another language would need expressions for the intended request, participants, and time.

Now imagine an ASL video conveying the intended meeting arrangement. An English gloss placed beneath each sign would be an analyst's label, not an English sentence that the signer literally produced. Timing, spatial reference, and nonmanual information can require additional annotation. The original video remains evidence that a short gloss cannot fully replace.

To evaluate an automatic caption or translation, ask which layer is being assessed. Correct written words can still be mistimed; correct sign labels can still lose a grammatical facial signal; a fluent translation can still choose the wrong meeting time. Preserve the context needed to resolve the message.

This is also why a text-only resource cannot teach accurate signing by itself. Link to well-documented video materials and learn from qualified signers; use textual analysis to explain what to notice.
:::

## Access changes the learning question

Early access to a language and later study of an additional language are distinct developmental situations. Research comparing signers with different acquisition histories must describe what language access they had, rather than treating their age of first ASL exposure as an isolated variable. The 2025 study linked in the research feed compares real signs with invented sign-like forms to investigate that distinction at the level of production.[^2]

For technology, involve the intended language community in choosing tasks and evaluating outputs. A system trained on a small set of rehearsed signs should not be described as a general interpreter for conversations, regional variation, and specialized settings.

A demonstration labels isolated signs correctly from a hand-only video. What has it not yet shown about translating a full signed conversation?

It has not shown that it handles sentence structure, facial and body signals, spatial reference, continuous transitions, turn-taking, or unfamiliar signers and settings. Recognition of the demonstrated signs is a useful component result, but conversation translation requires additional evidence.

## Summary

Language structure is not confined to sound, and writing is not language itself. Distinguish signed languages, annotation, spelling, transcription, transliteration, and translation. Each preserves some information and can omit other information essential to understanding.
