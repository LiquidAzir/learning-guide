## Diagnose the problem before reaching for a rule

| What you notice | What to investigate | Return to |
|---|---|---|
| Two pronunciations seem different | Is the difference contrastive, predictable from context, or variable? | [Sounds](#/linguistics/sounds) |
| A new word is understandable | Which base and word-building pattern support the interpretation? | [Words](#/linguistics/words) |
| A sentence permits two readings | Does grouping, attachment, or scope change? | [Sentences](#/linguistics/sentences) and [Meaning](#/linguistics/meaning) |
| Someone inferred more than you said | Which contextual assumption or implicature supplied the extra content? | [Conversation](#/linguistics/conversation) |
| A learner knows a word but cannot use it | Does the task require recognition, retrieval, or use in an unfamiliar context? | [Learning](#/linguistics/learning) |
| A variety receives negative judgments | Are you measuring a linguistic pattern, an attitude, or a task outcome? | [Variation](#/linguistics/variation) |
| Two languages have similar words | Do many inherited forms show recurring correspondences? | [Change](#/linguistics/change) |
| A translation sounds excellent | Did it preserve reference, quantities, negation, and uncertainty? | [Frontier](#/linguistics/frontier) |

## A notebook for learning another language

Record an expression with a situation in which a speaker used it. Add the pronunciation or a source recording when available, the relevant word forms, and the meaning in that setting. Note a contrasting example: what changes when there are several objects, the event already happened, or the request becomes more formal?

Separate “I recognize this” from “I can retrieve this” and “I can use this in a new exchange.” Revisit examples after a delay. When asking a speaker for feedback, make the intended meaning clear; a correction without a context may fix a different problem from the one you intended to investigate.

For signed languages, use appropriate video resources and instruction. A written English gloss is a label for analysis, not a full record of the signed form. For any community's materials, respect restrictions on recording, redistribution, and use.

## An editing pass for consequential messages

Identify who must act, what they must do, when, and under which conditions. Replace unresolved pronouns with names where necessary. Inspect *only*, *all*, *some*, *unless*, and negation: moving one can change the commitment. Check whether a modifier can attach to more than one phrase.

Then read the message as someone who lacks your background knowledge. A calendar date may communicate more clearly than *next Friday*. A defined deadline may be better than *soon*. Keep qualifications that affect the decision; remove repeated wording that does not add information. Finally, check the reader's actual understanding when the task warrants it.

## Read a research entry in five passes

1. Identify the question and the tested alternatives.
2. Identify the languages, varieties, participants, or datasets sampled.
3. Identify the task and measured outcome.
4. Compare the finding with the strongest claim the design supports.
5. Inspect limitations, materials, and whether the result transfers to your intended use.

For a formal or computational study, replace “participants” with the assumptions, data, and baselines actually used. For a language model, record the version and evaluation setup. For a brain study, distinguish association with a linguistic feature from evidence that a specific neural process is necessary for that feature.

## Resources with different jobs

**International Phonetic Association.** The official IPA chart organizes speech symbols and provides reference materials. Use it to connect a symbol with its articulatory description, not as a universal spelling system.[^1]

**Leipzig Glossing Rules.** Conventions for aligning language examples with morpheme-level annotations. Use them to read what an analysis claims each piece contributes.[^2]

**World Atlas of Language Structures.** Feature maps and explanatory chapters for cross-language comparisons. Inspect the definition and sample for each feature rather than assuming every map has the same coverage.[^3]

**Glottolog.** Genealogical classifications and references to descriptive work. Use it to trace the literature behind a classification or find documentation for a language.[^4]

**CHILDES.** Recordings, transcripts, and tools for research on language development. Start with the corpus documentation and access conditions.[^5]

**ACL Anthology.** Papers on computational linguistics and language technology, including methods and evaluation materials. A published benchmark is evidence about its tested task, not automatic validation for every application.[^6]

The [Keep Current feed](#/linguistics/research) supplies short explainers and links back to relevant chapters. The publication date belongs to the source; the date an entry was added to this guide is a separate fact.
