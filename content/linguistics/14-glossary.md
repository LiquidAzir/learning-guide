## Evidence and analysis

**Linguistics.** The systematic study of language structure, use, acquisition, processing, and change.

**Corpus.** A collected body of language data, such as texts, transcripts, or recordings; plural: corpora.

**Elicitation.** Obtaining examples or judgments from speakers or signers in a designed context.

**Acceptability.** A person's judgment of an expression in a setting, influenced by structure, meaning, memory, and context.

**Grammaticality.** Whether an expression fits a language's structural system under a particular analysis; distinct from clarity, truth, or social approval.

**Interlinear gloss.** An aligned annotation explaining words or word parts, usually accompanied by an idiomatic translation.

**Generalization.** Extension of a pattern or result beyond the examples from which it was learned or measured.

## Sounds and forms

**Phonetics.** Study of the production, transmission, and perception of speech; also used more broadly for the physical realization of linguistic forms.

**Phonology.** Organization of contrastive form and patterns in a language, including signed languages.

**Phoneme.** A contrastive category of sounds in a phonological analysis of a spoken language.

**Allophone.** A realization of a phoneme, often conditioned by its phonetic environment.

**Minimal pair.** Two words with different meanings that differ in one sound in the same position; analogous contrasts can be studied in signed forms.

**Aspiration.** A period of breathy airflow, often following the release of a stop consonant before voicing begins.

**Formant.** A resonance-related concentration of acoustic energy used in analyzing speech, especially vowels.

**Coarticulation.** Overlap or interaction among articulatory movements for neighboring sounds.

**Phonotactics.** A language's patterns and restrictions on possible combinations of sublexical units.

**Prosody.** Organization involving rhythm, stress, phrasing, intonation, and related properties above individual segments; signed languages have visual prosodic cues.

**Tone.** Contrastive pitch patterns used to distinguish lexical or grammatical meanings.

**Intonation.** Pitch organization across an utterance, contributing to phrasing and interpretation.

## Word and sentence structure

**Morphology.** Study of the internal structure and grammatical forms of words.

**Morpheme.** A minimal unit of lexical or grammatical meaning in a morphological analysis.

**Root.** A word's core lexical component, to which other material may attach.

**Affix.** A bound element attached to a base, such as a prefix or suffix.

**Derivation.** Formation of a new lexical item, sometimes with a change in word class.

**Inflection.** Formation of a grammatical variant of a lexical item, such as a tense or number form.

**Allomorph.** One form used to realize a morpheme.

**Suppletion.** Use of substantially different stems within a grammatical paradigm, as in go/went.

**Productivity.** The extent to which a pattern extends to new expressions rather than being limited to stored items.

**Syntax.** Study of how words combine into phrases, clauses, and sentences.

**Constituent.** A group functioning as a structural unit in a syntactic analysis.

**Head.** The central element around which a phrase or dependency structure is organized.

**Dependency.** A grammatical relation between a head and a dependent element.

**Argument.** A participant or complement selected by a predicate or construction.

**Adjunct.** A modifying expression not selected as an argument in the relevant analysis.

**Subject.** A grammatical relation identified through language-specific properties; it need not be the agent.

**Agent.** A participant responsible for initiating an event in a semantic analysis.

**Embedding.** Inclusion of a structure inside another structure, such as a clause inside a larger clause. In machine learning, the same word names a numerical representation; context distinguishes the senses.

## Meaning and interaction

**Semantics.** Study of conventional linguistic meaning and systematic meaning relations.

**Pragmatics.** Study of interpretation and action through language in context.

**Compositionality.** The principle that complex meanings depend systematically on component meanings and their combination.

**Homonymy.** Distinct lexical meanings sharing a form.

**Polysemy.** Multiple related senses of a lexical item.

**Reference.** Use of an expression to identify an entity, event, or other referent in a context.

**Entailment.** A relation in which one claim's truth requires another's under the relevant interpretation.

**Implicature.** An inferred meaning supported by communicative context; conversational implicatures can often be cancelled.

**Presupposition.** Information an expression tends to present as background, often persisting under negation or questioning.

**Scope.** The part of an interpretation over which an operator such as negation or a quantifier takes effect.

**Speech act.** An action performed through an utterance, such as requesting or promising.

**Common ground.** Information participants treat as shared for an interaction.

**Repair.** A conversational process for addressing trouble in speaking, hearing, or understanding.

## Learning, variation, and history

**Mental lexicon.** Organized knowledge of lexical forms, meanings, and associated properties.

**Garden path.** A processing difficulty caused by an initial interpretation that later material requires a reader or listener to revise.

**Code-switching.** Alternation between languages or varieties within interaction.

**Accent.** A pattern of pronunciation associated with a speaker or group.

**Dialect.** A language variety with potential differences in pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar.

**Register.** A pattern of language associated with an activity, setting, or communicative purpose.

**Standard variety.** A variety promoted through institutions and conventions; its status does not make other varieties structurally deficient.

**Cognate.** A form related to a form in another language through inheritance from a common ancestor.

**Comparative method.** Historical reconstruction using recurring correspondences among related languages.

**Grammaticalization.** Development of grammatical functions from other expressions, often accompanied by changes in form and distribution.

**Analogy.** Extension or reshaping based on an existing linguistic pattern.

**Language shift.** A change in which language a community uses across settings or generations.

**Revitalization.** Efforts to support renewed language use according to a community's goals.

## Media and technology

**Signed language.** A natural language whose primary expression is visual and manual, with its own structure and community history.

**Iconicity.** A motivated resemblance between aspects of a form and aspects of its meaning; interpretation still depends on conventions.

**Orthography.** A conventional writing system and its spelling practices.

**Transcription.** A representation of a recorded or observed linguistic signal at a chosen level of detail.

**Transliteration.** Representation of written forms using another writing system.

**Translation.** Conveying meaning from one language into another, with attention to context and purpose.

**Token.** In a corpus, an occurrence of a unit; in a language model, a unit defined by its tokenizer. Neither use automatically means a whole word.

**Surprisal.** The negative logarithm of an event's probability under a specified model; with base 2, measured in bits.

**Low-resource language.** A language with limited resources for a specified technological or research task; not a claim about linguistic richness.

**Benchmark.** A defined dataset and evaluation procedure for comparing performance on specified tasks.
