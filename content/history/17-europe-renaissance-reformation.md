## Recap

Chapter 16 ended with the Church discredited, the press invented, and Europe's population recovering. This chapter is about the explosion those three things produced: the Protestant Reformation, the Catholic response, and the wars of religion that followed. It is also about the states that grew strong in the fighting, because the sixteenth century is when the monarchies of Spain, France, and England became the powers that would dominate the next three.

## Ninety-five theses

On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor of theology at the small new University of Wittenberg in Saxony, sent his archbishop a list of ninety-five propositions for debate about **indulgences**, the certificates sold by the Church that remitted punishment for sin. A Dominican named Tetzel had been selling them in the region with the slogan that a coin in the box sprang a soul from purgatory, to fund the rebuilding of St Peter's in Rome and to pay off the archbishop's debts. Luther's objection was theological before it was moral: he had come to believe, from reading Paul, that salvation was by God's grace through faith alone, that no human act, purchase, or priest could earn it, and that the whole apparatus of penance and merit on which the Church's wealth rested was a fraud on the faithful.[^1] Whether he nailed the theses to the church door is uncertain; that they were in print across Germany within weeks is not. Print made Luther, and he knew it: he wrote in German, in short cheap pamphlets, at a rate of one every two weeks for the rest of his life, and in the early 1520s roughly a fifth of all pamphlets printed in Germany, and a larger share of those in German, were his.

The Church did what it had done to Hus: it summoned him, demanded recantation, and threatened excommunication in a papal bull (1520). Luther burned the bull, and was excommunicated in January 1521. At the imperial Diet at Worms that year, before the young emperor Charles V, he refused to recant unless shown his error from Scripture, and his prince, the Elector of Saxony, hid him. In hiding he translated the New Testament into German in eleven weeks, a translation that shaped the German language as the King James Bible would shape English. From 1520 he attacked the papacy itself as the Antichrist, the sacraments (he kept two of seven), clerical celibacy (he married a former nun), and monasticism, and he insisted that every Christian could read Scripture for himself. That last principle produced, within a decade, Christians reading it differently from Luther, and Protestantism was plural from the start.[^2]

## Why it spread

Luther was not the first to say these things; Wycliffe and Hus had said most of them. Why did it work this time? Print, above all: the Church could burn a man but not 300,000 pamphlets. Princes: the Reformation let a ruler confiscate the Church's lands, which were a third of some territories, appoint his own bishops, and stop the flow of money to Rome; German princes, Scandinavian kings, and Henry VIII of England all took the opportunity, and the Reformation succeeded where the state adopted it and failed where it did not.[^3] Cities: the imperial free cities of Germany and Switzerland, self-governing and literate, went Protestant early. Nationalism, before the word: Germans resented Italian popes. And grievance: the Church's wealth and the clergy's failings were universally deplored, and Luther gave the deploring a theology.

The Reformation split. Ulrich Zwingli in Zurich denied Christ's bodily presence in the bread of the Eucharist, which Luther held; the two could not agree, and Protestantism divided over what a sacrament was. The **Anabaptists** rejected infant baptism, the state church, oaths, and war, and were drowned and burned by Catholics and Protestants alike; their heirs are the Mennonites and Amish. In 1524–1525 German peasants, taking Luther's freedom in a sense he had not meant, rose in the largest popular revolt in European history before 1789; Luther told the princes to slay them like mad dogs, and perhaps 100,000 died.[^4] **John Calvin**, a French lawyer, made Geneva from 1541 the model of a reformed city: a church governed by elders, a doctrine of predestination in which God had chosen the saved before time, a discipline that policed morals, and a theology in the *Institutes* (1536, revised to 1559) that was the most systematic Protestantism produced. Calvinism spread to Scotland, the Netherlands, France (the Huguenots), Hungary, and, through the Puritans, England and New England, and its association with hard work, literacy, and republicanism has been argued over since Max Weber.[^5]

## England

Henry VIII's Reformation was about an heir. Married to Catherine of Aragon, who had given him a daughter and no living son, he wanted the marriage annulled to marry Anne Boleyn; the pope, whose master Charles V was Catherine's nephew, refused. Parliament, between 1529 and 1536, declared the king supreme head of the Church of England, ended payments to Rome, and dissolved the monasteries, whose lands, a quarter of England, went to the crown and its supporters.[^6] Henry kept Catholic doctrine and burned Protestants; his son Edward VI made England Protestant; his daughter Mary made it Catholic and burned 280 Protestants; his daughter Elizabeth (1558–1603) settled on a Protestant church with Catholic-looking bishops and ceremonies and executed Catholics for treason rather than heresy. The English Reformation was the state's, and the English church has been ambiguous ever since.

## The Catholic response

Rome reformed too, late and thoroughly. The **Council of Trent** (1545–1563) reaffirmed every doctrine Luther had attacked, ended the worst abuses (indulgence-selling, absentee bishops, uneducated priests), required seminaries, and standardized the Mass in a form that lasted, essentially unchanged, until the reforms after the Second Vatican Council (1969–1970).[^7] New orders carried the fight: above all the **Jesuits**, founded by Ignatius Loyola in 1540, disciplined, educated, obedient to the pope, who ran the best schools in Europe, reconverted Poland and much of Germany, and took Catholicism to India, Japan, China (chapter 31), and the Americas. The Inquisition was revived in Spain and Rome; the Index of Forbidden Books was created in 1559. By 1600 the religious map of Europe was roughly what it is today: the north Protestant, the south Catholic, Germany divided, France Catholic with a Protestant minority, and the line drawn mostly by which side the ruler took.

## The wars

Religion made war absolute. Charles V fought the German Protestant princes to a draw and conceded, in the Peace of Augsburg (1555), that each prince could choose his territory's religion. France fell into thirty years of civil war (1562–1598) between Catholics and Huguenots, with the massacre of some 3,000 Protestants in Paris on St Bartholomew's Day 1572 and tens of thousands across France; it ended when the Protestant heir Henry IV converted to Catholicism ("Paris is worth a Mass") and granted the Huguenots toleration by the Edict of Nantes (1598).[^8] The Netherlands, prosperous and with a militant Calvinist minority, revolted against Spanish rule in 1568 and fought for eighty years, producing the Dutch Republic, Europe's first modern commercial state. Philip II of Spain sent the Armada against Protestant England in 1588 and lost it to weather and English gunnery. The worst was to come: the Thirty Years' War (chapter 19) would kill perhaps a fifth of Germany.

:::story A miller's heresy
In 1584 the Inquisition in Friuli, in northeastern Italy, arrested a miller named Menocchio for heresy. He could read; he had read a vernacular Bible, a travel book, a chronicle, and possibly, the inquisitors suspected, the Koran. From these and from his own head he had built a cosmology: the world had come from chaos like cheese from milk, and the angels were the worms in it; all religions were equally valid; the Church's sacraments were merchandise; God was in everything. He argued with the inquisitors for years, lucidly and at length, and they burned him in 1599. His trial records, discovered in the 1970s by Carlo Ginzburg, show what the Reformation looked like from below: not Luther's doctrine but a peasant culture that had gained the Bible and lost its deference, thinking for itself in ways no reformer intended and no church could allow.[^9] For every Luther there were thousands of Menocchios, and the Inquisition's files are where we find them.
:::

## The states

While Christendom split, its states grew. **Spain**, united by the marriage of Ferdinand and Isabella (1469), conqueror of Granada (1492) and the Americas (chapter 18), inherited by Charles V with Austria, the Netherlands, and the imperial title, was the first European superpower, with American silver paying for armies that fought everywhere; under Philip II (1556–1598) it was the champion of Catholicism and, by his death, exhausted and bankrupt four times over.[^10] **France**, recovered from the Hundred Years' War, built the largest army in Europe and, under Francis I, a Renaissance court; the wars of religion nearly destroyed it and Henry IV rebuilt it. **England** under Elizabeth became a Protestant naval power and, in Shakespeare's generation, produced a national literature. The **Ottoman Empire**, which had taken Constantinople in 1453, conquered Hungary in 1526 and besieged Vienna in 1529; it was the largest power in Europe and its threat is the reason Charles V could not crush the Lutherans.

These were **new monarchies**: kings who taxed regularly, kept standing armies, ran professional bureaucracies, controlled their churches, and reduced their nobles to courtiers. The theory caught up with the practice. Machiavelli described power without morals; Jean Bodin, watching France's wars, argued in 1576 that a state needed a single sovereign whose word was law, above the churches and the factions; the wars of religion made **sovereignty** the answer to the question of who decides when religions disagree.[^11]

:::key
The Reformation began as an argument about how sinners are saved and became, within a generation, the largest redistribution of property since the fall of Rome, the creation of the modern state's control over religion, a century of war, and the permanent division of Western Christianity. It succeeded because of print and princes: print made Luther unsilenceable, and princes found in his theology a reason to take what the Church owned. Its unintended consequences, literacy for Bible-reading, the plurality of churches, the exhaustion that eventually produced toleration, and the sovereign state that ended the wars, are the ones that made the modern world.
:::

## Summary

- Luther's 1517 attack on indulgences rested on a theology of salvation by faith alone; print made him unsilenceable and in the early 1520s a fifth of German pamphlets were his.
- The Reformation succeeded where princes adopted it, for the Church's lands and independence from Rome; it split at once into Lutheran, Reformed (Zwingli, Calvin), Anabaptist, and Anglican forms.
- Rome reformed at Trent, founded the Jesuits, and reconverted much of Europe; the religious map of 1600 is roughly today's.
- Religion made war total: the German conflicts, the French Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt, and the Armada.
- Spain, France, and England became new monarchies with regular taxes, standing armies, and controlled churches; sovereignty became the answer to religious division.
