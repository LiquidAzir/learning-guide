## Recap

Chapter 4 left the Indus cities dispersing into villages around 1900 BCE. This chapter picks up the subcontinent from there: the arrival of the Indo-Aryan languages, the composition of the Vedas, the rise of cities and kingdoms in the Ganges valley, the two religions born there in one generation, and the two empires, Mauryan and Gupta, that briefly unified most of the land. India's ancient history is harder to date than Rome's, because its literature is vast and its chronicles few, and this chapter says so where it matters.

## The Vedic age

Between about 1500 and 1000 BCE, speakers of an Indo-European language, Sanskrit, entered northwestern India from the direction of Central Asia. Whether they came as invaders, migrants, or in a slow trickle is one of the most politically charged questions in Indian history, and the evidence, mainly linguistic and, since 2019, genetic, favors migration by pastoralist groups related to those who spread Indo-European languages across Europe and Iran (chapter 2), mixing over centuries with the far larger existing population.[^1]

They composed the **Vedas**, four collections of hymns, ritual formulas, and speculations, transmitted orally with such precision that the *Rigveda*, some 1,028 hymns to gods of storm, fire, and sky, is generally dated to around 1500–1200 BCE, older than any Greek text, though it was not written down for well over a thousand years.[^2] Vedic society was pastoral and tribal, organized by lineage, fond of cattle, horses, chariots, and a sacrificial religion in which priests (**brahmins**) fed the gods through fire. It moved east into the Ganges valley over the first millennium, took up rice farming and iron, and by 600 BCE had produced cities, kingdoms, coinage, and the four-fold **varna** order that ranks society as priests, warriors, producers, and servants, with a fifth category outside it altogether. This is the frame that would harden, over many centuries and with much regional variation, into the **caste** system of thousands of endogamous (in-marrying) groups (*jatis*), and the question of how old, how rigid, and how much a British colonial artifact caste was is another live and political argument.[^3]

The late Vedic texts called the **Upanishads** (c. 800–500 BCE) turned from ritual to philosophy: what is the self, what is the world, what survives death? Their answers, that the individual self (*atman*) is identical with the universal ground of being (*brahman*), that action (*karma*) determines rebirth, and that release (*moksha*) from the cycle is the goal, became the core of what would later be called Hinduism.[^4]

## The Buddha and the Jina

Around 500 BCE, in the small republics and kingdoms of the eastern Ganges, two men founded religions that rejected the Vedic sacrifice and the brahmins' authority and offered release to anyone. **Mahavira**, the Jina ("conqueror"), taught the Jain path of total non-violence, asceticism, and the liberation of the soul from matter. **Siddhartha Gautama**, the **Buddha** ("awakened one"), a prince from the Shakya clan who left his family at twenty-nine, taught for forty-five years, and died around 400 BCE (the traditional date of 483 is now thought too early), taught the Middle Way.[^5]

The Buddha's teaching, in its earliest recoverable form: existence is marked by suffering (*dukkha*, better "unsatisfactoriness"); suffering arises from craving; craving can be ended; the way to end it is an eightfold path of right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration. There is no permanent self; what we call a person is a stream of changing processes, and seeing this is liberation, **nirvana**, the extinguishing of the fire.[^6] He accepted karma and rebirth from his culture and denied that caste mattered to them. He founded an order of monks and, after some hesitation, nuns, dependent on lay support, and he refused to answer metaphysical questions about the origin of the world or the fate of the enlightened after death as unhelpful to the task at hand.

Buddhism spread slowly for two centuries and then, with the Mauryan empire, explosively. It would become the first world religion, carried by monks and merchants to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, China (chapter 28), Korea, Japan, and Southeast Asia, and it would nearly vanish from India itself after about 1200. Jainism stayed in India and is there still, some four to five million strong, with an influence on Indian ethics (and on Gandhi) out of all proportion to its numbers.

## The Mauryan empire and Ashoka

Alexander reached the Indus in 326 and left; within a few years a young adventurer named Chandragupta Maurya had seized the kingdom of Magadha in the Ganges valley and expanded it to cover most of northern India, defeating Alexander's successor Seleucus in the northwest and receiving a Greek ambassador, Megasthenes, whose account of India is our first outside description.[^7] The **Mauryan** state was the first to rule most of the subcontinent, from a capital at Pataliputra (modern Patna) that Megasthenes said was the largest city he knew, through a bureaucracy that the *Arthashastra*, a manual of statecraft attributed to Chandragupta's minister Kautilya, describes in terms that make Machiavelli look sentimental: spies everywhere, taxation of everything, the king's interest as the only measure.[^8]

Chandragupta's grandson **Ashoka** (reigned c. 268–232 BCE) is the reason the Mauryas are remembered. Around 260 he conquered Kalinga on the east coast in a war that, by his own account, killed 100,000 and deported 150,000. Then he had a change of heart, converted to Buddhism, and spent the rest of his reign publishing edicts, carved on rocks and polished stone pillars across the empire, in the local languages and scripts (Prakrit in Brahmi and Kharoshthi; Greek and Aramaic in the northwest), in which he expressed remorse for Kalinga, renounced aggressive war, urged his subjects to respect all sects and their parents and to treat animals kindly, described the wells he had dug and the hospitals he had built, and reported sending envoys to the Hellenistic kings.[^9] No other ruler in antiquity apologized in stone. The edicts were unreadable for two thousand years until James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in 1837, and the "Piyadasi, beloved of the gods" who wrote them was identified with the Ashoka of Buddhist legend only later. Ashoka's lion capital from Sarnath is India's national emblem and his wheel is on its flag.

The empire fell apart within fifty years of his death, and northern India returned to competing kingdoms and to invaders through the northwest passes: Greeks, Scythians (Shakas), Parthians, and the **Kushans**, a Central Asian people whose empire (first to third centuries CE) linked India to the Silk Road and under whose king Kanishka Buddhism took the form, called **Mahayana** ("great vehicle"), that would conquer East Asia, with its compassionate bodhisattvas, its celestial Buddhas, and the first images of the Buddha in human form, in the Greek-influenced art of Gandhara (chapter 7) and, independently, at Mathura.[^10]

## The Gupta age

The **Gupta** dynasty (c. 320–550 CE) reunified northern India from Magadha and presided over what later historians called a golden age, a phrase to handle with care. What is solid: Sanskrit literature reached its height in the plays and poems of Kalidasa; the great epics, the *Mahabharata* (some 100,000 verses, the longest poem in the world, containing the *Bhagavad Gita*) and the *Ramayana*, took their final form; Hindu temple architecture in stone began; the *Kama Sutra* and the law code of Manu, composed in the preceding centuries, became authoritative; the Chinese pilgrim Faxian traveled through and reported a prosperous, orderly, and tolerant land where Buddhism and Hinduism flourished side by side.[^11]

And **mathematics**. Indian mathematicians of this period developed the decimal place-value system with a symbol for zero, the system in which every number on Earth is now written. The earliest secure dated zero as a placeholder appears in a Cambodian inscription of 683 CE; the concept is at work in the Indian astronomer Aryabhata's treatise of 499, which also gives pi to four decimal places and proposes that the Earth rotates.[^12] Brahmagupta in 628 gave rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. Arab scholars adopted the system in the eighth century, Europe learned it from them (which is why the numerals are called Arabic), and it replaced Roman numerals over the next seven hundred years. It is the most important Indian contribution to world civilization and possibly the most important mathematical idea anyone ever had ([chapter 2 of the mathematics guide](#/mathematics/arithmetic)).

The Guptas fell to the Huns from Central Asia in the sixth century, and India returned to regional kingdoms for six hundred years. What the ancient period left, though, was durable: the religions (Hinduism as a synthesis of Vedic and local traditions, Buddhism, Jainism), the social order, Sanskrit as the learned language of a subcontinent, the epics that every Indian child still knows, and the numbers.

:::history How we got here
Ancient Indian history was largely reconstructed by British colonial scholars from 1784 onward, and it carried their assumptions: that India was timeless and unchanging, that its history was one of successive invasions, that caste was ancient and fixed, that the Vedic Aryans were a superior race kin to Europeans. Every one of these has been challenged since independence. The "Aryan invasion" became a migration and then, for some Indian nationalists, a colonial fiction to be denied altogether; the genetic evidence since 2019 confirms a migration from the steppe, which satisfies no one completely. Caste was shown to have been made more rigid by the colonial census, which fixed categories that had been fluid; how fluid remains argued. "Golden age" and "dark age" labels were shown to track the historian's politics as much as the evidence. Ancient India is the clearest case in this guide of history written by outsiders, rewritten by insiders, and contested by both, and the reader should treat confident claims about it, from any direction, with more than usual care.[^13]
:::

## Summary

- Indo-Aryan speakers entered India around 1500 BCE and composed the Vedas orally; Vedic society moved into the Ganges valley, took up rice and iron, and produced cities, kingdoms, the varna order, and, in the Upanishads, the ideas of karma, rebirth, and release.
- The Buddha and Mahavira, around 500 BCE, founded religions open to all that rejected the brahmins' sacrifice; Buddhism became the first world religion.
- The Mauryas built the first pan-Indian empire; Ashoka, after a bloody conquest, converted to Buddhism and carved his remorse and his ethics on rocks across the land.
- The Kushans linked India to the Silk Road and Mahayana Buddhism took shape; the Guptas presided over Sanskrit's classical age and the invention of decimal place-value notation with zero.
- Ancient Indian history was written by colonial scholars, rewritten after independence, and remains contested on migration, caste, and chronology.
