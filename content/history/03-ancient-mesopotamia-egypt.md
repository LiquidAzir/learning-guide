## Recap

Chapter 2 ended with farmers everywhere and cities nowhere. This chapter is about the two river valleys where cities, states, and writing appeared first, within a few centuries of each other, and about how differently they did it. Everything in the Ancient section descends from one or both.

## A complaint about copper

Around 1750 BCE a man named Nanni, in the city of Ur in southern Iraq, dictated a letter to a merchant named Ea-nasir. He was furious. He had paid for copper ingots of good quality; Ea-nasir had delivered bad ones, treated Nanni's messenger with contempt, and told him to take the copper or leave it. "What do you take me for," Nanni asks, "that you treat somebody like me with such contempt?" He demands his money back. The letter, pressed into a clay tablet in wedge-shaped signs and baked hard, was found in Ea-nasir's house, along with other complaints. It is one of hundreds of thousands of such tablets, and it is why we know Mesopotamia better than any other early civilization: its people wrote on the one material that lasts forever, and they wrote about everything.[^1]

## Sumer: the land between the rivers

**Mesopotamia**, Greek for "between the rivers," is the plain of the Tigris and Euphrates in modern Iraq. Its southern part, **Sumer**, is a flat, hot, treeless floodplain with almost no rain, no stone, no metal, and no timber. It has two things: silt, which the rivers deliver and which grows enormous crops of barley if watered, and the rivers themselves, which flood unpredictably and in the wrong season. To farm it you must dig canals, and to dig canals you must organize thousands of people. That, in one sentence, is the standard theory of why cities and states appeared here first, and while it has been complicated, it has not been replaced.[^2]

By about 3200 BCE the settlement of **Uruk** held perhaps 40,000 people, with monumental temples on raised platforms, mass-produced pottery, and craft specialists.[^3] It was the largest city in the world and, as far as we know, the first. Within a few centuries a dozen such cities dotted Sumer: Ur, Lagash, Kish, Nippur, Eridu. Each was a **city-state**, ruling the farmland around it, with a temple to its patron god at the center and, increasingly, a king (**lugal**, "big man") beside the temple. They fought each other over water and boundaries for a thousand years.

## The invention of writing

Writing was not invented to record poetry or history. It was invented to keep accounts. The temples of Uruk received grain, sheep, beer, and labor from thousands of people and had to track it; by 3300 BCE they were pressing pictures and number signs into clay tablets: so many sheep, so many jars of oil, received from so-and-so.[^4] Over the next five centuries the pictures became stylized wedges made with a cut reed (**cuneiform**, from Latin *cuneus*, wedge), the signs came to stand for sounds as well as things, and a system for counting sheep became one that could write anything in the Sumerian language, and then in others.

The earliest literature followed the earliest accounting by about seven hundred years. Around 2600 BCE Sumerians were writing hymns, proverbs, and king lists; by 2100 the tales of Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, were being composed, and by 1200 they had been shaped into the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the oldest long poem that survives, in which a king who has lost his friend to death travels to the end of the world to find immortality and is told, by the one man who has it, to go home and live.[^5] The flood story in its eleventh tablet, in which a god warns one man to build a boat and load it with animals, was written down a thousand years before the book of Genesis.

Egypt developed **hieroglyphs** at almost the same moment, around 3200 BCE, possibly on hearing that writing was possible; the systems are unrelated in form. China invented writing independently around 1200 BCE (chapter 26), and Mesoamerica around 600 BCE (chapter 11). Nearly every other script in the world descends from one of these four.

:::howto Reading a king's inscription
Rulers wrote most of what survives from antiquity, and they wrote it for the gods and posterity. The genre has rules.
1. **Every king is victorious.** Defeats are omitted or recast. When two neighboring kings each claim to have won the same battle (as the Egyptian and Hittite kings did over Kadesh in 1274 BCE), the historian concludes it was a draw.
2. **Every king is pious.** Temple-building and offerings are recorded in loving detail; they are the king's case for his own legitimacy.
3. **Numbers are round and large.** "I killed 100,000 of the enemy" means "many." Casualty figures in ancient sources are almost never usable as counts.
4. **The gods are the audience.** A stele buried in a temple foundation was never meant to be read by a human; it is a report to the divine landlord.
5. **What is useful anyway**: the king's name and titles (which tell you what he claimed to rule), the places named (geography), the gods invoked (religion), the year formula (chronology), and whatever the text takes for granted about how the world works.
:::

## Kings, laws, and empire

Around 2334 BCE a man of obscure origin named Sargon seized power in Kish, conquered every Sumerian city, and pushed his rule from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean: the first **empire**, meaning a state ruling many peoples and cities under one center.[^6] His dynasty, the Akkadians, lasted about 180 years and collapsed, in the Sumerian account, because of divine anger and, in the climate record, at the same time as a severe drought around 2200 BCE that also coincided with the fall of Egypt's Old Kingdom and the decline of the Indus cities.[^7] Empires would rise and fall in Mesopotamia for the next two thousand years: Ur's Third Dynasty, Babylon, Assyria, Babylon again, Persia. The pattern of a river-valley heartland conquered by outsiders who adopt its culture, and are conquered in turn, is the region's history.

The most famous Mesopotamian king is Hammurabi of Babylon (reigned c. 1792–1750 BCE), because of his law code, carved on a seven-foot stone stele and rediscovered in 1901.[^8] It is not the first law code (Ur-Nammu's is three centuries older) and not a code in our sense; it is a collection of 282 rulings, framed as the king's gift of justice. Its logic is the "eye for an eye" that later entered the Hebrew Bible, but graded by class: an injury to a nobleman cost the perpetrator the same injury; to a commoner, a fine; to a slave, a smaller fine. It regulates wages, interest rates, divorce, adoption, medical malpractice, and false accusation, and it assumes a society of free men, dependents, and slaves, with private property, contracts, and courts. Mesopotamian society was literate, litigious, commercial, and unequal, and had been for a thousand years before Hammurabi.

## Egypt: the gift of the Nile

Egypt is the other river valley, and almost everything about it was different. The **Nile** floods once a year, in late summer, predictably, depositing silt and receding in time for planting; farming it needed less organization than Mesopotamia's canals, and the valley, a green ribbon a few miles wide with desert on both sides, was easy to defend and hard to leave. Egypt was unified under one king around 3100 BCE, in the tradition by a ruler named Narmer, and stayed a single state, with interruptions, for three thousand years, longer than any other in history.[^9] Mesopotamia never managed unity for more than a few centuries at a time.

Egyptologists divide the story into kingdoms and intermediate periods. The **Old Kingdom** (c. 2686–2181 BCE) is the age of the pyramids: the Great Pyramid at Giza, built for Khufu around 2560 BCE, was the tallest structure on Earth for nearly four thousand years and was built, the archaeology of the workers' village shows, by paid and fed laborers organized in gangs, not by slaves, in about twenty years.[^10] It is the most extreme expression in history of the idea that the state exists to serve the king's afterlife. The Old Kingdom fell at about the same time as Akkad, probably for related climatic reasons, though internal causes are argued too. The **Middle Kingdom** (c. 2055–1650) reunified the country and extended it south into Nubia; the **New Kingdom** (c. 1550–1069) made Egypt an empire reaching to Syria, produced its most famous rulers (Hatshepsut, a woman who ruled as king; Akhenaten, who tried to replace Egypt's gods with one; Tutankhamun, whose only distinction is that his tomb was found intact; Ramesses II, who ruled sixty-six years and built more than anyone), and fought the Hittites to a standstill at Kadesh.[^11]

Egyptian civilization's central preoccupation, to the outsider, is death: pyramids, mummies, the *Book of the Dead*. That is partly an artifact of what survives, since tombs were built in the desert and houses in the floodplain. But it is also real. Egyptians believed that a person survived death if the body was preserved, the name remembered, and the heart found lighter than a feather when weighed against truth, and they spent an enormous share of the country's wealth on securing that outcome.[^12] The pharaoh was a god, the living Horus, and the state was his household; there was no law code because the king's word was law, and there was little of the commercial and legal texture of Mesopotamia because Egypt's economy ran through the temples and the palace rather than through merchants. The two civilizations show that "the first states" were not one thing.

## What we owe them

The list is long enough to make the point. From Mesopotamia: writing, the wheel (for pots first, then carts), the sexagesimal counting system that survives in our sixty-minute hour and 360-degree circle, the earliest astronomy, the earliest law, the earliest epic, the seven-day week, beer, and the plow. From Egypt: the 365-day solar calendar, monumental stone architecture, papyrus and the roll book, geometry for surveying, and a medical tradition recorded in papyri that describe wounds, examinations, and prognoses in a recognizably clinical voice.[^13] Both gave us the idea, novel in 3000 BCE and universal since, that a large population could be ruled by one center through officials, taxes, and records. Neither invented democracy, philosophy, or coinage, which come later and elsewhere (chapters 5 and 6), and neither had a word for freedom in the political sense. They were the first states, and the first states were not gentle.

:::story The scribes
A Mesopotamian schoolboy's exercise tablet from about 1700 BCE, found in Nippur, records a day at school: the boy arrived late and was beaten; wrote his tablet badly and was beaten; spoke without permission and was beaten; and went home to ask his father to invite the teacher to dinner and give him a gift, after which the teacher praised him as a future scholar.[^14] Scribal training took years, teaching a syllabary of hundreds of signs, Sumerian (already a dead language kept for scholarship, like Latin in medieval Europe), mathematics, and the literary classics. Scribes were perhaps one percent of the population and every letter, contract, tax record, and poem passed through their hands. Literacy was a profession, and the world it recorded is the world they saw. When we read about ancient Mesopotamia, we are reading what a small caste of trained men in the employ of temples and kings chose to write, and the 99 percent who could not write appear mainly as entries in their ledgers.
:::

## Summary

- Cities, states, and writing first appeared in southern Mesopotamia around 3500–3300 BCE, in a landscape that rewarded large-scale irrigation, and in Egypt within a few centuries.
- Writing began as accounting and became literature after seven hundred years; cuneiform tablets survive in the hundreds of thousands and make Mesopotamia the best-documented early society.
- Sargon created the first empire around 2334 BCE; Hammurabi's rulings show a commercial, litigious, and stratified society; the region's pattern was recurrent conquest by outsiders who adopted its culture.
- Egypt, unified around 3100 BCE, stayed one state for three millennia, ruled by a divine king through temple and palace, and devoted vast resources to the afterlife.
- Both civilizations bequeathed the tools of the state, calendars, mathematics, and monumental architecture; neither had democracy, philosophy, or coinage.
