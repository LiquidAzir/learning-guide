## Why could a successful republic fail to govern its own expansion?

While Alexander's successors fought over the East, a Latin city in central Italy was doing something no Greek polis had managed: conquering its neighbors and making them Romans. This chapter covers the Republic, the five centuries in which Rome grew from a town to the master of the Mediterranean under a government of elected magistrates, and the century in which that government tore itself apart. Its story was the political education of every European statesman from Machiavelli to the American founders, and they read it as a warning.

## The city and its constitution

Rome's own story began with Romulus in 753 BCE and a line of seven kings, the last expelled in 509 for tyranny. Archaeology confirms a settlement on the Palatine hill from the tenth century and a city with public buildings by the sixth; the rest is legend the Romans believed.[^1] What replaced the kings was a **republic** (*res publica*, "the public thing"): a state ruled by annually elected magistrates, a senate of former magistrates, and assemblies of citizens, arranged so that no one person could hold power for long or alone.

The pieces: two **consuls**, elected each year, each able to veto the other, who commanded the armies and presided over the state. Lesser magistrates, praetors (judges), aediles (city administration), quaestors (finance), also annual. The **Senate**, some 300 ex-magistrates who sat for life and whose advice was, in practice, law. The assemblies of the people, which elected the magistrates and passed the laws, voting by groups weighted toward the wealthy. And the ten **tribunes of the plebs**, elected by the common people to protect them, with the power to veto any act of any magistrate.[^2] The system was a fossil record of two centuries of class conflict between the **patricians**, the old aristocracy, and the **plebeians**, everyone else, who won their tribunes, a written law code (the Twelve Tables, c. 450), access to the consulship (367), and finally the rule that plebeian assembly decisions bound all Romans (287). The Greek historian Polybius, a hostage in Rome in the second century, admired it as a mixed constitution, monarchy in the consuls, aristocracy in the senate, democracy in the assemblies, each checking the others.[^3] The American founders read Polybius.

What the constitution did not say mattered as much. Roman public life ran on **patronage**: every powerful man had clients who owed him votes and support in return for protection and favors, and every magistrate was an aristocrat competing with other aristocrats for **honor**, measured in offices held, triumphs celebrated, and ancestors displayed at funerals. The system worked as long as the competition stayed within rules that were mostly unwritten.

## The conquest of Italy and the Mediterranean

Rome fought every year. By 264 BCE it controlled all of Italy south of the Po, not by ruling the conquered directly but by binding them as allies who supplied troops, and by settling Roman citizens in colonies across the peninsula. The **Roman legion**, a flexible formation of heavy infantry organized in maniples that could replace the phalanx's rigid line, was drawn from citizen farmers serving unpaid campaigns between harvests.[^4] Uniquely among ancient states, Rome extended citizenship to some of the conquered and, in time, to most of them. Its manpower was therefore effectively bottomless, and that, rather than tactical brilliance, is why it won wars it lost battles in.

The wars with **Carthage**, the Phoenician city that ruled the western Mediterranean's trade, are the ones to know. The first (264–241) was fought over Sicily and made Rome a naval power from nothing. The second (218–201) was Hannibal's: he marched from Spain over the Alps with an army and elephants, destroyed three Roman armies in three years, at Cannae in 216 killing perhaps 50,000 Romans in an afternoon, and spent fifteen years in Italy undefeated, while Rome refused to negotiate, raised new armies from the allies who mostly stayed loyal, and sent Scipio to attack Carthage itself. Hannibal was recalled and beaten at Zama in 202.[^5] The third war (149–146) was a siege that ended with Carthage burned and its site cursed. In the same year, 146, Rome destroyed Corinth and made Greece a province. Macedon had already fallen (168); the Seleucid kings had been humiliated; Egypt was a client. In a little over a century Rome had gone from one Italian power among several to the only power in the Mediterranean.

:::history How we got here
Why did Rome conquer? The Romans said self-defense: every war was provoked, every enemy a threat. Nineteenth-century historians, ruling empires themselves, tended to agree that Rome had stumbled into empire reluctantly. In 1979 William Harris argued the opposite, that Roman aristocratic culture required war, because military glory was the route to office and booty and slaves enriched everyone from senator to legionary, and that Rome went looking for enemies.[^6] The current view sits between: Rome's political system rewarded aggression and its alliance system generated constant pretexts, but the Mediterranean of the third century was a violent place in which every state fought when it could, and Rome's distinction was not that it fought but that it won and then absorbed. The debate is a case study in how the historian's own era shapes the question: imperial Britain asked whether empire could be innocent, and post-1945 scholarship asked how it could have been thought so.
:::

## The republic breaks

Conquest destroyed the republic in a century, and the mechanism is worth understanding because it is the mechanism of most later republics' failures.

**Wealth.** The plunder of the East, the taxes of the provinces, and hundreds of thousands of slaves flowed to Rome, and almost all of it to the senatorial class, who bought up Italian farmland and worked it with slave gangs while the citizen-farmers who had won the wars were away winning them.[^7] Displaced farmers drifted to Rome as a landless urban poor.

**Armies.** From 107 BCE the general Marius recruited the landless into the legions, promising land at discharge. The army became professional and loyal to the general who paid it rather than the state that did not.

**Violence.** In 133 BCE the tribune Tiberius Gracchus tried to redistribute public land to the poor; senators clubbed him to death. His brother Gaius tried again as tribune in 123–122 and died in 121 in a second round of political violence. Political murder, once introduced, did not leave. From 91 to 88 Rome's Italian allies revolted for citizenship and got it. In 88 the consul Sulla, told to give up his command, marched his army on Rome, the first Roman to do so, and by 82 was **dictator**, an emergency office he used to kill or outlaw well over a thousand opponents, posting their names on lists, and rewrite the constitution to strengthen the Senate. He then retired, and his settlement lasted a decade.[^8]

**The dynasts.** The generation after Sulla produced men who commanded loyal armies, held commands for years, and were richer than the treasury. **Pompey** conquered the East. **Crassus** was the richest man in Rome. **Julius Caesar**, a patrician who had made his career as a champion of the people, borrowed enormously from Crassus, allied with both, obtained a five-year command in Gaul, and spent it conquering the whole of it (58–50 BCE), killing, by the later reckoning of Plutarch and Pliny, a million people and enslaving as many, and writing his own account of the war for the Roman public.[^9] When the Senate ordered him home to face prosecution, he crossed the Rubicon into Italy with one legion in January 49, and the civil war began. He beat Pompey at Pharsalus in 48; Pompey fled to Egypt and was murdered on the beach. Caesar made himself dictator for life, reformed the calendar (the Julian year, with leap days, is still the skeleton of ours), and on 15 March 44 was stabbed to death by senators who believed they were saving the republic.

They were not. Thirteen more years of civil war followed: Caesar's heir **Octavian**, aged eighteen at the assassination, allied with Caesar's lieutenant Mark Antony, killed the assassins and thousands of others, then fought Antony and his ally Cleopatra of Egypt and beat them at Actium in 31 BCE. Antony and Cleopatra killed themselves. Egypt became Octavian's personal property. In 27 BCE the Senate voted him the name **Augustus**, and the republic, which he claimed to be restoring, was over, though no one said so for a long time.[^10]

:::story Cicero
Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BCE) was a lawyer from a small town who rose by talent to the consulship, the greatest orator in Roman history, and a philosopher whose Latin adaptations of Greek thought taught Europe its vocabulary for law, duty, and the state. He also left about 900 letters, to friends, to his brother, to his rich friend Atticus, that were published after his death and that make him the first person in history whose inner life we can follow week by week: his vanity, his fear, his grief at his daughter's death, his indecision in the civil war, his contempt for Caesar and his terror of him.[^11] He backed the losing side every time, tried to use the young Octavian against Antony, and was murdered on Antony's orders in December 43; his head and hands were nailed up in the Forum. He is the reason the fall of the republic is the best-documented political crisis in the ancient world, and his verdict on it, that a free state cannot survive when its leading men prefer their own power to its laws, was the one Europe took to heart.
:::

## Why it matters

The Roman Republic gave later history three things. A **legal tradition**: Roman law, developed by professional jurists over five centuries and codified by the emperor Justinian in the sixth century CE, is the basis of the civil law of most of Europe, Latin America, and much of Asia today (chapter 13). A **political vocabulary**: republic, senate, consul, dictator, veto, citizen, magistrate, plebeian, and the idea of a constitution as a balance of powers; the American founders named their upper house for Rome's and put Roman fasces on their seals and public buildings. And a **cautionary tale**: that a republic can be conquered by its own generals, that political violence once permitted escalates, that extreme inequality and a professional army loyal to individuals are fatal to self-government, and that the forms of liberty can outlast its substance by centuries. Every republic since has read that story and asked whether it was its own.

:::know Dates to hold onto
- 509 BCE: traditional founding of the Republic.
- c. 450 BCE: the Twelve Tables.
- 264–241, 218–201, 149–146 BCE: the three Punic Wars.
- 216 BCE: Cannae. 202: Zama.
- 146 BCE: Carthage and Corinth destroyed.
- 133 BCE: Tiberius Gracchus killed.
- 88 BCE: Sulla marches on Rome.
- 58–50 BCE: Caesar conquers Gaul. 49: crosses the Rubicon.
- 15 March 44 BCE: Caesar assassinated.
- 31 BCE: Actium. 27 BCE: Octavian becomes Augustus.

:::try Put the idea to work
Why is blaming Rome's republican collapse on one ambitious general less explanatory than tracing changes in armies, wealth, and political competition?

:::answer One way to reason it through
Ambition existed before the collapse. The deeper question is why institutions no longer contained it. Military loyalties, unequal gains from conquest, and precedents for political violence changed the opportunities and costs of breaking rules. Individual choices mattered within those changing conditions.
:::

## Summary

- The Republic's constitution, annual consuls, a life senate, popular assemblies, and tribunes with a veto, was the residue of two centuries of class conflict and was admired by Polybius as a balance of powers.
- Rome conquered Italy by alliance and citizenship, and the Mediterranean by out-lasting Carthage and dismantling the Hellenistic kingdoms; its manpower, not its generals, was decisive.
- Conquest concentrated wealth, displaced the citizen-farmers, and produced professional armies loyal to their generals; political murder entered in 133 and never left.
- Sulla, Pompey, Caesar, and Octavian each used armies against the state; Caesar's assassination in 44 did not save the republic, and Augustus ended it in 27 while claiming to restore it.
- Rome bequeathed the civil-law tradition, the vocabulary of republican politics, and the story of how a republic dies.
