## Recap

Most of this section's chapters so far have followed a chain from Mesopotamia through Greece and Rome, because that chain is the best documented and leads to the Europe of the next section. It is not the only chain. This chapter is about the ancient world outside it: the Americas, which developed cities, writing, and states with no contact with Eurasia at all; Africa beyond Egypt; and the Iron Age peoples of northern Europe and the steppe whom Greeks and Romans wrote about only as enemies. It is also about how much of this was invisible to history until archaeology, and lately laser scanning from aircraft, made it visible.

## The Americas: a second experiment

The Americas were peopled from Siberia by at least 16,000 years ago (chapter 2) and were then cut off from the Old World until 1492. Everything their inhabitants built, they built without wheat, rice, cattle, horses, sheep, pigs, the wheel as a working tool, iron, or any knowledge that Eurasia existed. They domesticated maize, beans, squash, potatoes, tomatoes, chili, cacao, cotton, and the llama; they built cities as large as most in Europe; they invented writing, the zero, and astronomical tables of an accuracy Rome never matched. The Americas are the control experiment for the question of whether civilization was a Eurasian accident, and the answer is no.[^1]

**Mesoamerica** (Mexico and northern Central America) produced its first complex society, the **Olmec**, on the Gulf coast around 1500–400 BCE: earthen pyramids, colossal stone heads weighing up to forty tons, and a ballgame with a rubber ball. From the Olmec, or alongside them, came the elements every later Mesoamerican civilization shared: pyramids, the 260-day ritual calendar interlocking with a 365-day solar one, human sacrifice, the ballgame, and writing.[^2] The **Maya**, in the lowlands of Guatemala, Belize, and Yucatán, took these furthest. Between about 250 and 900 CE, the Classic period, they built dozens of city-states, Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán, with stone temples over sixty meters high, and wrote their history on them in the only fully developed writing system of the pre-Columbian Americas: hieroglyphs recording the births, accessions, wars, and captives of their kings by exact date, in a system that combined signs for words and for syllables and that was deciphered, mostly, between 1952 and the 1980s.[^3] Their astronomers predicted eclipses and tracked Venus; their mathematicians used a place-value system with zero centuries before Europe had it. Around 800–900 the southern cities were abandoned in a collapse that involved drought (the lake and cave records are clear), overpopulation, war, and the failure of kingship; the northern cities lasted until the Spanish.

In the Valley of Mexico, **Teotihuacan** (c. 100 BCE–550 CE) was one of the largest cities in the world, perhaps 100,000 people on a grid plan dominated by pyramids of a scale to match Egypt's, and its rulers left no names and no royal portraits at all: a civilization apparently organized, like the Indus, on some other principle than the glorified king. Its influence reached the Maya; its collapse is unexplained. The Aztecs, who came a thousand years later and gave it its name ("place where gods were born"), regarded it with awe, and so should we.

In the **Andes**, civilization began even earlier and in an even less likely place: the coastal desert of Peru, where Caral around 2600 BCE, contemporary with the pyramids, had monumental platforms and plazas but no pottery and apparently no war.[^4] The Chavín cult (c. 900–200 BCE) spread across the highlands; the Moche (c. 100–800 CE) on the north coast built mud-brick pyramids and made the finest ceramics and metalwork in the Americas; the Nazca drew their lines in the desert. The Andean peoples built no cities on the Mesoamerican model, had no writing (they kept records on knotted strings, *khipu*, whose full logic remains undeciphered), and organized enormous labor projects through kinship obligations to the state. The Inca, who by 1500 ruled the largest empire in the Americas from Ecuador to Chile, inherited all of this; they are outside this section's span.

:::frontier
Until about 2010, the standard estimate of the Maya lowlands' Classic-period population was in the low millions, and the Amazon was assumed to have been nearly empty wilderness. Then archaeologists began flying LiDAR, laser scanning that maps the ground surface beneath forest canopy. In 2018 a survey of northern Guatemala found 60,000 previously unknown structures and revised the Maya lowland population toward 10 million or more; in 2024, a re-analysis of LiDAR collected for an environmental survey found an unknown Maya city, Valeriana, with pyramids and plazas in a part of Campeche archaeologists had never walked.[^5] The same year, LiDAR in the Ecuadorian Amazon revealed a network of platform settlements, roads, and drained fields occupied from 500 BCE to 600 CE, "garden urbanism" in a rainforest supposedly incapable of supporting cities.[^6] The pre-Columbian Americas were far more populous and urbanized than the textbooks of twenty years ago said, and their depopulation after 1492, by Old World disease above all, was correspondingly greater. The maps are being redrawn every year.
:::

## Africa beyond Egypt

Egypt was an African civilization, and it was not the only one.

**Nubia**, the Nile valley south of Egypt in modern Sudan, had a kingdom, Kerma, contemporary with the Egyptian Middle Kingdom, was conquered by the New Kingdom, and then, as **Kush**, conquered Egypt in turn: the Kushite kings ruled as pharaohs of the twenty-fifth dynasty (c. 744–656 BCE) until Assyria drove them out. Retreating south to Meroë, the Kushite kingdom lasted another thousand years, until about 350 CE, built more pyramids than Egypt (smaller ones), developed its own alphabetic script, Meroitic, which can be read but not understood, worked iron on an industrial scale, and fought the Romans to a treaty.[^7] Its queens, the *kandakes*, ruled in their own right and one of them, in the Roman account, was one-eyed and formidable.

**Aksum**, in the Ethiopian highlands, was in the first to seventh centuries CE one of the four great powers of the age in the estimate of a Persian writer, alongside Rome, Persia, and China. It traded through the Red Sea port of Adulis with Rome and India, minted gold coins, raised stone stelae up to thirty-three meters high, the largest single stones anyone has ever tried to stand upright, and in the 330s or 340s became one of the first states in the world, after Armenia, to adopt Christianity, which its heirs in Ethiopia have kept ever since.[^8]

**Carthage**, the Phoenician colony in Tunisia (chapter 5), was for three centuries the greatest commercial power in the western Mediterranean, ruling a maritime empire from Sardinia to Spain, exploring the Atlantic coast of Africa, and governing itself, according to Aristotle, through an admirably balanced constitution.[^9] Rome destroyed it in 146 BCE and wrote its history; almost all we know of Carthage is from its enemies, including the accusation of child sacrifice, which the archaeology of its infant cemeteries now suggests was real but not on the scale the Romans alleged. Rome then rebuilt it as a Roman city, and Roman Africa, the empire's breadbasket, produced emperors, the novelist Apuleius, and Augustine, the most influential Christian thinker between Paul and Luther.

South of the Sahara, the **Nok** culture of Nigeria (c. 1000 BCE–300 CE) made life-sized terracotta sculptures and smelted iron by 500 BCE, and the **Bantu expansion**, the spread of Bantu-speaking farmers from the Nigeria–Cameroon region across the whole southern half of the continent beginning as early as 3000 BCE and reaching southern Africa by 500 CE, carrying cattle, crops, and, in its later phase, iron, is one of the largest migrations in human history and is now traceable in genes as well as languages.[^10] The trans-Saharan trade that would make medieval Ghana and Mali rich began with the camel, introduced to North Africa in Roman times. Ancient sub-Saharan Africa left few monuments and no texts, and that, not the absence of history, is why the textbooks of a century ago said it had none.

## The barbarians

The peoples the Greeks and Romans called **barbarians**, originally just "people whose speech sounds like bar-bar," were not without history; they were without historians. The **Celts**, from the Iron Age Hallstatt and La Tène cultures of central Europe, dominated Europe north of the Alps from about 800 BCE, sacked Rome in 390, and were conquered by it from Spain (second century) to Gaul (Caesar, 58–50) and Britain (43 CE onward); their languages survive in Welsh, Irish, Gaelic, and Breton, and their art, the swirling metalwork of the La Tène style, is among the finest of antiquity.[^11] The **Germanic** peoples beyond the Rhine and Danube, described by Tacitus around 98 CE as noble savages whose virtues shamed Rome's decadence (the description was a stick to beat his fellow Romans with and has been misused ever since), traded, fought, and served with Rome for four centuries before, in the fifth, they took over its western half (chapter 13).[^12] On the steppe, the **Scythians**, horse-archers who buried their kings under mounds full of gold, held the Black Sea grasslands from the seventh century BCE, defeated Darius of Persia, and were described by Herodotus with the fascinated attention he gave anyone unlike a Greek; their eastern cousins, the Xiongnu, are the great enemy of Han China in chapter 27.[^13]

What archaeology has shown about all of these is that "barbarian" measured distance from Rome, not complexity. The Celtic *oppida*, fortified towns of the second and first centuries BCE, held thousands of people and minted coins. Scythian burials contain the finest goldwork of antiquity. The Germanic peoples had law, kingship, poetry, and trade networks that reached Rome. When these peoples enter the Roman histories they are always arriving, invading, threatening; when they enter the archaeological record they are farming, trading, and burying their dead with care, like everyone else.

:::key
Every list of "the cradles of civilization" is a list of where writing was invented, because writing is what historians read. Cities, states, monumental architecture, mathematics, and astronomy arose independently in the Americas without any Eurasian contact; iron, kingship, and long-distance trade arose across Africa; and the "barbarians" of Europe and the steppe had towns, coins, and law. The Eurasian chain from Sumer to Rome is one thread in a world that was weaving several at once, and the reason it dominates the textbooks is the accident of what survives and who wrote it. The current decade's discoveries, above all from LiDAR and ancient DNA, are correcting that faster than any before.
:::

## Summary

- The Americas developed cities, states, writing, zero, and precise calendars in complete isolation from Eurasia, with no draft animals, wheels, or iron: the Olmec, Maya, Teotihuacan, and the Andean cultures from Caral to the Moche.
- LiDAR since 2018 has multiplied the known scale of Maya and Amazonian settlement and revised pre-Columbian populations sharply upward.
- Africa beyond Egypt had Kush, which ruled Egypt and lasted a thousand years at Meroë; Aksum, a great power that adopted Christianity in the 330s or 340s; Carthage; Nok iron; and the Bantu expansion across half a continent.
- Celts, Germans, and Scythians were "barbarians" only by lacking historians; their towns, coins, law, and art are in the ground.
- What counts as a cradle of civilization has tracked what left texts; archaeology and genetics are rewriting the list.
