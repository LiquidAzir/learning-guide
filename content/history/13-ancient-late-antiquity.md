## Recap

Chapter 9 left the Roman empire at its height, with Commodus strangled in 192 and the machine still running. This chapter follows it through the crisis that nearly destroyed it, the reconstruction that saved it in a new form, its conversion to Christianity, the loss of the West, and the world that emerged around 600: three successor civilizations, Byzantine, Latin Christian, and, about to arrive, Islamic, where there had been one empire. It closes the Ancient section and opens the European one.

## The third-century crisis

Between 235 and 284 Rome had more than twenty emperors, nearly all of them generals raised by their troops and killed by them or by rivals; the average reign was under three years. The frontiers broke: Goths crossed the Danube and killed an emperor in battle in 251; Persia, under the new Sasanian dynasty that had replaced the Parthians in 224, captured an emperor alive in 260 and kept him; Gaul seceded for fourteen years and the East for three, as separate empires. A plague, perhaps smallpox, killed a large share of the population from 249. The currency collapsed: the silver coinage, debased to pay the armies, fell to a few percent silver, and prices rose many times over in a century.[^1] Cities shrank and walled themselves for the first time in centuries. The historian who lived through its start, Herodian, wrote that the empire had never seen such a time; the modern consensus agrees and adds that it very nearly ended there.

Why it did not is a matter of the soldiers on the Danube, who kept producing competent emperors from their own ranks, and of **Diocletian** (284–305), a Dalmatian soldier who reorganized everything. He divided the empire's rule among four emperors (the **Tetrarchy**), doubled the provinces and grouped them into dioceses, separated civil from military command, enlarged the army and the bureaucracy, fixed prices by decree (it failed), reformed taxation on a regular census, made many occupations hereditary, and launched the last and greatest persecution of Christians in 303. He retired to grow cabbages, the first emperor to abdicate voluntarily, and the Tetrarchy fell apart in civil war within a year.[^2]

## Constantine and the Christian empire

The civil war's winner was **Constantine** (306–337), who in 312, before the battle of the Milvian Bridge outside Rome, saw, or said he saw, a sign in the sky, put a Christian symbol on his soldiers' shields, won, and attributed the victory to the Christian God. In 313 he legalized Christianity; over the next twenty-five years he funded churches on a vast scale, exempted clergy from taxes, gave bishops judicial powers, called the Council of Nicaea in 325 to settle a dispute over Christ's divinity (and presided, though unbaptized), founded a new capital at Byzantium and named it Constantinople, and was baptized on his deathbed.[^3] Whether he converted from conviction or calculation has been argued since his own lifetime; the two are not exclusive, and the calculation would have been odd, since Christians were perhaps a tenth of the population and unpopular with the army and the Senate.

The consequences unrolled over a century. Christian emperors, with one pagan exception (Julian, 361–363), followed; imperial favor made conversion advantageous, and bishops became the most powerful men in most cities. In 380 Theodosius made Nicene Christianity the state religion; in 391–392 he banned pagan sacrifice and closed the temples. Paganism did not vanish, but it stopped being public and became a private and rural matter that the church spent centuries suppressing. By 400 the empire was Christian in law and largely in fact, and the church had acquired the empire's administrative geography (dioceses), its Latin, its buildings (the basilica), its imperial style, and its habit of enforcing uniformity. The persecuted became the persecutors of pagans, Jews, and, above all, other Christians who held the wrong views, a change that some churchmen noticed at the time and that Augustine, who came to defend the coercion of heretics, argued through in his own writings.[^4]

## The West falls

The fourth-century empire was strong, and the long decline the older textbooks described did not happen. What happened was quicker and more contingent. In 376, Goths fleeing the Huns, a steppe people newly arrived from the east, asked to cross the Danube and were admitted; mistreated, they revolted, and in 378 at Adrianople they destroyed a Roman army and killed the emperor Valens. Rome could not expel them and settled them inside the empire as an armed people under their own kings, the first such settlement. In 406 a coalition of Vandals, Alans, and Sueves crossed the Rhine into Gaul; in 410 the Goths under Alaric sacked Rome itself, the first time in eight centuries; the Vandals took Africa, the empire's breadbasket and tax base, in 429–439; the Huns under Attila ravaged the Balkans and Gaul in the 440s and 450s; and the western government, deprived of revenue, hired barbarian armies to fight barbarian armies until the generals of those armies were the government. In 476 one of them, Odoacer, deposed the last emperor in Italy, a boy nicknamed Augustulus ("little Augustus"), sent the regalia to Constantinople, and ruled as king. Nobody at the time treated 476 as the end of anything; the date was chosen by later historians because it is tidy.[^5]

By 500 the West was a set of kingdoms: Visigoths in Spain, Franks in Gaul, Ostrogoths in Italy, Vandals in Africa, Angles and Saxons in Britain, each ruled by a Germanic warrior elite over a Roman population, using Roman law for the Romans and their own for themselves, employing Roman administrators, and, except in Britain, keeping Latin and, in time, converting to the Romans' Catholic Christianity. The East, richer, more urban, less exposed, with a capital that could not be taken, went on. Its emperor Justinian (527–565) reconquered Africa and Italy, codified Roman law (chapter 9), built Hagia Sophia, and presided over a plague in 541 that killed a large fraction of the Mediterranean's population (how large is now disputed) and wars with Persia that resumed on and off until 628, when a final round exhausted both empires just before the Arabs arrived (chapter 14).[^6]

:::history How we got here
Edward Gibbon, in 1776, made the fall of Rome the type of all historical decline, and blamed it on internal decay: a corrupt court, a mercenary army, a population sapped of civic virtue, and Christianity, which turned the best minds from the state to the next world. For two centuries the question was which internal cause mattered most; over two hundred were proposed, from lead poisoning to the loss of manly vigor. In 1971 Peter Brown's *The World of Late Antiquity* changed the question. He argued that the period from 200 to 700 was not a decline but a transformation: creative, religious, and the true birth of the medieval world, and that "fall" was a Renaissance humanist's judgment about art and Latin style. For a generation "Late Antiquity" replaced "the Fall of Rome" in university syllabi. Then, around 2005, Bryan Ward-Perkins and Peter Heather pushed back with the archaeology: in the fifth-century West, pottery got cruder, coins vanished, literacy collapsed, roofs went from tile to thatch, and cattle got smaller. Something real and bad happened to material life, and Heather argued it was caused by the barbarian invasions, themselves set off by the Huns, rather than by internal rot. The current view holds both: the West suffered a genuine collapse in state capacity, trade, and living standards, caused proximately by the loss of Africa's taxes and the settlement of armed peoples the state could not control, while religious and cultural life was transformed rather than destroyed, and the East did not fall at all. Whether one calls it a fall depends on whether one is looking at the roofs or the theology.[^7]
:::

## What changed, and what did not

The centuries between 250 and 600 did three things that define what came after.

**They made Christianity the frame of Western civilization.** Doctrine was settled in the councils (Nicaea 325, Constantinople 381, Chalcedon 451); the Bible's canon was fixed; the Latin translation, Jerome's Vulgate, became the West's book; monasticism, invented in the Egyptian desert around 300, spread west and by 600 was the main institution preserving literacy; and Augustine wrote the theology, of original sin, grace, the two cities, and just war, that Latin Christendom would think within for a thousand years.[^8] The bishop of Rome, the **pope**, emerged as the West's one Roman institution still standing, and by 600, under Gregory the Great, was governing Rome, feeding it, and sending missionaries to England.

**They ended the classical city.** The self-governing city of chapter 9, with its baths, theaters, councils, and civic elites competing to build, was gone in the West by 600 and much reduced in the East. Its elites became bishops or landowners; its buildings became churches or quarries; its population shrank to a fraction. Rome fell from perhaps a million to a few tens of thousands. Literacy, which had been common among the urban classes, became a clerical specialty.[^9]

**They separated the Mediterranean into three.** By 600 the old Roman world was becoming three civilizations: the Greek Christian East, ruled from Constantinople, which called itself Roman until 1453 (we call it **Byzantine**, a name it never used); the Latin Christian West, poor, rural, and fragmented, whose kingdoms would become Europe's nations; and, from the 630s, the Islamic world, which would take the East's richest provinces and the West's Africa and Spain within a century (chapter 14). The next section of this guide follows the second of these.

What did not change: the peasants. For the eighty or ninety percent of people who farmed, the transition from Roman to Gothic or Frankish rule mostly meant a different set of masters, sometimes lighter taxes, and the same work. The ancient world ended for the literate few. For everyone else, one long age of muscle, grain, and weather went on as before, and would until the eighteenth century.

:::key
Rome did not fall in a day or from a single cause, and it did not fall in the East at all. What ended between 400 and 600 was a particular arrangement: a Mediterranean-wide state funding a professional army from the taxes of self-governing cities, in a culture of classical literacy and civic religion. What replaced it in the West was poorer and simpler in every material way and, in religion and thought, different rather than worse. The argument between "fall" and "transformation" is the discipline's oldest, and the resolution is that both are true of different things. When you read of any later civilization's decline, ask which of its things declined, for whom, and who is measuring.
:::

## Summary

- The third-century crisis, with twenty emperors in fifty years, Persian and Gothic breakthroughs, plague, and currency collapse, nearly ended the empire; Diocletian rebuilt it as a larger, more bureaucratic military autocracy.
- Constantine legalized and favored Christianity from 312; by 392 it was the sole legal religion, and the church acquired the empire's structure, language, and habit of enforcing uniformity.
- The West fell between 376 and 476 through a chain of contingencies, the Gothic settlement, the loss of Africa, and reliance on barbarian armies, and became a set of Germanic kingdoms over Roman populations; the East went on for a thousand years.
- Historians have argued since Gibbon whether this was a fall or a transformation; the archaeology shows a real material collapse in the West alongside a religious and cultural transformation.
- By 600 the Roman world was becoming three: Byzantium, Latin Christendom, and, imminently, Islam; for peasants, almost nothing changed.
