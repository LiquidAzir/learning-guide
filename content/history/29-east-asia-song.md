## Why didn't Song China's innovations lead to the same path as later Europe?

The Tang fell in 907 and China had fifty years of short dynasties. This chapter covers the Song (960–1279), which reunified most of China and presided over what historians call its medieval economic revolution, with Korea's Goryeo and Japan's Heian courts alongside. It is the chapter in which China is, by any measure of wealth, technology, and urbanization, the most advanced society on Earth, and in which a question that runs through the whole section first becomes sharp: why did the modern world begin in Europe and not here?

## A smaller, richer empire

The **Song** never held the Tang's territory. The northeast belonged to the Khitan Liao, a steppe dynasty ruling from Manchuria to Beijing, and the northwest to the Tangut Xia; the Song paid both annual tribute, in silk and silver, rather than fight, and their Confucian officials argued that it was cheaper than war, which it was. In 1127 the Jurchen **Jin**, who had destroyed the Liao, took the Song capital Kaifeng and the emperor with it; the court fled south, and the **Southern Song** (1127–1279) ruled the Yangzi and everything below from Hangzhou.[^1] A weaker state militarily than any major Chinese dynasty, the Song was the richest, and the contrast is the point.

The wealth had causes. The Yangzi valley, settled in the age of division, became the granary with early-ripening Champa rice from Vietnam, allowing two crops a year, and with dikes, terraces, and pumps. Population, 50 to 60 million under the Tang, passed 100 million around 1100. Trade was freed: the Tang's walled market wards and curfews were abandoned, shops opened on any street, and cities grew without limit. **Kaifeng** and then Hangzhou held about a million people each; Marco Polo, who saw Hangzhou under the Mongols, called it the finest city in the world and could not make Europeans believe his descriptions.[^2] The government, having lost the northern land tax, lived on commerce: by the twelfth century the Southern Song drew more revenue from trade and monopolies than from land, a situation no European state would reach before the eighteenth century.

## The inventions

The list is long and it is worth having.

**Paper money**, issued first by Sichuan merchants around 1000 and by the state from 1024, the first in the world, backed by reserves and then, as every government since has discovered, not.[^3] **Movable type**, invented by Bi Sheng around 1040 with ceramic characters, four centuries before Gutenberg; it never displaced woodblock printing, because a script of thousands of characters suits blocks better than a font, but printing of every kind exploded, and the Song produced the printed book as an ordinary object, with commercial publishers, bookshops, and a reading public. **Gunpowder**, known to Tang alchemists, became a weapon: fire-lances, bombs, rockets, and by the thirteenth century the first true guns, tubes firing projectiles by explosion, appear in Song and Jin armies. **The magnetic compass** was used for navigation by about 1100, and Song ships, with watertight compartments, stern-post rudders, and multiple masts, carried Chinese trade to India and the Persian Gulf. **Iron**: Song China produced perhaps 125,000 tons a year by 1078, using coke in blast furnaces, a figure Britain would not reach until the 1790s.[^4] Add the mechanical clock (Su Song's astronomical tower of 1092), the spinning wheel, canal pound-locks, inoculation against smallpox, and forensic medicine, and the Song looks like the threshold of an industrial age.

## The examination state

The Song made the **examination system** the way into government. Where the Tang aristocracy had dominated office, the Song emperors, wary of generals and great families after the Tang's collapse, expanded the examinations until they were the main route: three tiers, local, provincial, and palace, testing the Confucian classics and the ability to write essays and poetry on them, held every three years, anonymously graded, with quotas by region, and open to any man not a criminal, merchant (in theory), or laborer. Perhaps 400,000 sat the lowest tier each cycle by the late Song and a few hundred passed the highest.[^5] Success meant office, wealth, tax exemption, and honor for one's clan; failure, for most, meant a life as a teacher, clerk, or local notable with a classical education. The result was a **gentry** of degree-holders and aspirants, a class whose status came from learning rather than birth or arms, who ran local society and staffed the state, and who were the most literate ruling class in the pre-modern world. It also meant that the most talented men in China spent their lives mastering texts a thousand years old, and that the state's officials were generalists who despised specialists, merchants, and soldiers.

The examination curriculum was codified in the Song by **Neo-Confucianism**, above all by Zhu Xi (1130–1200), who synthesized Confucian ethics with a metaphysics of principle (*li*) and material force (*qi*) borrowed in part from Buddhism, selected the Four Books (the *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Great Learning*, and *Doctrine of the Mean*) as the core canon, and gave China, Korea, and Japan the orthodox philosophy they would teach until the twentieth century.[^6] Zhu Xi's system was a cosmology, an ethics of self-cultivation, and a social order in one, and its hierarchical family ethics, including the seclusion of women and, from the Song, the binding of their feet, hardened over the following dynasties.

## Heian Japan and Goryeo Korea

Japan's **Heian** period (794–1185) began as a Tang-style state and became something else. The court at Heian (Kyoto) stopped sending embassies to China in 894, and over the next three centuries the Fujiwara clan governed as regents for child emperors they had fathered through their daughters, the land-allotment system dissolved into tax-free private estates (*shoen*) owned by nobles and temples, and the capital's aristocracy, a few thousand people, developed a culture of extraordinary refinement, Buddhist, aesthetic, and inward: poetry exchanged at dawn, incense contests, and the *Tale of Genji*, written around 1010 by a lady-in-waiting known as Murasaki Shikibu, the world's first psychological novel and, some say, its first novel of any kind.[^7] Women of the court, writing in the phonetic *kana* script while men wrote Chinese, produced most of the age's literature. Outside the capital, provincial warriors hired to keep order on the estates became a class, the **samurai**, and by the twelfth century their two great clans, Taira and Minamoto, fought for control of the court; the Minamoto won in 1185 and set up a military government, the **shogunate**, at Kamakura, leaving the emperor in Kyoto as a sacred figurehead. That arrangement, an emperor who reigned and a shogun who ruled, lasted until 1868 (chapter 33).

Korea's **Goryeo** dynasty (918–1392), from which the name Korea comes, replaced Silla, adopted Song-style examinations (though birth still mattered more), made Buddhism the state religion and printed the entire Buddhist canon on 81,000 woodblocks (the Tripitaka Koreana, carved 1236–1251 and still intact), produced the world's finest celadon ceramics, and invented, around 1234, metal movable type, two centuries before Gutenberg.[^8] It also spent the thirteenth century being invaded by the Mongols, which is the next chapter.

:::history How we got here
Why did Song China, with paper money, coke-fired iron, printing, gunpowder, and a commercial economy, not have the Industrial Revolution? The question was posed sharply by Joseph Needham in the 1950s and has had many answers. Mark Elvin's **high-level equilibrium trap** (1973): Song technology was so good at using the resources it had that there was no pressure to invent labor-saving machinery, and population growth ate the surplus. The **Mongol conquest** (chapter 30) and the Ming's inward turn broke the trajectory; the Song was interrupted, not exhausted. The **examination state**: a ruling class chosen for literary learning, contemptuous of merchants and engineers, and a state that could always tax or confiscate commercial wealth, gave no secure place to the entrepreneur. **Coal geography** (Pomeranz): England's coal was next to its industry and China's was far from its cities. The **absence of competition**: a unified empire had no rival states to lose to if it stagnated, where Europe's fragmented states had to innovate or be conquered. And, from the "California School," the claim that the question is wrong: China's core regions were as rich as Europe's until about 1750 or 1800, and the divergence came late and from contingencies, colonies and coal, rather than from a deep difference. Broadberry and colleagues' reconstruction of Chinese GDP has China leading the world under the Song and falling behind Italy by 1300, with the Yangzi delta matching Europe's richest regions until about 1700. The dating of the divergence, and therefore its cause, is the field's central argument, and it is not settled.[^9]
:::

:::key
Song China was the most advanced economy the pre-modern world produced, and it did not become modern. That fact is the standing rebuke to every simple theory of progress: technology, commerce, cities, and literacy were all present in abundance, and the outcome was a Mongol conquest, a Ming restoration of agrarian orthodoxy, and a stable agrarian empire for six more centuries. Whatever produced the modern world in Europe, it was not any one of those things. Meanwhile Japan, having borrowed China's state, let it dissolve into aristocratic estates and then warrior rule, and Korea built the East Asian world's most devout Buddhist kingdom. The region's three paths were diverging.
:::

## Summary

- The Song lost the north to steppe dynasties, paid tribute rather than fight, and became the richest state in the world on Yangzi rice, freed commerce, and cities of a million.
- Song China had paper money, movable type, gunpowder weapons, the navigational compass, ocean ships, coke-fired iron at 125,000 tons a year, and a printed-book culture centuries before Europe.
- The examination system became the main route to office, creating a gentry of degree-holders, and Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucianism became the orthodoxy of East Asia until the twentieth century.
- Heian Japan let the Tang-model state dissolve into aristocratic estates and warrior clans, produced the *Tale of Genji*, and ended in the Kamakura shogunate; Goryeo Korea printed the Buddhist canon and invented metal type.
- Why Song China did not industrialize is the Great Divergence debate, unsettled between deep structural explanations and late contingent ones.
