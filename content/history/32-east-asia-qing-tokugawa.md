## What made Qing and Tokugawa rule stable, and where were its limits?

The Manchus took Beijing in 1644; Tokugawa Ieyasu won Sekigahara in 1600. This chapter covers the two regimes they founded at their height, in the century and a half before the West arrived in force: the **Qing** at the peak of Chinese imperial power and the **Tokugawa** peace in Japan. It also takes up the Great Divergence argument from chapter 29 at the point where it is sharpest, around 1750, when the richest parts of China and Japan and the richest parts of Europe were, on most measures, about equally well off, and asks what the eighteenth century looked like from Beijing and Edo.

## The Manchu conquest and the Manchu way of ruling

The **Manchus** were a people of perhaps a million from the forests and plains of the northeast, descendants of the Jurchen who had ruled northern China as the Jin (chapter 29), organized by Nurhaci and his son into a military-administrative system called the **banners**, and they conquered a country of 150 million. It took forty years: the south fought on under Ming loyalists until 1662 and Taiwan, held by the pirate-king Koxinga, until 1683. The conquest was accompanied by massacres, above all at Yangzhou in 1645, and by the **queue** edict, requiring all Chinese men to shave their foreheads and braid their hair in the Manchu style on pain of death, an order that provoked more resistance than the conquest itself and that made every Chinese man's head a badge of submission for 267 years.[^1]

Having conquered, the Manchus ruled as Chinese emperors and as something more. They kept the Ming bureaucracy, the examinations, the six ministries, and Neo-Confucian orthodoxy intact; they promoted Chinese officials and paired them with Manchus in every senior post; and they presented themselves to the Chinese as the Mandate's rightful heirs, mourning the Ming and punishing the rebels who had overthrown it. But they also kept the banners as a separate hereditary military caste, forbade Chinese settlement in Manchuria, banned intermarriage, ruled Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang not as Chinese provinces but as separate domains under the emperor as khan, patron of Tibetan Buddhism, and conqueror, and kept their own language for confidential documents.[^2] The Qing was a **multi-ethnic empire** in which China was the largest and richest part but not the whole, and the Manchu emperors' ability to be Confucian sage to the Chinese, Great Khan to the Mongols, and bodhisattva-king to the Tibetans at once is why the empire they built was twice the size of the Ming's, and why the People's Republic's borders are, roughly, the Qing's.

## The three emperors

Three long reigns spanned 134 years and made the High Qing. **Kangxi** (1661–1722), who took personal power at fifteen, put down a great rebellion, took Taiwan, fought the Russians to a treaty at Nerchinsk in 1689 (the first between China and a European power, negotiated in Latin by Jesuit interpreters), crushed the western Mongols, and toured the empire six times; he was a scholar who studied Western astronomy and mathematics with the Jesuits, and a ruler who, in his own account of his reign, said that the emperor's duty was to work and worry without cease.[^3] **Yongzheng** (1722–1735), his son, was an administrator who created the Grand Council, reformed the tax system, and read every memorial himself. **Qianlong** (1735–1796), his grandson, ruled for sixty years and abdicated only so as not to exceed his grandfather's reign, conquered Xinjiang in the 1750s (exterminating the Zunghar Mongols in the process, in what Peter Perdue has called the last great steppe war and a genocide), compiled the largest library project in history (36,000 volumes, with a purge of anti-Manchu texts as its shadow), collected art on a scale no ruler has matched, and received Britain's Macartney embassy in 1793 with an edict informing King George III that China had no need of his country's manufactures.[^4]

Under them, China's population roughly doubled, from perhaps 150 million in 1700 to 300 million or more by 1800, fed by New World crops (maize, sweet potatoes, peanuts) that grew on hillsides rice could not, by internal migration to Sichuan, Yunnan, and Taiwan, and by an eighteenth century that was, in the Chinese heartland, a long peace. The state's revenue held steady while the population grew, which meant the per-capita tax burden fell, and the Qing granary system stabilized prices with an efficiency Europe could not match.[^5] It was, on the evidence of the Jesuits and the philosophers who read them, the best-governed large state in the world, and Voltaire said so.

## Tokugawa Japan: the great peace

Tokugawa Ieyasu and his successors solved the problem that had destroyed every previous Japanese regime: how to keep the daimyo from fighting. They did not abolish them; some 260 domains kept their lords, their samurai, their castles, and their internal government. Instead the shogunate held a quarter of the country directly, including the cities and mines, required every daimyo to spend alternate years in Edo (Tokyo) and leave his family there as hostages, forbade them to build castles or marry without permission, and set them to ruinously expensive ceremonial. The **bakuhan** system, a central military government over semi-autonomous domains, gave Japan 250 years without a war.[^6]

It also sealed the country. Alarmed by Christianity's spread and by Spanish and Portuguese power in the Philippines and Macau, the shogunate banned the religion (1614), expelled the missionaries, crushed a Christian peasant revolt at Shimabara in 1638 with 37,000 dead, and by 1639 had expelled all Europeans except the Dutch, who from 1641 were confined to an artificial island, Dejima, in Nagasaki harbor, allowed one or two ships a year, and required to trample a crucifix. Japanese were forbidden to leave and, if they left, to return. The policy, called **sakoku** ("closed country") by a later writer, was not total: Chinese and Korean trade continued through Nagasaki and Tsushima, Dutch books came in and were studied by a small school of "Dutch learning" scholars, and the Ryukyus and Hokkaido were frontiers. But for two centuries Japan governed its contact with the outside world more tightly than any major state in history.[^7]

Peace made Japan rich and urban. Edo, a fishing village in 1590, held a million people by 1720, the largest city in the world; Osaka and Kyoto had 400,000 each; and perhaps a tenth of Japanese lived in towns, a higher share than any European country but the Netherlands. The samurai, a tenth of the population with no wars to fight, became a hereditary bureaucracy on fixed rice stipends and, increasingly, in debt to the merchants they despised. Rice yields rose, commercial farming spread, literacy reached perhaps 40 percent of men, and a merchant culture flourished in the "floating world" of the cities: kabuki theater, woodblock prints, the haiku of Basho, the novels of Saikaku, and the pleasure quarters that the prints depict.[^8] Neo-Confucianism was the official philosophy; a "national learning" school revived Shinto and the imperial line as Japan's true identity, an idea that would matter in 1868; and the four-class order of samurai, farmer, artisan, merchant was frozen by law and eroded by money.

:::story The Dutch on their island
Dejima was a fan-shaped artificial island of about 9,000 square meters, connected to Nagasaki by one guarded bridge. For 218 years it was Japan's only window on Europe. The Dutch East India Company's dozen or so residents lived there under constant surveillance, forbidden to leave except for the annual journey to Edo to pay homage to the shogun, and they traded silk, sugar, and books for copper and, later, for the isolation itself, which gave them a monopoly. Japanese interpreters attached to the island, at first forbidden to learn to read Dutch, learned it anyway, and from the 1720s, when the shogun relaxed the ban on Western books, they and the scholars who bought the books built **rangaku**, Dutch learning: anatomy (a 1774 Japanese translation of a Dutch anatomical atlas overturned Chinese medicine's picture of the body), astronomy, geography, gunnery, and, from the 1840s, the news that Britain had beaten China.[^9] When the Americans arrived in 1853 (chapter 33), Japan's leaders knew far more about the West than the West knew about Japan, and the reason was a fan-shaped island and the men who translated on it.
:::

## The world in 1800, seen from Asia

Around 1750 the Yangzi delta, Japan's Kanto plain, England, and the Netherlands were the richest regions on Earth, and by most measures historians can reconstruct, life expectancy, calorie intake, urbanization, literacy, market integration, sophistication of commerce and credit, they were close.[^10] China's economy was the world's largest and its state the most stable; Japan's was among the most urbanized and literate; and both were governed by regimes that had solved the problems that had destroyed their predecessors. From Beijing in 1793 the Macartney embassy looked like what it was, a tribute mission from a distant island with nothing China needed, and Qianlong's edict was not arrogance but an accurate description of the trade balance, in which Britain paid silver for tea and sold almost nothing. From Edo the world looked like a danger that had been successfully excluded.

Both views were about to become untenable, for reasons that were forming in Britain at that moment (chapter 21). But the reader should hold the picture: in 1800 East Asia was not backward, decadent, or stagnant, and the Europeans who visited it did not think so. The nineteenth century's catastrophe came to societies at or near their height, and the question of why they could not respond is the question of the next chapter.

:::history How we got here
The Qing were, for most of the twentieth century, the dynasty of decline: Chinese nationalists blamed Manchu backwardness for China's humiliation, Western historians described a stagnant despotism ripe for the impact of the West, and the eighteenth century was a prologue to the Opium War. Two revisions changed this. The "New Qing History" of the 1990s, using Manchu-language archives that Chinese-reading historians had ignored, showed a dynamic, expansionist, self-consciously multi-ethnic empire whose Manchu identity was central, not a veneer, and whose eighteenth-century conquest of Inner Asia was a Eurasian event comparable to Russia's; the Chinese government has criticized this school for questioning the Qing's Chineseness. And the "California School" of economic history (Pomeranz, Wong, Lee and Wang) argued that eighteenth-century China was not stagnant but as commercially developed as Europe, that its population growth was controlled by family strategies rather than Malthusian crisis, and that the divergence came late and from coal and colonies. Both revisions are contested, the second sharply (chapter 29's footnotes give the critics), but together they have replaced the picture of a China waiting to be awakened with one of an empire that was, in 1800, at its peak and about to meet something it had no category for.[^11]
:::

:::key
The High Qing and Tokugawa Japan were the two most successful regimes in East Asian history by the measures their own civilizations used: peace, population, prosperity, and order. The Qing doubled China's territory and population and governed the largest state on Earth with a bureaucracy of 20,000; the Tokugawa gave Japan the longest peace any large society has had and the world's biggest city. Both did it by controlling contact with outsiders on their own terms, and both were built for a world in which they were the center. In 1800 they were; roughly four decades later a power they had classified as a tributary would show that they were not, and the same institutions that had produced the peace would make the response slow.
:::

## Summary

- The Manchus conquered China with a million people, ruled it as Confucian emperors while keeping a separate Manchu military caste, and governed Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang as separate domains; the Qing was a multi-ethnic empire twice the Ming's size.
- Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong gave China 134 years of able rule, a doubled population fed by New World crops, and the best-governed large state of the eighteenth century.
- The Tokugawa kept the daimyo in check by hostage residence and expense, sealed Japan to all Europeans but the Dutch at Dejima, and gave the country 250 years of peace, a million-person capital, high literacy, and a merchant culture.
- Around 1750 the richest regions of China, Japan, and Europe were roughly equally prosperous; Qianlong's reply to Macartney described the trade balance accurately.
- Both regimes were at their height in 1800 and built for a world in which they were the center; the New Qing History and the California School have replaced the old picture of stagnation.
