## Recap

Chapter 33 ended in 1900 with the Qing eleven years from extinction, Japan a great power, and Korea a protectorate-in-waiting. This chapter covers the first half of the twentieth century, in which China had its revolution and then its two decades of chaos and civil war, Japan built an empire and then a war it could not win, and the two collided in the largest war in Asian history. It is the most violent period in East Asia's history, and the one whose memory most shapes the region's politics today.

## The end of the empire

After the Boxer disaster the Qing court finally reformed in earnest: the examination system, fourteen centuries old, was abolished in 1905; a modern army, schools, and provincial assemblies were created; a constitution was promised. The reforms produced the people who overthrew the dynasty. Students sent to Japan came back nationalists and revolutionaries; the new army's officers had no loyalty to Manchus; the provincial assemblies wanted power; and **Sun Yat-sen**, a Western-educated doctor from Guangdong, had spent fifteen years abroad organizing a revolutionary alliance whose program was nationalism, democracy, and people's livelihood. In October 1911 a mutiny in Wuhan spread; province after province declared for the republic; and on 12 February 1912 the six-year-old last emperor, Puyi, abdicated. The **Republic of China** was proclaimed with Sun as provisional president, and two thousand years of imperial rule ended almost without fighting.[^1]

The republic did not work. Sun handed the presidency to Yuan Shikai, the general who controlled the army, who suppressed the parliament, tried to make himself emperor in 1916, and died; his generals then divided China among themselves. The **warlord** era (1916–1928) was a decade of regional armies fighting for territory, taxing peasants to exhaustion, and selling railways and mines to foreigners, while Japan, which had taken Germany's Chinese concessions in 1914 and presented its Twenty-One Demands in 1915, moved into Manchuria. On 4 May 1919 students in Beijing demonstrated against the Versailles settlement's award of Shandong to Japan, and the **May Fourth Movement** became a cultural revolution: against Confucianism, the family, the classical language, and everything old, and for science, democracy, vernacular writing, and whatever Western idea might save China. Among the ideas was Marxism, and the **Chinese Communist Party** was founded in Shanghai in July 1921 by a dozen intellectuals with Soviet help.[^2]

Sun, back in Canton, rebuilt his party as the **Guomindang** (Nationalist Party, KMT) on Soviet lines, with Soviet advisers and money and the Communists inside it as allies. He died in 1925. His military chief, **Chiang Kai-shek**, led the Northern Expedition that reunified China in 1926–1928, and in April 1927, in Shanghai, turned on the Communists and massacred thousands of them. The survivors fled to the countryside, where **Mao Zedong**, a library assistant's son from Hunan who had been at the founding meeting, built a peasant base in the mountains of Jiangxi on land reform and guerrilla war, and when Chiang's armies drove him out in 1934, led the **Long March**, 6,000 miles over a year to the northwest, with perhaps 8,000 of 86,000 surviving, to Yan'an.[^3] Chiang's Nanjing government (1928–1937) built roads, a currency, a central bank, and a modern sector in the cities, and let the countryside, where 80 percent of Chinese lived in deepening poverty, alone.

## Japan's empire

Japan in 1918 was a great power with a parliamentary government, an industrializing economy, and an empire (Taiwan, Korea, southern Manchuria, the Pacific islands). The 1920s were its liberal decade: universal male suffrage (1925), party cabinets, jazz, department stores, and a navy treaty with Britain and America. The Depression ended it. Silk and rice prices collapsed, rural Japan starved, and young army officers, mostly of peasant origin, concluded that party politicians and capitalists had betrayed the nation and that Japan's salvation lay in Manchuria's resources and a military state. In September 1931 officers of the Kwantung Army in Manchuria blew up a section of their own railway, blamed the Chinese, and conquered the whole region in five months without orders from Tokyo, which could not stop them; the puppet state of Manchukuo followed, with Puyi as its emperor.[^4] The League of Nations condemned it and Japan left the League. At home, assassinations of prime ministers and businessmen (1930, 1932, 1936) intimidated the politicians, and by 1937 the army effectively controlled the government under an emperor, Hirohito, whose role in the decisions is still argued.

In July 1937 a skirmish at a bridge near Beijing became a full invasion. Japan took Beijing, Shanghai (after three months of fighting that cost the Chinese army some 200,000 casualties), and in December Nanjing, where over six weeks its troops murdered somewhere between 40,000 and 300,000 Chinese civilians and prisoners and raped tens of thousands of women, in an atrocity whose scale Japanese nationalists have denied and Chinese memory has made central.[^5] Chiang retreated up the Yangzi to Chongqing; Japan held the coast, the cities, and the railways, and could not win the interior, where Chinese armies, Nationalist and Communist, fought on for eight years with American aid, Soviet aid, and their own. The Sino-Japanese War of 1937–1945 killed perhaps 15 to 20 million Chinese, most of them civilians, by massacre, bombing, famine (the Nationalists breached the Yellow River dikes in 1938 to stop the Japanese and drowned or starved several hundred thousand of their own people), forced labor, and Japan's biological warfare unit.[^6]

In 1940 Japan joined Germany and Italy; in 1941, with its oil embargoed by the United States for its war in China, it decided to seize the oil of Southeast Asia and, to protect the seizure, to destroy the American fleet. On 7 December 1941 it struck Pearl Harbor and, within months, took the Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, Burma, and the Dutch East Indies, ending three centuries of European rule in Asia in a hundred days and announcing a Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere that its subjects experienced as forced labor, famine, and massacre.[^7] The Americans stopped the advance at Midway in June 1942, and for three years fought island by island toward Japan while bombing its wooden cities to ash; the firebombing of Tokyo on 9–10 March 1945 killed about 100,000 people in a night. On 6 August 1945 an atomic bomb destroyed Hiroshima and on 9 August a second destroyed Nagasaki; the Soviet Union declared war the same week and overran Manchuria; and on 15 August Hirohito, in the first radio broadcast of an emperor's voice, announced surrender. About 3 million Japanese had died, and Japan's empire, its army, its navy, and its cities were gone.

## Colonial Korea

Japan ruled Korea from 1910 to 1945 as a colony to be assimilated. It built railways, ports, schools, and, in the 1930s, heavy industry in the north; it also took land from Korean farmers for Japanese settlers, banned the Korean language from schools and newspapers by the late 1930s, required Koreans to take Japanese names and worship at Shinto shrines, and conscripted hundreds of thousands into mines, factories, and the army, and tens of thousands of women into military brothels as "comfort women." A nationwide independence demonstration on 1 March 1919 was crushed with some 7,500 killed by Korean count (Japanese records admitted a few hundred). Korean nationalists split between a government-in-exile in Shanghai, Communist guerrillas in Manchuria (among them Kim Il Sung), and collaborators; the division would outlast the colony.[^8] The Japanese colonial legacy in Korea, industrial infrastructure and educated cadres on one side and cultural erasure and forced labor on the other, is the most contested question in Korean historiography and in Japanese-Korean relations today.

:::history How we got here
Japan's road to war has been argued since the Tokyo trials of 1946–1948, which convicted twenty-five leaders and hanged seven while exempting the emperor. The trials' narrative, a militarist clique that hijacked a peaceful nation, served the American occupation, which needed Hirohito, and postwar Japan, which needed to be a victim. Historians since have complicated every part of it: the "clique" was the whole army and much of the civilian elite; the emperor was informed and involved, and Herbert Bix's 2000 biography argued he was a decision-maker, which Japanese conservatives reject; the war in China was popular in Japan; and the decision for Pearl Harbor was made by men who knew they would probably lose and preferred a gamble to retreat from China. On the other side, Chinese historiography under the Communists long minimized the Nationalists' role in fighting Japan, which Western and now Chinese scholarship (Mitter, van de Ven) has restored: Chiang's armies did most of the fighting and dying. The Nanjing death toll, the comfort women, and Japanese school textbooks remain live political disputes between Japan and its neighbors, and how each country remembers 1931–1945 is itself part of East Asian history, and is taken up in chapter 37.[^9]
:::

:::key
In 1900 China was an empire, Japan a rising power, Korea a kingdom. By 1945 China had abolished its empire and fought a revolution, a warlord era, and an eight-year war that killed 15 to 20 million, with its civil war about to resume; Japan had built and lost an empire and was an occupied ruin under two atomic clouds; Korea had lost and was about to regain its independence, divided. The Meiji state, which chapter 33 presented as a success, produced the militarism that destroyed it; the Chinese revolution, which chapter 33 presented as a failure, produced the parties that would rule China after 1949. The judgments of one century are reversed by the next, which is the reason historians distrust them.
:::

## Summary

- The Qing's late reforms produced the students and officers who overthrew it in 1911; the republic collapsed into warlordism, and the May Fourth Movement and the Communist Party (1921) emerged from the disillusion.
- Chiang Kai-shek reunified China, purged the Communists in 1927, and governed the cities; Mao built a peasant revolution and survived the Long March.
- Japan's liberal 1920s ended in the Depression; its army seized Manchuria in 1931 without orders, took over the government by assassination and intimidation, and invaded China in 1937, committing the Nanjing massacre and killing 15 to 20 million Chinese in eight years.
- Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor and conquest of Southeast Asia ended in firebombing, two atomic bombs, Soviet invasion, and surrender in August 1945.
- Korea endured thirty-five years of colonial assimilation, forced labor, and cultural erasure; its nationalists split in ways that would outlast the colony.
