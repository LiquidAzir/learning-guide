## Recap

Chapter 14 left Europe around 1000 as a poor frontier society with its kingdoms newly in place and its population beginning to grow. The next three centuries are the medieval boom. Almost every institution that the modern West regards as its own, from the university to representative assemblies to the common law, was built in them, by people who would have been astonished to hear it.

## Growth

The numbers first. Europe's population roughly doubled between 1000 and 1300, from perhaps 38 million to perhaps 75 million; England's rose from around 1.5 million to perhaps 5 million.[^1] The land under cultivation expanded enormously: forests were cleared, marshes drained, and settlers pushed east of the Elbe into Slavic lands, into Spain behind the Christian advance, and into the Celtic fringes. The climate helped, with the **Medieval Warm Period** giving longer growing seasons; so did technology, the heavy plow, the horse in harness, windmills (from about 1180), and the three-field rotation that left a third of the land fallow instead of half.

Growth made towns. In 1000 almost no town in Latin Europe outside Italy and Spain had 10,000 people; by 1300 Paris had perhaps 200,000, Florence, Venice, Milan, and Genoa around 100,000 each, and there were hundreds of towns of 5,000 to 20,000 with walls, markets, charters of self-government, and **guilds**, associations of merchants or craftsmen that regulated their trade, trained apprentices, and ran the town.[^2] The towns bought their freedoms from kings and lords who needed cash, and their citizens, the **bourgeoisie** (from *bourg*, town), were a third thing in a society that had known only lords, clergy, and peasants. Italian merchants invented double-entry bookkeeping, the bill of exchange, marine insurance, and the commercial company, and Venice and Genoa built maritime empires in the eastern Mediterranean that carried the spices and silks of Asia to the fairs of Champagne and Flanders.[^3] Money, which had almost vanished, returned: Florence's gold florin (1252) and Venice's ducat became Europe's currencies.

## The Church reformed, and the fight with the emperors

Around 1000 the Church was owned. Bishops were appointed by kings and lords who sold the offices; priests married and passed their churches to their sons; the pope was whoever the Roman nobility installed. A reform movement, beginning in the monastery of Cluny and taken over by the papacy from 1049, set out to free the Church from lay control, and it produced the most consequential political conflict of the age.[^4]

Pope Gregory VII (1073–1085) declared that no layman could appoint a bishop, that the pope could depose emperors, and that priests must be celibate. The emperor Henry IV, whose government depended on appointing bishops, declared Gregory deposed; Gregory excommunicated Henry, his princes revolted, and in January 1077 Henry stood barefoot in the snow for three days outside the castle of Canossa begging absolution. He got it, then made war on the pope anyway, drove him from Rome, and died still excommunicate; but the **Investiture Controversy** was settled in 1122 largely on the Church's terms, and the principle that spiritual authority was distinct from secular power, together with the papal claim that it was superior, had been placed at the center of European politics.[^5] The papacy that emerged, under lawyer-popes like Innocent III (1198–1216), was the most sophisticated government in Europe: a court of appeal for the whole continent, a legislature that issued canon law, a tax collector with agents everywhere, and a monarchy that claimed the right to judge kings. It also, from 1231, ran the **Inquisition** to find heretics, and in 1209 launched a crusade against the Cathars of southern France that killed tens of thousands.

The separation of church and state that the West takes for granted comes from this fight: neither side won, so Europe had two authorities where every other civilization had one, and the space between them was where universities, cities, and eventually the idea of liberty grew.

## Crusades

In 1095 Pope Urban II, at Clermont, called on the knights of France to stop fighting each other and go free Jerusalem from the Turks, who had taken it from the Arabs and whose advance was threatening Byzantium. Tens of thousands went, most of them dying on the way; the survivors took Jerusalem in July 1099 and massacred its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, and set up four Latin states in Syria and Palestine that lasted, shrinking, until 1291.[^6] Seven more numbered crusades followed: the Second failed, the Third (1189–1192) pitted Richard the Lionheart against Saladin, who had retaken Jerusalem in 1187, and ended in a truce; the Fourth (1204) never reached the Holy Land and instead sacked Constantinople, the Christian capital, an act from which Byzantium never recovered; the rest achieved less.

What the crusades meant has been argued for two centuries. For nineteenth-century Europeans they were a heroic and colonial prelude; for twentieth-century critics, an early Western aggression; for the Muslim world, largely forgotten until the nineteenth century, they became a symbol of Western intrusion. Current scholarship stresses that they were, for participants, acts of penance, that they were financed at ruinous cost, that they brought little to Europe economically (the trade came through Italian merchants regardless), and that their most enduring effects were the papacy's enhanced authority, the militarization of Christianity, and the pogroms against Jews that accompanied every one of them, beginning in the Rhineland in 1096.[^7] The **Reconquista**, the slow Christian conquest of Muslim Spain, was folded into the same ideology and was, in the long run, the crusade that succeeded: Toledo fell in 1085, Córdoba in 1236, Seville in 1248, leaving only Granada.

## Universities

Around 1100 the West knew Aristotle's logic and almost nothing else of Greek thought. Within a century it had all of Aristotle, plus Euclid, Ptolemy, Galen, and the Arabic commentators, translated from Arabic in Toledo and from Greek in Sicily, and it had built an institution to argue about them. The **university**, from *universitas*, a corporation, was a self-governing guild of masters and students with the right to examine and license; Bologna (law) and Paris (theology) existed by about 1150, Oxford by 1200, and there were some twenty by 1300.[^8] Their method was **scholasticism**: the posing of a question, the marshaling of authorities on both sides, and the resolution by distinction and argument, in Latin, in public disputation. Its masterpiece was Thomas Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274), which set Aristotle and Christian revelation in a single rational system that remains Catholic philosophy's foundation.[^9] Scholasticism has been mocked since the Renaissance for arguing about angels on pinheads (a debate no scholastic ever held), and it was the West's training in rigorous argument. The university's degrees, faculties, lectures, examinations, and gowns are medieval, and so is the idea that learning is a corporation independent of church and state.

## Law and parliaments

Two legal traditions took shape. On the continent, the rediscovery of Justinian's *Digest* at Bologna around 1070 launched the study of **Roman law**, which princes adopted because it served centralized rule, and which the Church paralleled in **canon law**; together they became the civil-law systems of Europe. In England, Henry II (1154–1189) built the **common law**: royal judges traveling the country, applying uniform rules, using juries of local men to establish facts, and recording decisions that bound future cases.[^10] The two systems still divide the world.

Kings who needed money for wars found they had to ask. In 1215 the English barons forced King John to seal **Magna Carta**, a list of feudal grievances containing two clauses, that no free man be imprisoned except by lawful judgment of his peers, and that no tax be levied without the realm's common counsel, that later ages made into constitutional principles.[^11] By 1295 the English king was summoning knights and townsmen alongside lords and bishops to consent to taxation: a **parliament**. Spain's Cortes, France's Estates, the German Diet, and the Polish Sejm were parallel developments. Representative assemblies are a medieval invention, made because kings were weak, not because anyone believed in democracy.

:::story Building a cathedral
Around 1140 Abbot Suger rebuilt the east end of the abbey church of Saint-Denis outside Paris with pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and walls that were mostly stained glass, so that, he wrote, the building seemed to be made of light. The style, later called **Gothic**, spread across Europe in a century: Chartres, Reims, Amiens, Canterbury, Cologne, each town trying to outbuild the last, with vaults of up to forty meters and more held up by flying buttresses that let the walls dissolve into glass.[^12] A great cathedral took fifty to a hundred years and the income of a whole diocese, was designed by master masons who signed nothing and whom we mostly cannot name, and was built by a town of a few thousand as the largest building most of them would ever enter. Beauvais, pushing the vault to forty-eight meters, collapsed in 1284. The cathedrals are the Middle Ages' argument for itself: what a society that had rediscovered geometry, had money to spend, and believed in one thing above all else chose to do with it.
:::

## Where things stood

By 1300 Europe had gone from the edge of the world to a civilization that could match anyone's. It had the largest cities in Latin Christendom since Rome, a commercial network from Flanders to the Levant, a Church that governed a continent, universities that had absorbed Greek and Arabic learning and were producing original work, kingdoms with royal courts and taxation and representative assemblies, and an architecture without precedent. It also had serfs (most peasants, bound to their lord's land), a knightly class whose occupation was violence, expulsions of Jews (England 1290, France 1306), an Inquisition, and a population pressing against the limit of what the land could feed. The next chapter is about what happened when the limit was reached.

:::key
The High Middle Ages built the institutions the modern West thinks of as its own: the university, the parliament, the common law, the chartered city, the corporation, the separation of spiritual and secular authority. None was designed; each was a compromise in a struggle nobody won outright, between popes and emperors, kings and barons, lords and towns. That pattern, of institutions emerging from stalemate, is the distinctive thing about Europe's political development, and it is why the period should be understood as an age of construction, whatever it did to Jerusalem or to the Cathars.
:::

## Summary

- Between 1000 and 1300 Europe's population doubled, forests fell, towns grew from nothing to cities of 100,000, and Italian merchants invented the tools of commerce.
- The papal reform movement freed the Church from lay control, fought the emperors to a draw, and made the papacy Europe's most sophisticated government; the stalemate created the West's separation of church and state.
- The crusades took and lost Jerusalem, sacked Constantinople, and brought pogroms; the Reconquista slowly succeeded.
- Universities arose as self-governing corporations to argue about the newly translated Aristotle; scholasticism was the West's training in rigorous reasoning.
- Roman law and English common law took their lasting shapes, and kings who needed money created parliaments; the Gothic cathedral was the age's monument.
