## Why did seventeenth-century states fight over authority as well as territory?

Chapter 17 left Europe divided by religion and armed; chapter 18 gave it an Atlantic economy and American silver. This chapter follows the seventeenth century, which historians call a general crisis: the worst war Europe had yet fought, revolutions in England, and famine, plague, and cold nearly everywhere. Out of the crisis came two answers to the question of how to govern, the absolute monarchy of Louis XIV and the constitutional settlement of England, and, at the same time, the Scientific Revolution, which is the most important thing that happened in the century and had almost nothing to do with the kings.

## The Thirty Years' War

It began in 1618 with a Protestant revolt in Bohemia against a Catholic Habsburg king, and Bohemian nobles throwing the king's regents out of a window in Prague. It became, over thirty years, a war of all against all in Germany: the Habsburg emperor and Catholic League against the Protestant princes; Denmark, then Sweden, intervening on the Protestant side; Spain on the Catholic; and finally Catholic France, under Cardinal Richelieu, subsidizing the Protestants and then fighting the Habsburgs directly, because dynasty mattered more than religion.[^1] The armies, mostly mercenary, paid themselves by plunder, and Germany was their pasture. Magdeburg was sacked in 1631 and 20,000 of its 25,000 people killed. When the **Peace of Westphalia** ended it in 1648, Germany had lost perhaps a fifth of its population, from about 20 million to 16, to war, famine, and the plague and typhus that marched with the armies; some regions lost more than half, and the older estimate of a third overall is now thought too high.[^2] No European war until the twentieth century approached it.

Westphalia mattered as much as the war. It confirmed that each German prince chose his territory's religion (now including Calvinism), which ended the Reformation's wars by freezing its map. It recognized the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederation as independent. It reduced the Holy Roman Emperor to a figurehead over 300 effectively sovereign states, which is why Germany would not be unified until 1871. And it established, by practice more than declaration, the principle that Europe was a system of **sovereign states**, each supreme within its borders, dealing with each other by treaty and balance of power rather than under any higher authority, papal or imperial.[^3] International relations as the modern world understands it dates from 1648, and the phrase "Westphalian system" is still used for it.

## The English revolutions

England avoided the war and had its own. Charles I (1625–1649) believed he ruled by divine right, tried to govern without Parliament for eleven years, raised money by expedients his subjects thought illegal, and imposed on Presbyterian Scotland a prayer book that provoked a rebellion he could not afford to fight. He called Parliament in 1640 to pay for it; Parliament impeached his ministers, abolished his courts, and demanded control of the army. In 1642 the two sides went to war.[^4]

Parliament won, largely because of Oliver Cromwell's New Model Army, and then split, the army radicals against the Presbyterian majority. The army purged Parliament, tried the king for treason against his people, and on 30 January 1649 beheaded him in front of his own palace: the first public trial and execution of a reigning monarch by his subjects. England was a republic for eleven years, five of them under Cromwell as Lord Protector, and conquered Ireland with a brutality the Irish have not forgotten and Scotland with a thoroughness the Scots have. When Cromwell died in 1658 nobody could hold it together, and in 1660 Charles II was invited back.[^5]

The Restoration did not settle the question. Charles's brother James II was Catholic and had a Catholic son; in 1688 a group of nobles invited William of Orange, the Dutch ruler married to James's Protestant daughter Mary, to invade. He landed with an army, James fled, and Parliament made William and Mary joint monarchs on conditions: the **Bill of Rights** (1689) barred the crown from suspending laws, taxing, or keeping an army without Parliament, and guaranteed free elections and free speech in Parliament. It was called the Glorious Revolution because almost nobody in England died; in Ireland, where James's supporters fought on with French help until 1691, many did.[^6] Its result was a monarchy that governed through Parliament, a national debt guaranteed by Parliament and therefore cheap to borrow (the Bank of England, 1694), a toleration of Protestant dissenters, and, in John Locke's *Two Treatises* (1689), the argument that government exists by consent to protect life, liberty, and property and may be overthrown when it does not.[^7] The American founders read Locke.

## Louis XIV

France took the other road. After the Wars of Religion and a noble revolt (the Fronde, 1648–1653) that terrified the young Louis XIV, he governed for fifty-four years (1661–1715) on the principle that the state was himself. He never called the Estates-General; he broke the nobles' independence by making them courtiers dependent on his favor at **Versailles**, a palace built to hold 10,000 people and to display the king as the sun around which everything turned; he ruled through professional ministers (Colbert for finance, Louvois for war) and royal intendants (salaried agents answerable only to the crown) in the provinces; he built the largest army Europe had seen, 400,000 men, and fought four major wars to expand France's borders and put his grandson on the throne of Spain; and he revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, driving 200,000 Huguenots, many of them France's best craftsmen, into exile in England, Holland, Prussia, and America.[^8]

**Absolutism**, the theory that the king's authority was unlimited by any earthly body because it came from God, was Bishop Bossuet's formulation of what Louis practiced. In fact his power was limited at every turn by distance, custom, privilege, and the impossibility of taxing the nobles and clergy, and his wars left France exhausted and bankrupt; but the model of the centralized bureaucratic monarchy with a standing army and a splendid court was imitated everywhere from Madrid to St Petersburg, where Peter the Great built a capital from nothing and dragged Russia into Europe by the beard.[^9] The eighteenth century's monarchies were Louis's, and so was the revolution that ended them.

## The Scientific Revolution

While the kings fought, a few hundred men across Europe were dismantling the picture of the universe that had stood since Aristotle, and building a method for finding out how things actually work. The story is told in full in the physics guide; here is what it meant for history.

In 1543 Copernicus published the argument that the Earth goes round the Sun. In 1609 Galileo pointed a telescope at the sky and saw mountains on the Moon, moons around Jupiter, and phases of Venus, none of which Aristotle's cosmos allowed; in 1633 the Inquisition made him recant and kept him under house arrest for the rest of his life. Kepler found that planets move in ellipses. Harvey showed the blood circulates. Descartes proposed that all of nature was matter in motion, knowable by reason. Boyle experimented with air pumps and insisted that knowledge came from experiments others could repeat. And in 1687 Isaac Newton published the *Principia*, which showed that a single mathematical law of gravitation explained the fall of an apple, the orbits of the planets, and the tides, and that the universe was a machine whose workings the human mind could read.[^10]

Three things made this a revolution and not just a set of discoveries. **Method**: the insistence that claims about nature be tested against observation and experiment, and expressed in mathematics, rather than deduced from Aristotle or Scripture. **Institutions**: the Royal Society of London (1660) and the Académie des Sciences (1666), which published, reviewed, and rewarded, so that knowledge accumulated across generations rather than dying with its discoverer.[^11] **Confidence**: the conviction that nature was lawful, that the laws could be found, and that finding them would improve human life. That confidence, which Francis Bacon had preached in 1620 and Newton seemed to vindicate, is the seed of the Enlightenment (chapter 20), and it spread to politics, economics, and religion a question no one had asked before: if the heavens can be understood by reason, why not everything?

:::history How we got here
The Scientific Revolution was named in the 1940s, by historians who saw it as the moment Europe broke with the past and the reason for its later dominance. The picture then was of heroic individuals overthrowing superstition. Since about 1980 historians have qualified almost every part of it. Medieval and Islamic scholars had done much of the groundwork; the "revolution" took 150 years and most of its participants were devout, Newton above all; magic, alchemy, and astrology were pursued by the same men as astronomy; the Church's opposition was inconsistent (it employed astronomers and condemned Galileo for defiance as much as doctrine); and the connection to industry and power came a century later. Some argue there was no revolution at all, only a gradual change. The label survives because something did change, decisively and in Europe: the standard for what counts as knowing about nature. Why in Europe and not in China, which had better astronomers in 1400, is debated as fiercely as "why Europe" went to sea, and with as little resolution.[^12]
:::

## Everyone else

The seventeenth century was a hard time to be alive. The **Little Ice Age**, a period of cooler climate from roughly 1300 to 1850, was at its coldest around 1650; harvests failed, the Thames froze, and glaciers advanced on Alpine villages. Famine struck France in 1693–1694 and killed some 1.3 million people, about 6 percent of the population. Plague returned to Italy in 1630 and to London in 1665, its last major visit to England. Witch trials peaked between 1580 and 1650, mostly in Germany, Switzerland, and France, with perhaps 40,000 to 50,000 executions, three quarters of them women, in a panic that fed on war, religious conflict, and bad harvests and burned itself out as courts demanded better evidence.[^13] For the peasants who were 80 percent of Europe, the century of Versailles and the *Principia* was one of cold, hunger, soldiers, and fear.

:::key
The seventeenth century produced two answers to the question the wars of religion had posed: who decides? France answered with the absolute king, England with the sovereign Parliament, and Westphalia answered for Europe as a whole with the sovereign state, none above another. Meanwhile, in the study and the laboratory, a different question was answered: how do we know? The answer, observation, experiment, mathematics, and public testing, had nothing to do with kings and would in time undo them. The century's politics gave Europe its state system and the century's science gave it its confidence, and the eighteenth century was what happened when the confidence was turned on the state.
:::

## Summary

- The Thirty Years' War killed perhaps a fifth of Germany and ended, at Westphalia in 1648, with the religious map frozen, the emperor a figurehead, and Europe recognized as a system of sovereign states.
- England fought a civil war, executed its king, tried a republic, restored the monarchy, and in 1688–1689 settled on a crown governed by Parliament, cheap public credit, and Locke's theory of consent.
- Louis XIV built the model absolute monarchy: Versailles, professional ministers, a 400,000-man army, and religious uniformity; it was imitated across Europe and exhausted France.
- The Scientific Revolution, from Copernicus to Newton, replaced Aristotle's cosmos with a mathematical, testable one and created the method and institutions of modern science.
- For most people the century meant the Little Ice Age, famine, plague, soldiers, and, in its first half, the peak of the witch trials.
