## Recap

Chapter 5 left the Greeks on the edge of the Persian empire, having just adopted the alphabet. This chapter is about what they did with it and with the two centuries of freedom from imperial control that geography gave them. It is the most written-about period in ancient history, and the reason is not that Greeks were cleverer than Babylonians but that they wrote about themselves, argued in public, and left the arguments for us to read.

## A country of small places

Greece is mountains and sea. Its arable land comes in small pockets separated by ridges, its coast is endless, and its islands are everywhere. The unit of Greek life was therefore the **polis** (plural *poleis*), a self-governing community of a town and its fields, typically a few thousand people, and there were about a thousand of them, from Spain to the Black Sea, sharing a language, gods, and games and agreeing on almost nothing else.[^1] No polis ever united Greece. That failure is the reason for everything else: with no king and no empire, Greeks had to work out among themselves how to be governed, and they tried everything.

The **Iron Age** recovery after 800 BCE brought population growth, colonization (Greeks founded cities from Marseille to Crimea between 750 and 550, relieving land hunger at home and spreading the alphabet), trade, coinage (borrowed from Lydia around 600), and the first written laws. It also brought the **hoplite**, the citizen infantryman with round shield and spear who fought in a tight formation, the phalanx, in which each man's shield protected his neighbor.[^2] The phalanx needed farmers who could afford armor and would stand in line, and men who fought for the polis expected a voice in it. The military and the political revolution were the same event.

## Sparta and Athens

Two poleis dominate the record because they dominated Greece, and they were opposites.

**Sparta** solved the land problem by conquering its neighbors, the Messenians, around 700 BCE and turning them into **helots**, state serfs who farmed for their masters. To hold down a subject population many times its size, Sparta made itself a barracks: boys left home at seven for military training, men ate in common messes until sixty, and the whole citizen body, never more than about 8,000, was a professional army whose only occupation was war and whose annual ritual included a formal declaration of war on the helots.[^3] It worked for three centuries. Sparta was the strongest land power in Greece, was admired by other Greeks for its discipline and its stability, and produced almost no literature, art, or philosophy.

**Athens** took the other road. Around 594 BCE the lawgiver Solon canceled debts, abolished debt slavery, and opened offices to men by wealth rather than birth. A tyrant, Pisistratus, ruled for a generation and built the city. In 508 BCE, after the tyranny fell, Cleisthenes reorganized the citizenry into ten artificial tribes that cut across the old clan loyalties, and made the assembly of all citizens the sovereign body. This is the conventional birth of **democracy**, "rule by the people" (*demos*), and over the next fifty years it deepened: officials chosen by lot, jury courts of hundreds of citizens paid to serve, generals elected annually, and any citizen able to speak and vote in an assembly that met forty times a year and decided war, peace, and taxation directly.[^4]

Two qualifications are required and the Athenians would have made them. The people were adult male citizens: about 30,000 to 50,000 in a population of perhaps 300,000, the rest being women, resident foreigners, and slaves, who may have been a third of the total. And the democracy was direct, not representative; it executed generals for losing, ostracized statesmen by popular vote, and put Socrates to death for what he said. Its critics, who included most of the philosophers whose works survive, called it rule by the mob. Its defenders, in the funeral speech Thucydides gives Pericles, called it a city that regarded "him who takes no part in these duties not as unambitious but as useless."[^5]

## The Persian Wars

In 499 the Greek cities of the Anatolian coast, under Persian rule since 546, revolted, with Athenian help. Darius crushed them and in 490 sent a fleet to punish Athens. At **Marathon** the Athenian hoplites charged the Persian line and broke it; the runner who carried the news is legend, but the victory of a citizen army over the world's greatest empire was real and became the founding story of Athenian confidence.[^6]

Ten years later Darius's son Xerxes came in person with the largest army the Mediterranean had seen, perhaps 200,000 men and 600 ships (Herodotus says two million and 1,200, which is the round-number problem of chapter 3). Sparta's king Leonidas and 300 Spartans, with several thousand allies, held the pass of **Thermopylae** for three days and died. Athens was abandoned and burned. Then, at **Salamis** in September 480, the Athenian fleet, built with the profits of a new silver mine on the proposal of Themistocles, destroyed the Persian navy in the narrows, and at **Plataea** the next year a Greek army under Spartan command destroyed the land force.[^7] Persia never invaded Greece again. Herodotus of Halicarnassus, who grew up a Persian subject, wrote the history of these wars a generation later, asking not just what happened but why, interviewing survivors, weighing accounts, and admitting when he did not know; his book is the first work of history in the sense this guide uses the word, and he is why we have a word for it.[^8]

## The fifty years

Between Plataea and 431 BCE, Athens became the dominant sea power, leading an alliance against Persia that it converted, step by step, into an empire: allies became tributaries, their tribute paid for the Athenian fleet and, from 447, for the Parthenon.[^9] Pericles led the democracy for thirty years. The city that Xerxes had burned became the center of the Greek world: Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides wrote tragedies for the festival of Dionysus, in which the whole citizenry watched kings destroyed by their own choices; Aristophanes mocked the politicians by name in comedies that were also state-funded; Herodotus read his history aloud; Socrates asked questions in the marketplace; Hippocrates of Cos, in the same generation, argued that disease had natural causes; and sculptors and architects produced the buildings and statues that Europe would imitate for two thousand years.[^10] It lasted one lifetime.

## The war that ended it

In 431 Athens and Sparta went to war, each with its allies, and fought, with a pause, for twenty-seven years. Thucydides, an Athenian general who was exiled for losing a campaign, wrote its history, and his book is the other founding text of the discipline: no gods, no oracles, only men acting from fear, honor, and interest, and a determination to record what was said and done accurately, so that his work would "be judged useful by those inquirers who desire an exact knowledge of the past as an aid to the interpretation of the future, which in the course of human things must resemble if it does not reflect it."[^11]

The war's shape: Sparta ravaged Athens's fields each summer; Athens, behind its walls and fed by sea, raided the coasts. A plague killed a third of Athens, including Pericles. Athens crushed a rebellious island and massacred its men; Thucydides gives the Athenian envoys at Melos the speech in which they tell the islanders that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." In 415 Athens sent an enormous expedition to conquer Sicily and lost all of it. Persia financed a Spartan fleet. In 405 that fleet caught the Athenian navy beached at Aegospotami and destroyed it, and in 404 Athens surrendered, tore down its walls, and lost its empire.[^12] It kept its democracy, which the victors briefly abolished and the Athenians restored within a year. Sparta's supremacy lasted a generation before Thebes broke it in 371. Greece then fought itself into exhaustion until, in 338 at Chaeronea, Philip II of Macedon, a kingdom the Greeks had considered half-barbarian, defeated the combined armies of Athens and Thebes and ended the age of the independent polis. His son was Alexander, and he is the next chapter.

## Philosophy

While the cities fought, some Greeks asked questions that had no precedent. From about 600 BCE, thinkers in the Anatolian Greek cities asked what the world was made of and answered without reference to gods: water (Thales), an infinite substance (Anaximander), atoms and void (Democritus). Pythagoras made number the key to nature. Parmenides argued that change was impossible and Heraclitus that it was the only thing there was; Zeno made puzzles about motion that took two thousand years to solve ([chapter 7 of the mathematics guide](#/mathematics/calculus-change)).[^13]

**Socrates** (469–399) asked instead how one should live, and asked it by cross-examining anyone who claimed to know, until Athens, which had just lost a war and blamed the intellectuals, tried him for impiety and corrupting the young and made him drink hemlock. His student **Plato** (c. 428–348) wrote dialogues in which Socrates is the main speaker, founded the Academy, and argued that the world we see is a shadow of a realm of perfect forms knowable by reason; his *Republic* imagines a city ruled by philosophers and is the first work of political theory. Plato's student **Aristotle** (384–322) rejected the forms, insisted on observation, and wrote on logic, physics, biology, ethics, politics, poetry, and rhetoric with a range no one has matched; his logic was the framework of European thought until the nineteenth century, and his biology was not superseded until the seventeenth.[^14] The three of them lived within a century of each other, in one city of thirty thousand voters, and the question of why that city produced them is one historians have never satisfactorily answered.

:::key
Athens is the most consequential small town in history, and the reason is not any single achievement but the combination: a citizenry that governed itself directly, argued in public, funded art and drama from the state treasury, and wrote down what it thought. The arguments about democracy's dangers, about whether might makes right, about what justice is and whether the state may kill a man for his opinions, were had there first and in forms we still use. That Athens was also a slave society and an imperial power that destroyed weaker cities is not a footnote; it is part of what makes it instructive. The Greeks did not invent freedom. They invented the argument about it.
:::

## Summary

- Greek geography produced a thousand self-governing city-states that never united, forcing experiments in government; the hoplite phalanx tied military service to political voice.
- Sparta became a barracks state resting on serf labor; Athens, through Solon, Cleisthenes, and Pericles, built a direct democracy of male citizens that excluded women, foreigners, and slaves.
- The Greeks repelled two Persian invasions (490, 480–479); Herodotus's account of the wars is the first work of history.
- Athens's fifty-year golden age produced tragedy, comedy, the Parthenon, and Socrates, funded by an empire over its former allies; the Peloponnesian War (431–404) destroyed that empire and Thucydides recorded it without gods.
- Greek philosophy moved from asking what the world is made of to how one should live; Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle framed the questions Europe would argue for two millennia.
