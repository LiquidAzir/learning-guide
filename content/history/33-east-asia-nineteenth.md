## Why did nineteenth-century China and Japan respond differently to foreign pressure?

Chapter 32 left China and Japan in 1800 at their height and closed to the world on their own terms. This chapter is about what happened when the world, in the form of the industrialized West, forced them open: China's nineteenth century of defeat, rebellion, and failed reform, Japan's astonishing transformation after 1868, and Korea's fate between them. It is the century that made modern East Asia and it is remembered very differently in Beijing and Tokyo.

## Opium

Britain drank tea, all of it Chinese, and paid in silver, because China wanted nothing Britain made. By the 1780s the East India Company had found something China's people did want: **opium**, grown in Bengal, smuggled into Canton through Chinese dealers in defiance of Qing law, and by the 1830s the largest commodity trade in the world, some 40,000 chests a year, enough to reverse the silver flow and drain China of the metal its taxes were paid in.[^1] In 1839 the Qing sent an incorruptible official, Lin Zexu, to Canton to stop it; he confiscated and destroyed 20,000 chests and wrote a letter to Queen Victoria asking how she could permit a trade her own country forbade. Britain sent a fleet.

The **First Opium War** (1839–1842) was the first demonstration of what industrial war meant. British steam gunboats went where Chinese junks could not, British rifles and artillery outranged everything the Qing had, and the Qing military, banner and Green Standard troops who had not fought a modern enemy, lost every engagement. The Treaty of Nanjing (1842) ceded Hong Kong, opened five ports to British trade and residence, and imposed an indemnity, and the supplementary treaties of 1843 fixed China's tariffs at about 5 percent and established **extraterritoriality**: British subjects in China would be tried by British law. France and the United States demanded and got the same. A second war (1856–1860), over the opium trade's legalization and other grievances, ended with British and French troops looting and burning the Qing summer palace outside Beijing and the Convention of Beijing opening the whole country. The **unequal treaties**, as the Chinese would call them, made China a semi-colony: sovereign in name, but with its tariffs set by foreigners, its ports governed by foreign councils, its coast patrolled by foreign gunboats, and its opium trade legal.[^2] Every Chinese government since has defined itself against them.

## The Taiping

The Qing's worse enemy was internal. Hong Xiuquan, a village schoolteacher from Guangdong who had failed the examinations four times, had a vision after reading Christian tracts that he was the younger brother of Jesus, sent to destroy the Manchu demons and build the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace, **Taiping Tianguo**. In 1851 his followers, poor Hakka farmers and miners, rose in Guangxi; by 1853 they had taken Nanjing with an army of half a million and held it for eleven years, ruling a state across the Yangzi valley that abolished private property, foot-binding, opium, and the Confucian classics, separated men and women, and fought the Qing to the edge of collapse. The **Taiping Rebellion** (1850–1864) was the largest civil war in history: the best estimates of deaths, from fighting, massacre, famine, and disease, run from 20 to 30 million, and some regions of the lower Yangzi lost half their people.[^3] It was suppressed not by the Qing's own armies, which were useless, but by regional forces raised by Chinese gentry-officials, above all Zeng Guofan's Hunan Army, financed by new provincial taxes and armed with Western guns. The dynasty survived, but the men who had saved it, not the court, now had the armies and the money, and the pattern of provincial military power under nominal central rule would produce the warlords of chapter 34.

The Taiping were not alone. The Nian rebellion in the north, Muslim rebellions in Yunnan and the northwest that killed millions more, and the Panthay and Dungan wars kept China at war for a quarter of a century. By the 1870s the Qing had reconquered everything and lost the demographic and fiscal gains of the eighteenth century.

## Self-strengthening and its failure

The Qing did try to respond. The **Self-Strengthening Movement** of the 1860s to 1890s, led by the provincial officials who had beaten the Taiping, built arsenals, shipyards, a navy, telegraphs, a steamship company, coal mines, a translation bureau, and schools for Western learning, on the principle of "Chinese learning as the essence, Western learning for practical use." It produced, by 1890, a fleet larger than Japan's. It did not produce a modern state: the court, dominated after 1861 by the Empress Dowager **Cixi**, a former concubine who ruled through child emperors for forty-seven years, kept the examination system, the Confucian curriculum, and the fiscal structure intact, treated Western learning as a set of tools, and is said to have diverted naval funds to rebuild the summer palace.[^4] In 1894–1895 Japan, which had begun its own reform a generation later, destroyed the Qing navy and army in the **Sino-Japanese War**, took Taiwan, and forced China to recognize Korea's independence; the shock was worse than the Opium Wars, because the victor was the small Asian neighbor China had regarded as a cultural dependent for two thousand years. A last reform effort in 1898 lasted a hundred days before Cixi ended it; the anti-foreign **Boxer** rising of 1900, which the court backed, ended with eight foreign armies in Beijing and another indemnity. The dynasty had eleven years left.

## Japan: the Meiji Restoration

In July 1853 Commodore Matthew Perry of the United States Navy anchored four warships, two of them steam-powered and black with smoke, in Edo Bay and demanded that Japan open its ports. The shogunate, which knew from Dejima what had happened to China, signed a treaty in 1854 and, by 1858, the same unequal treaties China had: open ports, fixed tariffs, extraterritoriality. The humiliation destroyed it. Samurai from the outer domains of Satsuma and Choshu, under the slogan "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians," fought the shogunate and, in January 1868, seized the imperial palace in Kyoto, declared the shogun's powers returned to the fifteen-year-old emperor, and won a short civil war. The **Meiji Restoration** was a coup by a few hundred young samurai in the name of an emperor who had reigned without ruling for a thousand years.[^5]

What they then did has no parallel. The men who had said "expel the barbarians" decided, within months, that Japan could survive only by becoming what the barbarians were, and they did it in a generation. The domains were abolished (1871) and replaced by prefectures; the samurai class was abolished, its stipends converted to bonds, its swords banned, and a conscript army of peasants created (1873); the land tax was fixed in money on individual owners; the calendar, dress, currency, and legal codes were Westernized; a national school system was built and by 1905 enrolled over 90 percent of children; railways, telegraphs, a postal service, a central bank, and state-founded factories (later sold to private conglomerates, the *zaibatsu*) followed; missions went abroad to study every Western institution and bring back the best of each (the army from Prussia, the navy from Britain, the law from France and Germany, the schools from America); and in 1889 a constitution, modeled on Prussia's, created a parliament with limited powers under an emperor who was sovereign and sacred.[^6] The slogan was **fukoku kyohei**, rich country, strong army, and the purpose was explicit: to revise the unequal treaties, which Japan achieved by 1899, and to become a power the West had to treat as an equal, which it achieved by beating China in 1895 and Russia in 1905.

The costs were real. The peasantry paid for industrialization in taxes and conscription; the textile mills ran on the labor of girls indentured from farms; the samurai who lost everything rose in 1877 and some 12,000 died; and the state that had been built to resist Western imperialism became, with Taiwan in 1895, Korea in 1910, and Manchuria in 1931, an imperial power itself.

:::history How we got here
Why did Japan succeed and China fail? The question has structured the field since the 1890s, and the answers have shifted with it. The first answers were cultural: Japan was flexible and China was rigid, or Japan had a warrior ethic that valued strength and China a scholar ethic that valued texts. Then structural: Japan was small, unified, and used to borrowing (it had done it from China in the seventh century); China was vast, decentralized after the Taiping, and had never needed to borrow anything. Then institutional: the Tokugawa system had already produced a national market, high literacy, a professional (if idle) administrative class, and, crucially, a shogunate that could be overthrown and an emperor who could be used, whereas the Qing court was the state and could not be replaced without destroying it. Then the question itself was challenged: China's response was not failure but a different timing, and the reforms of 1901–1911, the republic, and the Communist state were the same modernization arriving later and under worse conditions; and Japan's "success" produced a militarist empire that destroyed itself in 1945 and killed millions of Chinese on the way. The current view keeps the institutional answer, stresses that the Qing faced the Taiping while Japan faced only Perry, and notes that the Meiji leaders' greatest asset was that the West arrived in Japan fifteen years after China and they could watch.[^7]
:::

## Korea between

Joseon Korea, Confucian, isolated, and a Qing tributary, watched both and could copy neither. Japan forced it open in 1876 with a Perry-style treaty; China and Japan then competed for it for twenty years; reformers and conservatives fought at court; a peasant religious rising, the Donghak, brought in both Chinese and Japanese troops in 1894 and gave Japan its war with China. Queen Min, who had leaned on Russia, was murdered by Japanese agents in her palace in 1895. In 1897 the king proclaimed himself emperor of a Korean Empire to assert independence; in 1905, after beating Russia, Japan made Korea a protectorate; and in 1910 it annexed the country outright.[^8] Korea's nineteenth century ended with the loss of its independence for thirty-five years, and Korean history since has been shaped by the memory.

:::key
The nineteenth century divided East Asia's three societies by their responses to one shock. China, the largest and proudest, took the shock first and worst, could not respond because the response required dismantling the state that was responding, and spent the century in defeat, civil war, and half-reform; the memory of it, the "century of humiliation," is the founding narrative of the People's Republic. Japan took the shock fifteen years later, overthrew its government, and built a modern state and empire in a generation, at the cost of its peasants and, eventually, its neighbors. Korea, caught between, lost its independence. The three trajectories were set by 1900 and they run through the rest of the section.
:::

## Summary

- Britain balanced its tea trade with opium, China tried to stop it, and the Opium Wars (1839–1842, 1856–1860) imposed the unequal treaties, treaty ports, fixed tariffs, and extraterritoriality that made China a semi-colony.
- The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), led by a failed examination candidate who believed himself Christ's brother, was the largest civil war in history, killed 20 to 30 million, and was suppressed by provincial armies that the court never again controlled.
- Self-strengthening built arsenals and a navy without changing the state; Japan destroyed both in 1895, and the Boxer disaster of 1900 left the Qing eleven years.
- Perry's ships in 1853 brought Japan the same treaties; the Meiji Restoration of 1868 overthrew the shogunate, abolished the domains and the samurai, and built a modern state, army, and economy in a generation, becoming a great power by 1905 and an empire.
- Korea was forced open by Japan in 1876, fought over by China and Japan, and annexed in 1910; why Japan succeeded and China did not is the century's central question.
