## Recap

Chapter 1 said the past leaves traces beyond writing. This chapter is built from them alone: stone tools, bones, seeds, pollen, and, since 2010, the DNA of the dead. It covers the longest stretch of the human story and the two events without which nothing else in this guide happens: the spread of our species across the planet, and the invention of agriculture.

## The long beginning

The first stone tools appear in East Africa about 3.3 million years ago, before our genus; *Homo* itself, whose story is this chapter's span, appears about 2.5 million years ago.[^1] *Homo erectus*, from about 1.9 million years ago, made better tools, used fire, walked upright with a body much like ours, and spread from Africa to Java and China. Our own species, *Homo sapiens*, is African, and the oldest fossils generally accepted as ours are about 300,000 years old, from Morocco.[^2] For most of that time we shared the planet with other kinds of human: Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, Denisovans in eastern Asia, small-bodied populations on the islands of Flores and Luzon. Ancient DNA shows that we met them and had children with them. Every living person outside Africa carries about 2 percent Neanderthal DNA, from encounters dated to roughly 50,000 years ago; people in New Guinea and Australia carry several percent Denisovan.[^3] By 40,000 years ago the others were gone, for reasons still argued: competition, climate, absorption, or some mixture.

*Homo sapiens* left Africa in waves, the successful one beginning around 60,000 to 70,000 years ago, and reached Australia by about 50,000 years ago, crossing open sea to do it; Europe by 45,000; the Americas, across a land bridge from Siberia, by at least 16,000 and possibly considerably earlier.[^4] These people were **hunter-gatherers**, living in small mobile bands, and they were us: the same brains, the same capacities. The cave paintings of Lascaux and Chauvet in France (about 17,000 and 36,000 years old) and of Sulawesi in Indonesia (over 45,000) are art by any standard.[^5] The oldest known musical instruments, bone flutes from Germany, are 40,000 years old. Whatever it is that makes humans human was fully present forty millennia before the first city.

## What hunter-gatherers were like

For two centuries the standard picture was Thomas Hobbes's: life before civilization was "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." That picture was overturned by fieldwork among surviving foraging peoples in the 1960s and by the archaeology of prehistoric health, and the revised version has itself been qualified since.[^6]

What the evidence supports: foragers worked fewer hours to feed themselves than most farmers, ate a more varied diet, and were taller and had better teeth than the farmers who followed them. Violence existed, at rates that varied enormously between groups and periods; some prehistoric cemeteries show many skeletons with weapon wounds, others almost none. Life expectancy at birth was low, around thirty, but that figure is dragged down by infant mortality; a forager who survived childhood could expect to live into their sixties. Social organization was flexible: the same people might live in large gatherings in one season and small bands in another, and the archaeological record of the last ice age includes monumental sites, elaborate burials, and long-distance trade in shells and stone that do not fit any picture of simple egalitarian bands.[^7]

:::history How we got here
The idea that human history is a ladder, from savagery through barbarism to civilization, was the nineteenth century's organizing assumption, and it made sense: the peoples Europeans encountered without cities or writing were assumed to be living fossils of Europe's own past. Twentieth-century anthropology dismantled it in stages. Foraging peoples turned out to have complex kinship, law, and cosmology; the archaeology showed that farming brought worse health, not better; and ancient DNA showed that prehistoric populations moved, mixed, and replaced each other constantly, rather than each region's people evolving in place. The current view is that there was no ladder, that societies moved between forms of organization in many directions, and that "complexity" is not a single dimension. The 2021 book *The Dawn of Everything* pushed this argument furthest and drew criticism for overstating it; the debate is live.[^8]
:::

## The Neolithic revolution

Around 11,700 years ago the last ice age ended, the climate warmed and stabilized, and within a few thousand years, in at least seven separate regions that had no contact with each other, people began to farm. In the **Fertile Crescent** (the arc from the Levant through Syria to Mesopotamia) wheat, barley, lentils, sheep, and goats, from about 9500 BCE. In China, rice in the Yangzi valley and millet in the Yellow River valley, from about 8000 BCE. In New Guinea, taro and bananas. In Mesoamerica, maize, squash, and beans, from about 7000 BCE. In the Andes, potatoes and llamas. In Africa's Sahel, sorghum and pearl millet. In eastern North America, sunflowers and squash.[^9] The independence of these inventions is the strongest evidence that farming was not a stroke of genius but a response to conditions that recurred: a stable warm climate, populations dense enough that wild food was under pressure, and plants that happened to be domesticable.

**Domestication** is a genetic change in the plant or animal produced by human selection, often unintentional: wheat whose seeds stay on the stalk instead of scattering (useless for the wild plant, ideal for the harvester) will be over-represented in what gets replanted. It took centuries to millennia, and it was preceded by a long period of managing wild plants without farming them. The transition was a gradient, not an event.

Why did people do it, if it made them work harder and eat worse? The honest answer is that the question assumes a choice nobody made. Each step was locally sensible: storing wild grain, planting a little near the camp, staying longer where the planting was. Populations grew because settled women bore more children; once they had grown, the band could not go back to foraging, because the land could no longer feed that many foragers. Farming was a trap that closed slowly, and it closed everywhere it started.[^10]

:::key
The Neolithic revolution was the most consequential change in human history and it made most individual humans worse off for several thousand years: shorter, sicker, more crowded, more unequal, and working longer. It won anyway, because farming societies could feed more people per square mile and therefore outnumbered, displaced, or absorbed their neighbors. History is full of changes that were bad for most people and unstoppable for that reason, and this is the first.
:::

## What farming brought

**Villages, then towns.** Çatalhöyük in Anatolia held several thousand people by 7000 BCE, in mud-brick houses entered through the roof, with the dead buried under the floors and wall paintings of bulls and vultures.[^11] Jericho had a stone wall and tower by 8000 BCE. These were not cities: no palaces, no temples clearly distinguishable from houses, no obvious rulers. Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey, with its carved stone pillars arranged in circles, was built around 9500 BCE by people who had not yet fully adopted farming, which upended the assumption that monuments require agricultural surplus.[^12]

**Property, inequality, and inheritance.** Foragers own little and share much, because a mobile life allows nothing else. Farmers own land, stores, animals, and houses, and can pass them on. Skeletal evidence from Neolithic cemeteries shows, within a few centuries of farming's arrival, the first systematic differences in diet and burial between rich and poor. Ancient DNA from Neolithic Europe shows that men buried with wealth had more descendants, and that some communities were organized around male lineages that controlled land and married in women from outside.[^13]

**Disease.** Living in dense settlements with animals and their own waste, farmers acquired the infectious diseases that would shape all later history: tuberculosis, measles, smallpox, and plague are all diseases of settled life, several of them jumping from domestic animals.[^14] The germs that Europeans would carry to the Americas in 1492 were the long consequence of the Neolithic.

**Migration.** Farming did not spread mainly by neighbors learning it. It spread mainly by farmers moving. Ancient DNA shows that the farmers who reached Britain around 4000 BCE descended largely from Anatolians who had begun moving west three thousand years earlier, and that they largely replaced the foragers they met; a second great replacement, by pastoralists from the steppe north of the Black Sea, followed around 2500 BCE and brought, most linguists now think, the Indo-European languages that most Europeans speak.[^15] The prehistoric world was not static. It was a world of movement, on a scale nobody guessed until the genomes were read.

## The steppe, and the horse

The **Eurasian steppe**, the belt of grassland from Hungary to Manchuria, becomes a recurring actor in this guide, and its role begins here. Its peoples were managing horses by about 3500 BCE, and by about 3000 BCE the **Yamnaya** culture north of the Black Sea combined herding with wheeled wagons into a mobile way of life that could exploit the whole grassland; when riding began, and how much it powered the Yamnaya expansion, is argued, since the lineage of all modern domestic horses spread only from about 2200 BCE, though some Yamnaya skeletons show signs of riding.[^16] Their expansion, west into Europe and east toward Siberia and eventually India, is the migration ancient DNA detected, and it is the likely origin of the language family that includes English, Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Persian, and Russian: a family whose relationships had been noticed by linguists since 1786 and whose homeland was debated for two centuries before the genomes settled much of the argument.[^17] Every steppe people after them, Scythians, Huns, Turks, Mongols, played a version of the same part: mobile, militarily formidable, and periodically decisive for the settled civilizations on the steppe's edges.

## On the threshold

By 4000 BCE the world had farmers on every inhabited continent, towns of thousands in several regions, long-distance trade, metallurgy in copper and soon bronze, monumental building, and social inequality. It did not yet have cities, states, or writing. Those appear, first, in the river valleys of Mesopotamia and Egypt, and they are the subject of the next chapter. But it is worth pausing on how much of the human story was already written by then. The peopling of the planet, the extinction of our cousins, the invention of art and music and religion, the domestication of every major crop and animal we still depend on, and the diseases and inequalities we still live with: all of it happened before anyone could record it, and we know it only because the dead kept their bones.

:::know Dates to hold onto
- c. 300,000 years ago: oldest *Homo sapiens* fossils, Morocco.
- c. 60,000–50,000: the successful migration out of Africa; Australia reached.
- c. 51,000: oldest known figurative art (Sulawesi). c. 45,000: *Homo sapiens* in Europe.
- c. 40,000: Neanderthals gone.
- c. 16,000 or earlier: the Americas.
- 11,700: end of the last ice age.
- c. 9500 BCE: farming begins in the Fertile Crescent; Göbekli Tepe.
- c. 8000 BCE: rice and millet farming in China.
- c. 7000 BCE: maize in Mesoamerica; Çatalhöyük.
- c. 3000 BCE: Yamnaya expansion begins; horse domestication is dated anywhere from 3500 to 2200 BCE.
:::

## Summary

- *Homo sapiens* is about 300,000 years old, African in origin, and spread across the world from about 60,000 years ago, interbreeding with and outlasting Neanderthals and Denisovans.
- Ice-age hunter-gatherers were fully modern in capacity: art, music, trade, and complex social forms are all older than farming.
- Farming was invented independently in at least seven regions after the ice age ended; it spread mostly by farmers migrating, and it made most people's lives harder while making their societies larger.
- Settled life brought villages, property, inheritance, systematic inequality, and the infectious diseases of later history.
- Ancient DNA has revealed repeated large-scale migrations and population replacements in prehistory, including the steppe expansion that likely spread the Indo-European languages.
