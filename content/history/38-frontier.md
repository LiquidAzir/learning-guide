## What can new methods change about an old historical argument?

Every section of this guide has cited findings that did not exist in 2005: the genetic replacement of Britain's Neolithic farmers, the Kyrgyz origin of the Black Death, the three-year drought that ended the Hittites, the Maya city found under Campeche's canopy. This chapter steps back to survey the methods behind them, what they have changed, where they have gone wrong, and what is coming. It is the chapter that the research feed on this subject's page updates.

## Ancient DNA

The technique that has changed the most: extracting and sequencing DNA from bones and teeth of the long dead. The first Neanderthal genome was published in 2010 by Svante Pääbo's laboratory, which won him the 2022 Nobel Prize; by 2024 more than 10,000 ancient human genomes had been sequenced, and the number doubles every few years as costs fall and the petrous bone of the inner ear, which preserves DNA best, is sampled systematically.[^1] What the genomes have shown, in rough order of impact:

- **Prehistory was migration.** Europe's population was replaced almost wholesale twice, by Anatolian farmers around 6000 BCE and by steppe pastoralists around 2500 BCE (chapter 2); Britain's Neolithic builders were replaced by newcomers within a few centuries; the Indo-European languages spread with the second migration, whose source population has now been located in the Caucasus–Lower Volga region around 4400–4000 BCE.[^2] The archaeologists' assumption of the 1970s to 2000s, that cultural change happened in place through the diffusion of ideas, was wrong for most of the cases tested.
- **We are all mixed.** No living population descends from a single ancient one; every people is a blend of several migrations, and the genetic clusters of the deep past do not correspond to any modern nation or race.
- **Kinship can be read.** Family trees have been reconstructed in Neolithic tombs, showing patrilineal descent and women marrying in; a 4,000-year-old Chinese walled city has yielded 144 genomes and its elite's lineage structure; the Xiongnu's aristocratic women have been shown to come from narrower lineages than their subjects.
- **Disease has a history.** The plague bacterium has been sequenced from Bronze Age, Justinianic, Black Death, and later graves, its family tree drawn, and its origin located; smallpox, hepatitis B, leprosy, and tuberculosis have their own ancient genomes.

And the errors: the 2022 claim that the Black Death selected for immune-gene variants was challenged in 2025 on statistical grounds; the 2023 claim of a near-extinction bottleneck 900,000 years ago from modern genomes has been contested by reanalyses. Population genetics rests on models, and the models can be wrong in ways that produce confident, publishable, false results.[^3]

## Dating

**Radiocarbon dating** depends on knowing how much carbon-14 was in the atmosphere when the sample was alive, which has varied; the calibration curve that converts raw radiocarbon ages to calendar years was revised in 2020 (IntCal20) and shifted thousands of published dates by decades to centuries.[^4] **Tree rings** give exact years and, through their width and isotopes, annual climate: the Hittite drought of 1198–1196 BCE, the Norse presence in Newfoundland in exactly 1021 CE (dated by a cosmic-ray spike of 993 that marks one ring in every tree alive then), the Ming's Wanli megadrought of the 1580s. **Ice cores** record volcanic eruptions (the 536 and 540 CE double eruption that opened the Late Antique Little Ice Age) and pollution (Roman lead smelting, now converted into an estimate of the empire-wide drop in children's IQ). **Speleothems**, cave deposits, record rainfall year by year for millennia and have dated the floods that ended Liangzhu and the droughts that ended the Maya. Climate has become a historical actor with a documented record, and the collapses of chapters 4, 13, 16, and 31 all now have climate evidence beside the texts.

## Seeing through things

**LiDAR**, airborne laser scanning that strips forest canopy from the terrain in the data, has mapped tens of thousands of unknown structures in the Maya lowlands, garden cities in the Ecuadorian Amazon occupied from 500 BCE, and Khmer and Southeast Asian landscapes, and has multiplied estimates of pre-Columbian populations several times over (chapter 11). **Satellite radar** found the lost Nile branch along which the pyramids were built. **Multispectral imaging** reads erased and overwritten manuscripts: forty-two lost pages of a sixth-century Greek codex of Paul's letters were recovered in 2026 from the mirror-image ink they had left on facing pages.[^5] And **X-ray tomography with machine learning** has begun to read the Herculaneum scrolls, carbonized in 79 CE and unopenable for two thousand years: the first words in 2023, a prize-winning passage of Epicurean philosophy in 2024, the title and author of a still-rolled scroll (Philodemus, *On Vices*) in 2025, and, by the project's announcement in June 2026, the first scroll read from beginning to end.[^6] Several hundred scrolls remain; if the method scales, the recovery would be the largest addition to classical literature since the Renaissance.

## Machines that read

Two tools from the AI guide have reached the historian's desk. Neural networks trained on inscriptions can restore missing letters, date a text to within a few decades, and place it geographically: Ithaca (2022) did this for Greek and Aeneas (2025) for Latin, with the models most useful, their authors stress, as a way of finding parallels a scholar would otherwise miss rather than as oracles.[^7] Large language models can read, translate, and summarize documents at scale, which is transforming archival work and producing a new problem: the American Historical Association's 2025 guidelines note that such models routinely invent sources and quotations, and insist that historical thinking is a skill the tools cannot replace.[^8] The same decade has made the historian's future source base born-digital: the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine passed a trillion archived pages in 2025, and the record of the twenty-first century will be read from web archives that are vast, fragile, and, unlike clay, do not survive neglect.

## Big data and its discontents

The most ambitious methodological bet of the period has been **comparative history at scale**: coding hundreds of past societies into databases and testing theories statistically. The Seshat Global History Databank produced, in 2019, a *Nature* paper claiming that belief in moralizing gods followed rather than caused the rise of complex societies; a 2021 critique showed that missing data had been coded as absence, the paper was retracted, and the authors republished a version elsewhere maintaining the conclusion.[^9] The episode is the field's best test case for what quantitative history can and cannot do: it can test claims that narrative history only asserts, and it depends on coding decisions that can determine the result. Historians trained on documents and social scientists trained on datasets are still learning to check each other's work.

## What is coming

Three developments are visible. Ancient DNA is moving from the bones of elites to whole cemeteries and to pathogens, sediments, and the animals and plants people lived with, so that the next decade's picture will be of populations rather than individuals. The scroll-reading methods, if they generalize to the hundreds of Herculaneum rolls and to other carbonized or unopenable texts (Egyptian mummy cartonnage, medieval book bindings), could add more ancient writing than has been recovered in a century. And the debate about the period this guide's last chapters cover, the twenty-first century, will be conducted from a documentary base of a scale and fragility no historian has faced, with tools that can read it and also fabricate it. The discipline's method, ask how we know, will be more necessary, not less.

:::key
The new methods have not replaced the historian's craft; they have given it more evidence and new ways to misread it. Genomes tell you who moved, not why; tree rings tell you it was dry, not what people did about it; a LiDAR map tells you a city was there, not who ruled it; and a neural network restoring an inscription is guessing well, not knowing. Every finding in this chapter had to be interpreted by someone who knew the texts and the dirt, and several were interpreted wrongly before they were interpreted right. That is how the discipline has always worked. The difference is that the past now speaks in more registers than it did, and the historian who can read all of them is rare.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
Ancient DNA shows that migration occurred. Does that by itself tell you the migrants' language, political identity, or reason for moving?

:::answer One way to reason it through
No. Genetic relationships can constrain ancestry and movement, subject to sampling and dating limits. Language, identity, and motive require other evidence and may not map neatly onto genetic groups. The strongest account connects methods without asking one measurement to answer every historical question.
:::
:::

## Summary

- Ancient DNA has shown prehistory to be a history of migration and replacement, located the Indo-European homeland, reconstructed Neolithic kinship, and traced the plague; its models can also produce confident wrong results, as two recent disputes show.
- Recalibrated radiocarbon, tree rings, ice cores, and cave deposits have given climate an annual record and put drought and flood beside the texts in every major collapse.
- LiDAR, radar, multispectral imaging, and X-ray tomography have found cities under canopy, a lost Nile branch, erased manuscripts, and, for the first time, the text inside rolled Herculaneum scrolls.
- Neural networks restore and date inscriptions and read archives at scale; the profession has responded with guidelines warning that the tools fabricate and cannot replace historical judgment.
- Big-data comparative history can test what narrative only asserts and depends on coding decisions that can decide the result; the Seshat retraction is the cautionary case.
