## Recap

Chapter 22 ended on 4 August 1914. This chapter covers the two world wars and the years between as a single catastrophe, because that is how it looks from the end: the first war destroyed the old order and settled nothing, the peace created the conditions for the second, and the second finished what the first began. It is the most documented period in this guide and the one on which the most has been written; the chapter therefore gives the shape and the arguments, and the sources give the rest.

## The First World War

Everyone expected a short war. Germany's Schlieffen Plan aimed to take Paris in six weeks; it failed at the Marne in September 1914, and by Christmas the Western Front was a line of trenches from Switzerland to the sea that would move less than ten miles in either direction for three and a half years. The reason was technology: the machine gun, barbed wire, and quick-firing artillery made defense far stronger than attack, and the railway let defenders reinforce faster than attackers could advance on foot. The result was **attrition**: at Verdun (1916) France and Germany lost 700,000 men over ten months for no gain; on the first day of the Somme (1 July 1916) Britain lost 57,000, the worst day in its army's history, and the battle took 1.2 million casualties over four months to advance six miles.[^1] In the East the front moved, Russia lost millions, and in 1917 its army and state collapsed. Elsewhere the war was global: the Ottoman Empire joined Germany and was attacked at Gallipoli and in Mesopotamia and Palestine; Japan took Germany's Pacific islands; Africans fought Africans in the German colonies; Indians, Australians, Canadians, Senegalese, and Vietnamese fought in Europe.

It was the first **total war**: whole economies mobilized, women in factories, food rationed, the press controlled, and the enemy's civilians targeted by blockade (the British blockade of Germany contributed to perhaps 400,000 civilian deaths) and submarine (the German campaign brought in the United States in April 1917). In 1915 the Ottoman government deported and murdered its Armenian population, perhaps a million people, the century's first genocide on a continental scale.[^2] The war ended when Germany's last offensive failed in the summer of 1918, its allies collapsed, its navy mutinied, and its Kaiser fled; the armistice took effect at 11 a.m. on 11 November 1918. About 10 million soldiers and 7 million civilians were dead. Then influenza killed 50 million more worldwide.

## Revolution and the peace

Russia had two revolutions in 1917. In March the tsar abdicated after bread riots in Petrograd; in November Lenin's **Bolsheviks**, a small, disciplined Marxist party, seized power promising peace, land, and bread, and held it through a civil war (1918–1921) that killed perhaps ten million, by terror, the Red Army, and the incompetence of their enemies.[^3] The Soviet Union that emerged was the first state built on Marx's theory, and for seventy years every European politics defined itself for or against it.

The victors met at Paris in 1919. The **Treaty of Versailles** stripped Germany of its colonies, Alsace-Lorraine, and territory to Poland; limited its army to 100,000; forbade it to unite with Austria; assigned it "responsibility" for the war; and demanded reparations eventually set at 132 billion gold marks. Austria-Hungary was dissolved into Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, and parts of Poland, Romania, and Italy, on the principle of national self-determination that the multinational empires' peoples had demanded and that left every new state with minorities of its own. The Ottoman Empire was dismembered; Britain and France took its Arab provinces as "mandates," drew the borders of Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, and Jordan, and made promises to Arabs and Zionists that could not both be kept. A **League of Nations** was created to prevent war; the United States, whose president had proposed it, refused to join.[^4] Keynes, who had been at the conference, called the treaty a Carthaginian peace, one meant to ruin the loser as Rome ruined Carthage; German nationalists called it a *Diktat*, a dictated peace; historians now mostly think it was neither harsh enough to keep Germany down nor generous enough to reconcile it, and that its failure was less in its terms than in the victors' unwillingness to enforce it.

## The twenty years

The 1920s were a partial recovery and the 1930s a collapse. Europe's economies, distorted by war debts and reparations, depended on American loans; when the Wall Street crash of October 1929 stopped the loans, the **Great Depression** followed: world trade fell by two thirds in value, German industrial production by 40 percent, and unemployment reached 6 million in Germany and 3 million in Britain ([the business-cycles chapter of the economics guide](#/economics/cycles)).[^5] Democracies that had been new in 1919 fell one by one to authoritarian rule: Italy (1922), Poland (1926), Yugoslavia (1929), Germany (1933), Austria (1934), Spain, after a civil war in which Germany and Italy armed one side and the Soviet Union the other, in 1939. By 1939 only about ten European states were democracies.

**Fascism**, invented by Mussolini, was a politics of the nation as a single organism, led by one man, organized for struggle, contemptuous of liberalism and hostile to socialism, and it appealed to veterans, the frightened middle class, and everyone who had lost faith in parliaments. Its German form, **National Socialism**, added a racial theory in which history was a struggle between races, Germans were the master race, Jews were the enemy of all humanity, and Germany's destiny was conquest of living space in the East.[^6] Adolf Hitler, an Austrian ex-corporal and failed painter, built the Nazi party on it, won 37 percent of the vote in 1932 in the depths of the Depression, was appointed chancellor by President Hindenburg in January 1933 as the head of a coalition the conservatives thought they could control, and within eighteen months had abolished every other party, the trade unions, the federal states, and the rule of law, and put his opponents in concentration camps. He then rearmed, took back the Rhineland (1936), annexed Austria (March 1938), took the Sudetenland with British and French consent at Munich (September 1938) and the rest of Czechoslovakia without it (March 1939), signed a pact with Stalin to divide Poland (August 1939), and invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France, having promised, declared war two days later.

Meanwhile Stalin, who had won the succession to Lenin by 1928, collectivized Soviet agriculture by force, which caused a famine that killed some 5 to 7 million people in 1932–1933, most of them in Ukraine and Kazakhstan; industrialized the country in a decade; and in the Great Terror of 1937–1938 had some 700,000 people shot and millions sent to the Gulag.[^7] The two totalitarian states that would fight the largest war in history were, in 1939, allies.

## The Second World War

Germany conquered Poland in five weeks, Denmark and Norway in April 1940 and the Netherlands, Belgium, and France in six weeks from 10 May (the French army, thought the best in Europe, collapsed; Britain was left alone and, under Churchill, refused terms), and by mid-1941 controlled Europe from the Pyrenees to the Soviet border. On 22 June 1941 it invaded the Soviet Union with 3 million men, the largest military operation in history, and nearly won: by December the Germans were at the gates of Moscow and had taken 3 million prisoners, most of whom they let starve. Then the winter, the Red Army's reserves, and the sheer size of the country stopped them; at Stalingrad (August 1942–February 1943) a German army was destroyed, and from Kursk (July 1943) the Red Army advanced for two years to Berlin.[^8] The **Eastern Front** was where the war in Europe was decided: about 80 percent of German military deaths occurred there, and the Soviet Union lost some 27 million people, soldiers and civilians, a seventh of its population.

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941) and Hitler's declaration of war on the United States four days later made it a world war in the full sense (chapter 34 for the Asian war). The Western Allies fought in North Africa, invaded Italy in 1943, bombed German cities into rubble (some 400,000 German civilians died; Hamburg, Dresden), and on 6 June 1944 landed in Normandy. Germany fought to the end; Hitler killed himself on 30 April 1945 as the Red Army took Berlin, and Germany surrendered unconditionally on 8 May. The war had killed perhaps 40 million people in Europe, more than half of them civilians, and left the continent in ruins, with tens of millions displaced.[^9]

## The Holocaust

The Nazi regime murdered about six million Jews, two thirds of Europe's Jewish population, between 1941 and 1945: by shooting, in the occupied Soviet Union, where mobile killing units and their local helpers murdered between half a million and a million in the six months after the invasion and about 1.5 million in all; and by gas, in six extermination camps built in occupied Poland, of which Auschwitz-Birkenau alone killed about a million. It also murdered some 200,000 to 500,000 Roma, 250,000 disabled people, 3 million Soviet prisoners of war, and millions of Polish and Soviet civilians, in a program of racial reordering of which the Jews were the central target.[^10] The **Holocaust** (in Hebrew, the *Shoah*) was planned at the top, carried out by hundreds of thousands of ordinary Germans and their collaborators, made possible by the modern state's capacity to identify, register, transport, and process, and known, in outline, to the Allied governments by 1942. It was unlike anything before it in its aim, which was not to conquer, exploit, or expel a people but to kill every one of them, everywhere, as an end in itself.

:::history How we got here
How the Holocaust happened has been the central question of twentieth-century historiography. The "intentionalist" view, dominant in the 1960s, saw a straight line from Hitler's early writings to Auschwitz: he intended it, and did it. The "functionalist" view, from the 1970s, stressed the chaotic, competitive Nazi state in which officials radicalized policy to please the Führer, and saw the decision for total murder emerging in 1941 from the failure of expulsion plans and the war in the East rather than from a long-held plan. The synthesis since about 1990 (Browning, Kershaw, Longerich, Friedländer) holds that the ideological goal of a Europe without Jews was constant, that the method escalated step by step in response to circumstances, that the decision for systematic murder was taken in the second half of 1941, and that Hitler's will was necessary but the participation of the bureaucracy, the army, and the population was what made it possible. Christopher Browning's study of one police battalion showed that ordinary middle-aged men from Hamburg shot Jews all day because they were asked to and their comrades were doing it. Raul Hilberg showed the machinery; Saul Friedländer restored the victims' voices. The debate about German uniqueness, comparative genocide, and the role of the "bystander" continues, and the archives, above all the Arolsen records with their 30 million documents on some 17.5 million people, are still being read.[^11]
:::

:::key
The two wars are one because the second could not have happened without the first: without Versailles, no revanchism; without 1917, no Soviet Union and no anti-Bolshevism; without the trenches, no brutalized veterans; without the Depression's collapse of the postwar order, no Hitler. Between 1914 and 1945 Europe destroyed the world it had ruled in 1914: its empires would be gone within twenty years, its economies were wrecked, its moral claim to lead was buried at Auschwitz, and its future was decided by the two powers, American and Soviet, that occupied it in 1945. The rest of the section is about how it recovered, and it recovered better than anyone in 1945 expected.
:::

## Summary

- The First World War became a four-year stalemate of trenches and attrition because technology favored defense; it was the first total war and killed 17 million, followed by influenza and the collapse of four empires.
- Russia's Bolshevik revolution created the Soviet Union; Versailles left Germany resentful and the new states of Eastern Europe unstable; the League failed.
- The Depression destroyed the postwar order and most of Europe's new democracies; fascism and Nazism rose on nationalism, fear, and, in Germany, a racial theory of history; Stalin's Soviet Union killed millions in famine and terror.
- The Second World War was decided on the Eastern Front, where the Soviet Union lost 27 million people; Germany's unconditional surrender in May 1945 left Europe in ruins.
- The Nazis murdered six million Jews and millions of others in a state-organized genocide; how it happened remains historiography's central question.
