## Why did a connected Bronze Age world become vulnerable?

Mesopotamia and Egypt were not alone. By 2500 BCE the Old World had several literate civilizations trading with each other across thousands of miles, in a system that historians call the **Bronze Age** after the metal, an alloy of copper and tin, that its elites fought and displayed with. This chapter surveys that world, its interconnection, and its sudden end, which is the first documented collapse of a civilization-wide system and still one of the most argued-about.

## The Indus

In the 1850s, British engineers building the Lahore–Multan railway used ancient fired bricks as ballast; the bricks came from Harappa, and when excavation began there seventy years later, in 1921, the source turned out to be a civilization nobody had known existed.[^1] The **Indus Valley civilization** (c. 2600–1900 BCE) covered an area larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia combined, with at least five large cities (Harappa and Mohenjo-daro the best known), a hundred towns, and a thousand villages, all sharing a script, standardized weights and measures, and brick sizes.

What is striking is what it lacks. No palaces, no royal tombs, no monumental statues of rulers, no clear temples, no evidence of large-scale warfare. Its cities had the best drainage of any until Rome: nearly every house at Mohenjo-daro had a bathroom connected to covered street drains. Its one monumental building is a great bath. Its script, some 400 signs on seals and pottery, has never been deciphered and may not encode a full language.[^2] Whether the Indus was ruled by kings who left no trace, by councils, or by something we have no name for is unknown, and it is the standard reminder that the Mesopotamian and Egyptian model of the divine king was not the only way to run a civilization. The cities declined around 1900 BCE, in a period when the monsoon weakened and the rivers shifted, and their people dispersed into villages; there was no conquest, and the later history of India (chapter 10) begins from that dispersal.

## Crete and Mycenae

On Crete, from about 2000 BCE, the **Minoans** built sprawling palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos, and elsewhere: courtyards, storerooms holding thousands of gallons of oil and grain in great jars, workshops, and walls painted with dolphins, bull-leapers, and processions of women in flounced skirts.[^3] The palaces had no fortifications. Minoan writing, Linear A, is undeciphered; their society seems to have been organized around the palaces as redistributive centers, and the prominence of women in their art has been read, without much evidence either way, as everything from matriarchy to fashion. Around 1450 BCE the palaces burned and Greek-speakers from the mainland took over Crete.

Those Greek-speakers were the **Mycenaeans**, named for their citadel at Mycenae, whose shaft graves, excavated by Heinrich Schliemann in 1876, held gold death-masks and enough weaponry to confirm that Homer's "Mycenae rich in gold" was not entirely invented.[^4] Mycenaean palaces were fortified with walls of stones so large that later Greeks assumed giants had built them. Their script, Linear B, was deciphered in 1952 by Michael Ventris, an architect, and turned out to be Greek: the earliest Greek, five hundred years before Homer, and every tablet is an inventory. Sheep, wheels, bronze, slaves, offerings to gods whose names (Zeus, Poseidon, Athena) are already familiar.[^5] The Trojan War, if there was one, would fall at the end of this period; Troy was a real city, excavated, and its sixth and seventh layers were destroyed around 1300–1180 BCE, but no text of the period mentions a war with Greeks, and Homer wrote four centuries later.

## The Hittites, and the first international system

In Anatolia, the **Hittites** built an empire from about 1650 BCE, centered on Hattusa, that by 1350 rivaled Egypt. Their archive of some 30,000 clay tablets, found from 1906, includes the first known peace treaty between great powers: the agreement with Egypt after Kadesh, sealed around 1259 BCE, a replica of whose cuneiform version hangs at the United Nations.[^6] The Hittites spoke an Indo-European language, the oldest attested, and their laws, unlike Hammurabi's, mostly replaced death and mutilation with fines.

The Late Bronze Age (c. 1550–1200 BCE) was an international system. Its great powers, Egypt, the Hittites, Babylon, Assyria, and Mitanni in northern Syria, exchanged ambassadors, royal brides, and gifts; addressed each other as "brother"; and wrote in Akkadian, the diplomatic language, whatever they spoke at home. The **Amarna letters**, some 380 tablets from the Egyptian court of Akhenaten found in 1887, show the system working: kings of Babylon complaining that Egyptian gold arrived short weight, Canaanite vassals begging for troops, a Mitanni princess sent to marry the pharaoh with a dowry list running to hundreds of lines.[^7] Below the great powers were vassal city-states, and across the whole system moved copper from Cyprus, tin from Afghanistan and perhaps Cornwall, Baltic amber, Egyptian gold, Lebanese timber, and textiles from everywhere. A shipwreck off Uluburun in Turkey, sunk around 1320 BCE, carried ten tons of copper ingots, a ton of tin, glass, ebony, ivory, and goods from at least seven cultures.[^8] It was a globalized age, at the pace of a sailing ship.

## The collapse

Between about 1200 and 1150 BCE, nearly all of it fell. The Hittite empire vanished so completely that its existence was forgotten until the tablets were dug up. Every Mycenaean palace was destroyed or abandoned, writing disappeared from Greece for four hundred years, and the population fell by perhaps three quarters. The great cities of the Levant, Ugarit and Hazor and many others, were burned and never reoccupied. Egypt survived, barely, after repelling two invasions by the **Sea Peoples**, a coalition of migrants and raiders whose origins are still argued, and then declined into a long weakness. Babylon and Assyria shrank. Trade routes closed. Of the Late Bronze Age great powers, only Egypt and Assyria emerged recognizable.[^9]

:::frontier
Why? The old answer was the Sea Peoples, on the strength of Egyptian inscriptions that name them. The current view is that they were a symptom as much as a cause. Three lines of evidence have accumulated since about 2000. Tree rings and pollen show a severe, prolonged drought across the eastern Mediterranean beginning around 1200; a 2023 study of juniper timbers preserved at Gordion in central Anatolia found three consecutive years of extreme drought at exactly the moment the empire fell.[^10] Earthquakes struck several sites. And the system's very interconnection made it fragile: palaces that depended on imported copper and tin, and on grain shipped from abroad, could not survive the disruption of trade, and each collapse deepened the others. The historian Eric Cline's phrase is a "perfect storm"; the model of a networked system failing through cascading dependencies is the same one applied to 2008. It is the first collapse we can study in detail, and the one thing it does not support is a single cause.
:::

## What survived, and what came next

Collapse is not the end of history, and the centuries after 1150 were not empty. Iron replaced bronze, not because it was better (early iron was worse) but because it was everywhere and tin was not; the change democratized weapons and tools. The alphabet, invented by Semitic speakers in Egypt or the Levant around 1800 BCE as a simplified script for their languages (a contested 2024 claim would push it back to 2400 BCE in Syria), spread with Phoenician traders after the collapse and became, in Greek and then Latin hands, the writing system of the West (chapter 5). Small kingdoms grew in the space the empires had left: Israel and Judah in the hills of Canaan, the Phoenician cities on the coast, the Aramaean states of Syria, the Greek communities that would become the *poleis*. The period Greeks later called their Dark Age was the one in which the *Iliad* was composed and carried in memory. When the lights come on again, around 800 BCE, the world is different: more literate, more iron, less palace, and about to produce, in three separate places, the ideas of the next chapter.

:::key
The Bronze Age shows two things that recur throughout this guide. First, that interconnection is a source of both wealth and fragility: the trading system that made the palaces rich was what made them fall together. Second, that collapse is real but rarely total. The Hittites vanished; the Greeks lost writing for four centuries and kept their language, their gods, and their stories. Which things survive a collapse, and why, is one of history's most useful questions.
:::

:::know Dates to hold onto
- c. 2600–1900 BCE: Indus Valley civilization.
- c. 2000–1450 BCE: Minoan palaces on Crete.
- c. 1650 BCE: Hittite kingdom founded.
- c. 1450–1200 BCE: Mycenaean Greece at its height; Linear B.
- c. 1350 BCE: Amarna letters.
- 1274 BCE: Kadesh; c. 1259 the treaty.
- c. 1200–1150 BCE: the Late Bronze Age collapse.
- c. 1180 BCE: Hittite empire ends; Troy VIIa destroyed.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
Several palaces are destroyed within a broad period. Does that establish one invading people as the cause of every collapse?

:::answer One way to reason it through
No. Destruction layers need careful dating, and similar outcomes can have different causes. Inscriptions, climate evidence, earthquakes, trade disruption, and local politics may each contribute. A connected system can transmit shocks without every place falling in the same way or at the same moment.
:::
:::

## Summary

- The Indus civilization was as large as Egypt and Mesopotamia together and shows no kings, temples, or wars; its script is undeciphered and it declined with the monsoon around 1900 BCE.
- Minoan Crete and Mycenaean Greece were palace societies; Linear B, deciphered in 1952, is the earliest Greek and consists of inventories.
- The Late Bronze Age was an international system of great powers exchanging treaties, brides, and goods across the eastern Mediterranean, documented in the Amarna letters and the Uluburun wreck.
- Between 1200 and 1150 BCE the system collapsed: drought, earthquakes, migration, and cascading trade failure; the Hittites vanished and Greece lost writing for four centuries.
- Iron, the alphabet, and small kingdoms emerged from the wreckage and set up the next age.
