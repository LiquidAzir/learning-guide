## How did Korea and Japan adapt ideas from China?

The Han fell in 220 and China divided. This chapter covers the long division, the arrival and triumph of Buddhism, the reunification under the Sui and Tang, and the entry of Korea and Japan into the record, which happens in these centuries because both adopted Chinese writing to keep it. By 907 East Asia is a cultural world with China at its center and Korea, Japan, and Vietnam as distinct civilizations sharing its script, its Buddhism, its Confucian statecraft, and its law.

## The age of division

For 370 years after 220, with a brief reunion (280–316), China was several states. The **Three Kingdoms** (220–280), Wei, Shu, and Wu, gave Chinese culture its favorite heroes and its great historical novel, but were a period of war and depopulation; the census of 280 counted 16 million, a quarter of the Han's 58, partly through collapse of registration and partly through death. In 311 the northern capital Luoyang fell to Xiongnu-descended invaders, and for the next 270 years the north was ruled by dynasties founded by steppe peoples, Xiongnu, Xianbei, and others, who governed a Chinese population, intermarried with Chinese elites, and Sinicized in stages, while the Chinese court fled south to Nanjing and ruled the Yangzi valley through a succession of short-lived dynasties.[^1] The **Northern and Southern Dynasties** (420–589) were the pattern: barbarian north, Chinese south, each claiming to be the legitimate empire. The parallel with Rome is again close: Germanic kings in the West, Roman emperors in the East. The difference is that in China the north eventually reconquered the south and reunified everything.

The division had consequences. The great families of the south developed an aristocratic culture of calligraphy, poetry, landscape painting, and Daoist mysticism that set the standard for Chinese high culture; the Yangzi valley, hitherto a frontier, was settled and became China's rice bowl; and the northern dynasties, above all the Northern Wei (386–534), built the institutions, the equal-field land system, the militia, the fusion of steppe cavalry with Chinese infantry, that the Sui and Tang would use to reunify.

## Buddhism comes to China

Buddhism had reached China along the Silk Road by the first century CE (chapter 10), as a foreign cult among merchants. In the age of division it conquered. Its appeal to a Chinese population living through collapse was what Christianity's had been to Romans: personal salvation, a moral universe of karma in which suffering made sense, compassion, and a community that cared for the poor and the dead. Its appeal to the northern rulers was that it was not Confucian, and therefore not the property of the Chinese scholar-elite they distrusted; the Northern Wei made it a state religion and carved the colossal Buddhas of Yungang and Longmen. By 500 there were perhaps 30,000 monasteries in the north alone.[^2]

Buddhism had to be translated, in both senses. Indian texts came in Sanskrit; the great translators, above all the Central Asian monk Kumarajiva (344–413) at Chang'an, created a Chinese Buddhist vocabulary, borrowing Daoist terms where they fit. Chinese pilgrims went to India for the originals: Faxian (399–412) and, most famously, Xuanzang (629–645), who spent sixteen years in India, brought back 657 texts, and became the hero of the novel *Journey to the West*.[^3] Chinese Buddhism developed schools of its own: Pure Land, in which faith in the Buddha Amitabha brought rebirth in his paradise, the religion of the masses; Tiantai, a scholastic synthesis; and **Chan**, which taught that enlightenment came suddenly, through meditation and the master's shock, not through texts, and which Japan would receive as Zen.[^4] Confucians attacked Buddhism as foreign, celibate (and so unfilial), and parasitic (monks paid no tax); Daoists borrowed its organization and competed. In 845 the Tang state, needing the monasteries' bronze and land, closed 4,600 of them and returned 260,000 monks and nuns to lay life; Buddhism survived but never again dominated the Chinese state. It had, by then, changed China permanently: its temples, its festivals, its art, its vocabulary of mercy and rebirth, its printing (the oldest dated printed book in the world is a Chinese *Diamond Sutra* of 868), and its transmission of the whole package to Korea and Japan.

## Korea and Japan enter

**Korea** had Bronze Age cultures by 1000 BCE and was partly conquered by Han China in 108 BCE, which planted Chinese writing, administration, and settlers in the northwest for four centuries. As the Han fell, three Korean kingdoms took shape: **Goguryeo** in the north, stretching into Manchuria, a formidable military state; **Baekje** in the southwest, the most refined and the closest to Japan; and **Silla** in the southeast, the latest developer.[^5] All three adopted Buddhism in the fourth to sixth centuries and Chinese script, law, and court ritual with it; Baekje transmitted them onward to Japan. In 668 Silla, allied with Tang China, destroyed Goguryeo and Baekje and then, in 676, expelled the Tang and unified the peninsula south of the Daedong River for the first time: **Unified Silla** (668–935), with its capital at Gyeongju, its royal tombs full of gold crowns, its Buddhist temples (Bulguksa), its aristocratic "bone-rank" system, and its diplomatic and cultural dependence on the Tang. Ancient DNA from Gaya-period Gimhae (fourth to fifth century) shows a population close to modern Koreans but with a Jomon-related component shared with prehistoric Japan, evidence that the straits were crossed in both directions.[^6]

**Japan** was settled by the Jomon hunter-gatherers, makers of the world's oldest pottery (c. 14,000 BCE), and transformed from about 1000 BCE by the Yayoi migrants from the Korean peninsula who brought rice, bronze, iron, and a large share of the ancestry of modern Japanese; the genetic picture, confirmed by genomes from Yayoi and Kofun sites, is a three-way mixture of Jomon, East Asian farmer, and Northeast Asian ancestry, with the immigrants mostly arriving via Korea.[^7] Chinese chronicles of the third century describe a land of "Wa" with a hundred chiefdoms and a shaman-queen named Himiko. From about 250 CE the **Kofun** period is named for the keyhole-shaped burial mounds of its chiefs, the largest, near Osaka, longer than the Great Pyramid's base, and by the sixth century a paramount clan, the **Yamato**, who claimed descent from the sun goddess and whose descendants are Japan's imperial house today, ruled from the Nara basin.

In 552 (or 538) Baekje sent the Yamato court a Buddha image and scriptures, and Japan's transformation began. Under Prince Shotoku (regent c. 593–622) and then the Taika reforms of 645, the Yamato state borrowed the Sui and then the Tang model wholesale: a written law code, a centralized bureaucracy with ranks, a census and land allotment, a capital laid out on a Chinese grid (Nara, 710; then Heian, modern Kyoto, 794), Chinese characters to write Japanese, Buddhism as a state religion with great temples (Todaiji's Buddha, cast in 752, is fifteen meters high), Confucian ethics, and the title *tenno*, "heavenly sovereign," for the ruler.[^8] Japan took what it wanted and left what it did not: it never adopted the examination system that would define Chinese government, keeping office hereditary, and it kept its native gods (later called **Shinto**) alongside the Buddhas. The Japanese written language, using Chinese characters for meaning and, from the ninth century, two syllabaries for sound, is the visible record of the borrowing and its limits.

## The Tang

The **Sui** (581–618) reunified China from the north, built the Grand Canal linking the Yangzi to the Yellow River (which fed the northern capitals with southern rice for the next thirteen centuries), and exhausted themselves in wars against Goguryeo. The **Tang** (618–907) inherited the reunification and built on it the empire that Chinese memory regards as its golden age. Its founders were a part-Turkic aristocratic family from the northwest, and its second emperor, Taizong (626–649), who had killed his brothers to take the throne, was the model of the Confucian ruler in Chinese historiography and the "Heavenly Khan" of the Turks he defeated.

Tang China at its height under Xuanzong (712–756) had about 50 to 60 million people; its capital **Chang'an**, on a grid nine kilometers by eight, held a million, the largest city in the world, with Persian, Sogdian, Turkic, Indian, Korean, and Japanese residents, Zoroastrian and Nestorian Christian churches, Muslim traders, and a Western Market where foreign goods were sold.[^9] Its writ ran to the Tarim basin and Central Asia, where in 751 a Tang army lost to the Arabs at the Talas River, the one battle between the Islamic caliphate and China. Its government perfected the **examination system**: candidates from across the empire sat papers on the Confucian classics and literary composition, and the successful entered a civil service that was, in principle, open to talent; in the Tang the aristocracy still dominated, and it would take the Song to make the system decisive. Its law code (653) became the model for Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Its poets, Li Bai, Du Fu, Wang Wei, Bai Juyi, are the summit of Chinese poetry; some 50,000 Tang poems survive. Its only woman emperor, **Wu Zetian**, rose from concubine to empress to ruler in her own name (690–705), founded her own dynasty, promoted the examinations and Buddhism against the aristocracy, and was reviled by Confucian historians for a millennium.[^10]

The Tang broke in 755. An Lushan, a general of Sogdian and Turkic descent commanding the northeastern armies, rebelled, took both capitals, and drove Xuanzong into flight; the war lasted eight years, killed millions, and ended only with the help of Uyghur cavalry. The dynasty survived for 150 years as a shadow: the frontier armies became warlords, the salt monopoly replaced the land tax as the state's income, the Tarim basin was lost to Tibet, the 845 suppression of Buddhism was a symptom of fiscal desperation, and in 907 a warlord deposed the last emperor. The **Five Dynasties** that followed lasted fifty years, and then the Song (chapter 29) reunified China again.

:::key
The four centuries of division gave China Buddhism, the Yangzi rice economy, and the northern institutions of reunification; the Tang gave it the cosmopolitan empire, the examination system, the law code, and the poetry that East Asia would regard as the standard. And in these same centuries Korea and Japan became literate civilizations by adopting the Chinese package, script, Buddhism, law, bureaucracy, and city plan, and became distinct ones by what each declined to adopt. The East Asian world, one script and three (soon four, with Vietnam) states, existed by 900 and has never stopped existing.
:::

## Summary

- After 220 China divided for 370 years: steppe-founded dynasties in the north, Chinese courts in the south; the Yangzi was settled and the north built the institutions of reunification.
- Buddhism conquered divided China as Christianity conquered Rome, was translated and Sinicized, produced Pure Land and Chan, and was cut back by the state in 845 after changing China permanently.
- Korea's three kingdoms and Yamato Japan entered history by adopting Chinese script, Buddhism, law, and administration; Silla unified Korea in 668 and Japan built a Tang-model state at Nara and Heian while keeping hereditary office and native gods.
- The Tang built the age's most cosmopolitan empire, with a capital of a million, the examination system, the law code that East Asia copied, its greatest poetry, and a woman emperor.
- The An Lushan rebellion of 755 broke the Tang; it lingered until 907, and the East Asian world of one script and several states was in place.
