## Recap

Chapter 34 ended in August 1945 with Japan's surrender, China exhausted, and Korea liberated. This chapter covers the three decades in which the region took its Cold War shape: the Chinese Communist victory and the Mao era, the American occupation and reconstruction of Japan, the division of Korea and the war that fixed it. It ends with Mao's death in 1976, when China was poor, isolated, and about to change direction, Japan was rich, and the two Koreas were a dictatorship and a dictatorship.

## China: civil war and victory

The Nationalists and Communists had fought Japan side by side in theory and against each other in practice. When Japan surrendered, Chiang's government held the cities and had American backing, three million troops, and the recognition of the world; Mao's party held rural bases in the north with a million troops and the peasants' support, won by land reform and by having fought the Japanese where the peasants lived. The **Chinese Civil War** (1946–1949) was decided in Manchuria, where the Communists, armed with captured Japanese and surrendered Nationalist weapons, destroyed Chiang's best armies in 1948, and then in three great battles in the north that winter; the Nationalist regime, corrupt, inflationary (prices rose a thousandfold in 1948), and hated in the cities it had governed, dissolved. On 1 October 1949 Mao proclaimed the **People's Republic of China** in Beijing. Chiang fled to Taiwan with two million followers and the imperial art collection, and the Republic of China has governed the island since.[^1]

## The Mao era

The new state's first years were its most successful. Land reform, carried out village by village with mass meetings at which landlords were denounced and perhaps one to two million killed, gave the land to the peasants; inflation was stopped; opium, prostitution, and foot-binding were eliminated; a marriage law gave women divorce and property rights; literacy and health campaigns began; and the Korean War (below), which China entered in October 1950, was fought to a draw against the United States, which no Chinese government had done to a Western power in a century.[^2] The party, with Soviet advisers and a Soviet-model five-year plan, collectivized agriculture and nationalized industry by 1956.

Then Mao broke with the model and with reality. The **Great Leap Forward** (1958–1962) merged the collectives into communes of thousands of households, set impossible steel and grain targets, sent peasants to build backyard furnaces and dams while the harvest rotted, and, when local officials reported fictitious record harvests to avoid punishment, requisitioned grain on the basis of the fictions and exported it while the villages starved. The **Great Famine** of 1959–1961 killed, by the estimates of demographers working from the 1982 census and party archives, between 30 and 45 million people, the largest famine in recorded history, in peacetime, caused by policy.[^3] Mao, criticized within the party, retreated for a few years, and then, in 1966, launched the **Cultural Revolution**: a call to the young to attack the party apparatus, "bourgeois" culture, and everything old, in his name. Red Guards beat teachers to death, destroyed temples and libraries, and denounced their parents; the party's leaders, including the head of state, were purged, humiliated, and in several cases killed; the universities closed for a decade; and the country was ruled by the army and by Mao's court. Perhaps one to two million died and a generation lost its education.[^4] The chaos ebbed after 1969; in 1972 Mao received Richard Nixon in Beijing, ending twenty-three years of hostility with America to balance the Soviet Union; in September 1976 he died, and the radicals around him, the Gang of Four, were arrested within a month.

Mao's China had, by 1976, doubled life expectancy (from about 35 to about 65), made most of the population literate, built a heavy-industrial base, and tested nuclear weapons; it had also killed tens of millions of its own people, kept the peasantry poorer than under the Qing, and cut itself off from the world. The party's own verdict, in 1981, was that Mao's achievements were primary and his errors, "especially the Cultural Revolution," grave; it has not revised that since, because the party's legitimacy rests on him.[^5]

## Japan: occupation and miracle

The United States occupied Japan from 1945 to 1952 under General Douglas MacArthur, who governed through the Japanese bureaucracy and the emperor, whom he kept, stripped of divinity, as a symbol of continuity. The occupation demilitarized Japan, tried its war leaders, purged 200,000 officials, broke up the great landholdings and gave the land to the tenants who farmed it, dissolved the *zaibatsu* conglomerates (partially), legalized unions, gave women the vote, and wrote Japan a constitution (1947), in English, in a week, that renounced war forever in its Article 9, made the emperor a symbol without power, and guaranteed rights more extensive than the American constitution's.[^6] Then the Cold War reversed the emphasis: from 1948 the occupation wanted Japan as an ally and an industrial base, stopped the purges, suppressed the left, and in 1951 signed a peace treaty and a security treaty that kept American bases in Japan and put Japan under the American nuclear umbrella. Japan's foreign policy for the rest of the century was set: alliance with America, minimal rearmament, and everything into the economy.

The economy delivered. The Korean War's procurement orders restarted Japanese industry in 1950; from 1955 to 1973 the economy grew at about 10 percent a year, the fastest sustained growth any major country had achieved. The instruments were high savings, a bureaucracy (the Ministry of International Trade and Industry) that steered credit and protected infant industries, lifetime employment in large firms, quality control learned from American statisticians and improved on, and export of ever more sophisticated goods: textiles, then steel and ships, then cars and electronics. By 1968 Japan had the world's second-largest economy after the United States, a status it kept until 2010; the Tokyo Olympics of 1964 and the first bullet train the same year announced it.[^7] Politics was stable to the point of stasis: the conservative Liberal Democratic Party governed continuously from 1955 to 1993.

## Korea: division and war

Korea was liberated in August 1945 and divided the same month, by two American colonels who drew a line at the 38th parallel on a National Geographic map so that Soviet troops would accept Japanese surrender in the north and Americans in the south; it was meant to be temporary. Each occupier installed its people: in the north the Soviets built a state around **Kim Il Sung**, a guerrilla who had fought the Japanese in Manchuria and spent the war in the Soviet army; in the south the Americans backed **Syngman Rhee**, an elderly Christian nationalist who had spent forty years in exile. Two republics were proclaimed in 1948, each claiming the whole peninsula, and the south spent 1948–1950 suppressing leftist insurgencies with tens of thousands killed.[^8]

On 25 June 1950 Kim's army, with Stalin's reluctant approval and Soviet tanks, invaded the south and nearly won in two months. The United States intervened under a United Nations mandate; MacArthur's landing at Inchon in September cut the northern army off and destroyed it; American and South Korean troops drove to the Chinese border by November; and China, which had warned it would not tolerate American troops on the Yalu, sent 300,000 "volunteers" across and drove them back below Seoul by January 1951. The front stabilized near the 38th parallel and the war became two years of trench fighting and negotiation while American bombing destroyed every city and most of the villages in the north. The **Korean War** ended in an armistice on 27 July 1953, never followed by a peace treaty; perhaps 2 to 3 million Koreans, most of them civilians, some 400,000 Chinese by Western estimates (180,000 by China's count), and 36,000 Americans were dead, the peninsula was a wasteland, and the line was almost where it had started.[^9]

The two Koreas then diverged. The north, under Kim, built the most closed and total state in the world: a personality cult exceeding Stalin's, a hereditary dynasty (Kim's son and grandson have succeeded him), a command economy that outgrew the south's until the 1970s and then stagnated, and a foreign policy of hostility and provocation. The south, under Rhee and then, after a coup in 1961, General **Park Chung-hee**, was a military dictatorship that jailed and tortured its opponents and, from the mid-1960s, launched an export-led industrialization on the Japanese model, with state-directed credit to family conglomerates (the *chaebol*), that took South Korea from one of the poorest countries in the world in 1960 to an industrial economy by 1979, when Park was assassinated by his own intelligence chief.[^10] Chapter 36 takes the story on.

:::history How we got here
The Mao era is the most contested period in Chinese historiography because it is the most politically constrained. Inside China, the party's 1981 resolution set the limits: the Great Leap was a "serious mistake" caused partly by natural disasters, the Cultural Revolution a catastrophe caused by Mao's errors and the Gang of Four, and the death tolls are not discussed. Outside China, the archival opening of the 1990s and 2000s let historians (Yang Jisheng, a Chinese journalist whose *Tombstone* was published in Hong Kong and banned on the mainland; Frank Dikötter; Jasper Becker) document the famine from provincial party records, and the estimates rose from the 15 to 30 million of the 1980s to the 30 to 45 million now standard. On Korea, the question of who started the war was argued for forty years, with revisionists (Cumings) stressing the south's provocations and the civil-war character of the conflict; Soviet archives opened after 1991 showed Kim asking Stalin for permission to invade and getting it, which settled the question of the first shot without settling the question of the war's nature. On Japan, the debate is whether the occupation's reforms took root because they were imposed or because Japanese liberals had wanted them since the 1920s; John Dower's *Embracing Defeat* made the case for a genuinely Japanese appropriation of defeat.[^11]
:::

:::key
Between 1945 and 1976 East Asia became three experiments in how to build a modern state from ruin. China chose revolution, achieved unity, literacy, and longer lives, and paid for the choice with the largest famine in history and a decade of self-inflicted chaos. Japan had a democracy and a pacifist constitution written for it, kept both, and built the world's second economy in twenty years. Korea was divided by an accident of occupation and fixed in division by a war that killed three million and settled nothing, and its two halves became the twentieth century's clearest controlled experiment: one people, one culture, two systems, and, by 1976, two very different countries.
:::

## Summary

- The Communists won China's civil war in 1949 on peasant support, land reform, and Nationalist collapse; Chiang fled to Taiwan.
- Mao's first years brought land reform, unity, and a draw with America in Korea; the Great Leap Forward caused a famine that killed 30 to 45 million, and the Cultural Revolution destroyed a decade of education and killed a million more.
- The American occupation gave Japan a pacifist democratic constitution, land reform, and an alliance; Japan grew at 10 percent a year and became the world's second economy by 1968.
- Korea was divided at the 38th parallel by accident in 1945; the war of 1950–1953 killed three million and ended in an armistice that still stands.
- North Korea became the world's most closed hereditary dictatorship; South Korea's military dictatorship launched the export industrialization that made it rich.
