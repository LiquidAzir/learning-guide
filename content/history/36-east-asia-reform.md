## Recap

Chapter 35 ended in 1976 with Mao dead, Japan rich, and the Koreas divided between a hereditary dictatorship and a military one. This chapter covers the quarter century in which China changed course and grew faster for longer than any large country ever had, Japan reached the top and stalled, and South Korea and Taiwan became the first non-Western societies to move from developmental dictatorship to democracy. It is the making of the East Asia the reader knows.

## Deng's China

**Deng Xiaoping**, a veteran of the Long March who had been purged by Mao in 1966 and again in 1976, took control of the party by December 1978 and never held its top office. His program was pragmatic to the point of anti-ideology: "it doesn't matter if a cat is black or white so long as it catches mice," and "seek truth from facts." The communes were dismantled from 1979 as peasants, first illegally and then with permission, returned to family farming under the **household responsibility system**, and grain output rose by a third in five years while a hundred million surplus farm workers became available for other work. **Special Economic Zones** at Shenzhen, a fishing village across the border from Hong Kong, and three other coastal sites let foreign capital in under market rules; township and village enterprises, collectively owned and market-driven, absorbed the labor; state firms were given autonomy and then, in the 1990s, restructured or privatized; prices were freed in stages; and foreign trade, negligible in 1978, grew at 15 percent a year.[^1] The economy grew at about 10 percent a year for three decades. The number of Chinese living in extreme poverty fell from around 800 million in 1981 to under 10 million by 2015, the largest reduction in poverty in human history, and the party's legitimacy shifted from revolution to growth.[^2]

The reforms had limits that Deng set. He rehabilitated the Cultural Revolution's victims, ended the cult of Mao, allowed a decade of intellectual opening, and permitted no challenge to the party's monopoly. In April 1989, after the death of a reformist leader, students occupied **Tiananmen Square** in Beijing demanding press freedom, an end to corruption, and dialogue; the movement spread to hundreds of cities and drew workers and officials; the party split, the reformist general secretary, Zhao Ziyang, was ousted, and on the night of 3–4 June the army cleared the square and the streets around it, killing several hundred to over a thousand people, the number still unknown because the state has never allowed it to be counted.[^3] The lesson the party drew, and has applied since, was that economic reform and political control were separable, and that the Soviet Union, which fell two years later, had proved what happened when a communist party tried the other way. Growth resumed after 1992, when Deng, aged eighty-seven, toured the south to demand it; Hong Kong returned from Britain in 1997 under a promise of fifty years of autonomy; China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001; and by 2000 it was the workshop of the world and had not held a free election.

## Japan: the peak and the bubble

Japan in the 1980s was the future. Its cars had taken a quarter of the American market; its electronics, cameras, and machine tools dominated theirs; its banks were the world's largest; its management methods were studied in every business school; and the land under the Imperial Palace in Tokyo was said to be worth more than California. American politicians spoke of a Japanese threat and American authors of Japan as number one. Then, in 1990, the **bubble** burst: the Nikkei index fell 60 percent in two years, land prices fell for fifteen, and the banks, holding loans secured on both, were insolvent and pretended not to be. The **lost decade** of the 1990s became two: growth averaged about 1 percent, deflation set in, government debt rose past 200 percent of GDP, and the lifetime-employment system frayed into precarious work for the young.[^4] Japan did not collapse; it remained rich, safe, orderly, and technologically advanced, with the world's longest life expectancy. But it stopped being the future, and its population, which peaked in 2008, began the fastest aging any large country had seen. The Liberal Democrats lost power in 1993 for the first time since 1955, briefly, and Japanese politics entered a period of short-lived prime ministers and unfinished reform.

## Democratization from below

**South Korea** under Park Chung-hee and, after his assassination in 1979, General Chun Doo-hwan, was a developmental dictatorship: extraordinary growth (the economy grew about 9 percent a year from 1963 to 1996), universal education, a rising middle class, and torture chambers. In May 1980 Chun's troops killed several hundred protesters in the city of Gwangju; the massacre became the founding trauma of Korean democracy. In June 1987, with the Seoul Olympics a year away and millions in the streets, Chun's designated successor conceded direct presidential elections; a new constitution followed, and in 1992 a civilian, Kim Young-sam, was elected, in 1997 the long-persecuted dissident Kim Dae-jung, and in 1996 Chun himself was tried and sentenced to death (later commuted) for Gwangju and for the coup.[^5] South Korea's democracy survived the Asian financial crisis of 1997, which nearly bankrupted it, and consolidated: it is the one clear case in modern history of a poor country becoming rich under a dictatorship and then removing the dictatorship by its own citizens' pressure.

**Taiwan** followed a similar arc under different conditions. The Nationalists had ruled it since 1949 under martial law, then the world's longest, as the Republic of China in exile, with a Taiwanese majority governed by a mainlander minority and a memory of the 1947 massacre that had begun their rule. Land reform, American aid, and export industrialization made Taiwan rich by the 1980s; Chiang Kai-shek's son, Chiang Ching-kuo, lifted martial law in 1987 and allowed an opposition party; his successor Lee Teng-hui, the first Taiwan-born president, held the first direct presidential election in 1996, which China greeted with missile tests; and in 2000 the opposition Democratic Progressive Party won the presidency, completing a peaceful transfer of power.[^6] Taiwan became a democracy with a Taiwanese rather than a Chinese identity, which the People's Republic regards as a province in rebellion, and the strait between them became, and remains, the most dangerous body of water in the world.

**North Korea** went the other way. Kim Il Sung died in 1994 and his son Kim Jong Il succeeded him, the first hereditary succession in a communist state; the Soviet subsidies that had sustained the economy had ended in 1991; and in 1995–1998 floods, the collapse of the state distribution system, and the regime's refusal to reform produced a famine that killed somewhere between 240,000 and 3.5 million people, the range itself a measure of how closed the country was.[^7] The regime survived by letting markets grow at the bottom while it built nuclear weapons at the top.

:::history How we got here
How did East Asia grow so fast? The **East Asian miracle** produced its own historiography. The World Bank's 1993 report credited market fundamentals: high savings, education, stable macroeconomics, openness to trade. The **developmental state** school (Chalmers Johnson on Japan, Alice Amsden on Korea, Robert Wade on Taiwan) credited the opposite: states that picked industries, directed credit, protected infant firms, and disciplined them with export targets, which the free-market account had to explain away. Paul Krugman argued in 1994 that the miracle was mostly capital accumulation and would slow, which Japan's stagnation and the 1997 crisis seemed to confirm and China's continued growth did not. The consensus, if there is one: land reform and universal education first, then export discipline under state guidance, then, in the successful cases, the state's withdrawal as firms matured; and in every case an American security umbrella and an open American market that were the Cold War's gift. Whether the model is transferable, and whether China's version, with its state firms and party control, is the same model or a different one, is the question that the next chapter's China poses. The democratic transitions have their own literature: Huntington's "third wave," the modernization thesis that wealth produces democracy (Korea and Taiwan confirm it; Singapore and China do not), and the argument that what mattered was not wealth but the specific coalitions, of students, workers, churches, and a middle class that had something to lose, that turned out in Gwangju and Seoul and Taipei.[^8]
:::

:::story Shenzhen
In 1979 Shenzhen was a market town of about 30,000 people on the Hong Kong border, notable mainly as the place where mainlanders drowned trying to swim to the colony. Deng made it a Special Economic Zone in 1980, an enclave where foreign firms could build factories with Chinese labor under market rules and tax breaks, insulated from the planned economy around it by a fence; his rationale, offered to skeptics in the party, was that if it failed it could be closed. Hong Kong manufacturers moved across the border for the wages, then Taiwanese, then everyone; migrants came from every province for the jobs; and the city grew at a pace no city had, to a million people by 1990, seven million by 2000, and some 17 million by 2020, with a GDP larger than Hong Kong's, the headquarters of Huawei, Tencent, and BYD, and the world's largest electronics manufacturing cluster.[^9] It is the fastest-built major city in history and the reform era in one place: a Communist party running a capitalist enclave as an experiment, discovering it worked, and extending the enclave until it was the country. The fence is gone; the border with Hong Kong, in a reversal the 1979 swimmers could not have imagined, is now crossed mainly by Hong Kongers going north to shop.
:::

:::key
The last quarter of the twentieth century turned East Asia from the poorest region on Earth into the fastest-growing, and it did so under three different political arrangements: a Communist party that kept power by delivering growth, a democracy that grew rich and then stalled, and two dictatorships that grew rich and then democratized under pressure from their own citizens. The Chinese case broke the assumption, held since Marx and Weber, that capitalism required liberal politics; the Korean and Taiwanese cases confirmed the assumption that wealth eventually demands them; and Japan showed that the miracle ends. Which lesson East Asia's future would follow is the question of the last chapter.
:::

## Summary

- Deng Xiaoping dismantled the communes, opened coastal zones to foreign capital, freed prices in stages, and produced three decades of 10 percent growth and the largest poverty reduction in history, while keeping the party's monopoly; Tiananmen in 1989 fixed the terms.
- Japan peaked in the 1980s as the presumed future, and its bubble burst in 1990 into two decades of stagnation, deflation, and the fastest aging of any large country.
- South Korea and Taiwan grew rich under dictatorships and then became democracies through their own citizens' pressure, in 1987–1997 and 1987–2000 respectively.
- North Korea survived the end of Soviet subsidies through a famine that killed hundreds of thousands to millions and by building nuclear weapons.
- The East Asian miracle is explained by land reform, education, export discipline under state guidance, and an American umbrella; whether the model transfers, and whether growth requires democracy, are the open questions.
