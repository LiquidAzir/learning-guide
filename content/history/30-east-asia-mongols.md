## How did Mongol conquest both connect and devastate Eurasia?

Chapter 29 left the Song rich and militarily weak, the Jin ruling northern China, Goryeo devout, and Japan under its first shogunate. All of them were about to meet the largest land empire in history. This chapter is about the Mongol century in East Asia and, because the Mongols joined the continent, about what it meant for the world; it is the point at which the European and East Asian sections of this guide touch, and the reader of chapter 16 will meet the plague here from the other end of its road.

## Temüjin

The steppe north of China had produced empires before (the Xiongnu, the Turks, the Uighurs), and they had risen and fallen by the same logic: a leader united the clans by conquest and plunder, the tribute of settled neighbors held the confederation together, and the succession quarrel broke it. What Temüjin did was the same thing, done better. Born around 1162 to a minor Mongol chief who was poisoned when the boy was nine, abandoned with his mother and siblings by their clan, enslaved for a time by rivals, he built a following by loyalty and terror, destroyed every rival tribe, and in 1206 was proclaimed **Chinggis Khan** ("Genghis" in the older spelling), universal ruler, of all the peoples of the felt tents.[^1]

His innovations were organizational. He broke the tribes and reorganized the whole population into decimal military units (tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands) that mixed clans and answered to him; he promoted by merit and loyalty rather than birth, so his generals included a former enemy who had shot his horse; he imposed a written law, the *yasa*, and adopted the Uyghur script for Mongolian; he built a relay post system, the *yam*, that carried messages 200 miles a day; and he made his army, of perhaps 100,000 mounted archers each with several horses, the most mobile and disciplined force in the world, capable of coordinating movements across hundreds of miles and of the feigned retreat that destroyed every army that pursued it.[^2] He also made terror policy: cities that resisted were destroyed and their populations killed, and the news preceded the army so that the next city surrendered.

He attacked the Jin in 1211, the Central Asian empire of Khwarazm in 1219 (its shah had killed his envoys; its cities, Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv, Nishapur, were depopulated), and died in 1227 campaigning against the Tangut. His empire then ran from the Pacific to the Caspian. His sons and grandsons took the rest: the Jin fell in 1234, Kiev in 1240, Hungary and Poland were ravaged in 1241 (the army withdrew in 1242, traditionally because of the Great Khan's death, though a wet spring, stone fortifications, and the commanders' own calculations are now also argued), Baghdad and the Abbasid caliphate in 1258, Korea after thirty years of invasions (1231–1259), and the Southern Song, whose river cities and navy held out for forty years, in 1279.[^3] By then the empire had split into four khanates, in China, Persia, Central Asia, and Russia, but it still stretched from Korea to Hungary, one sixth of the world's land, and it was the largest contiguous empire there has ever been.

## The cost

The Mongol conquests killed people on a scale that has few parallels. Persian and Arab chroniclers give figures for individual cities (1.3 million at Merv, 1.7 million at Nishapur) that are the round numbers of chapter 3, but the depopulation of Khwarazm and Khorasan is archaeologically real and Persia did not recover its pre-Mongol population for centuries; northern China's registered population fell from about 50 million under the Jin to under 10 million in the first Yuan census, though how much was death, flight, and non-registration is argued.[^4] Estimates for total deaths range from 20 to 40 million or more; the honest statement is that it was one of the two or three largest demographic catastrophes before the twentieth century and cannot be measured precisely. The Mongols were not more cruel than other conquerors; they were more successful, and they used massacre as an instrument with a consistency that the sources on every side, Chinese, Persian, Russian, and their own, confirm.

## The Yuan

Chinggis's grandson **Khubilai** (Kublai) completed the conquest of China and ruled it from 1260 to 1294 as a Chinese-style emperor of a new dynasty, the **Yuan**, from a new capital at Beijing (then Dadu, "great capital"), the first time a foreign dynasty had ruled all of China. He kept the Chinese bureaucracy but placed Mongols and Central Asians above Chinese in a four-tier ethnic hierarchy, suspended the examinations for decades, taxed through Muslim and Uighur financiers, and tolerated every religion (Tibetan Buddhism was the court's, but Daoists, Confucians, Muslims, and Nestorian Christians all had privileges). He extended the Grand Canal to Beijing, built roads and post stations, issued paper money on a scale that ended in inflation, and received Marco Polo, whose account of the Great Khan's court, its wealth, its cities, and its paper money was read across Europe for two centuries and inspired Columbus.[^5] He also failed twice against Japan (1274 and 1281), where his fleets, the second reportedly 4,000 ships and 140,000 men, were wrecked by storms the Japanese named *kamikaze*, "divine wind," and failed against Vietnam and Java.

The Yuan lasted less than a century after Khubilai. Succession fights, financial collapse, Yellow River floods, famine, and possibly plague in the 1330s and 1340s, and a Chinese population that never accepted Mongol rule produced rebellions, and in 1368 a peasant rebel named Zhu Yuanzhang, once a Buddhist novice, took Beijing and founded the Ming (chapter 31). The Mongols went back to the steppe and remained a threat for two hundred years.

## The Pax Mongolica

The empire's other face was connection. For about a century, from the 1240s to the 1340s, a merchant could travel under one authority from the Black Sea to Beijing, and many did. The post roads carried envoys, missionaries, and traders; Genoese and Venetian colonies sat on the Black Sea at the road's western end; the papacy sent Franciscans to the Great Khan and the Khan sent embassies to the pope; Chinese engineers served in Persia and Persian astronomers in China; gunpowder, printing, the compass, and Chinese medical knowledge moved west; Persian and Arab mathematics, astronomy, and cobalt blue (for the Yuan's blue-and-white porcelain) moved east.[^6] Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, and William of Rubruck are the names we know; there were thousands of others. It was the first time the Eurasian continent was one economic and informational space, and it happened because the most destructive army in history had removed every border.

The connection carried death as well. **Plague**, endemic among rodents in the Tian Shan and the steppe, traveled the Mongol trade routes west, reaching the Genoese port of Caffa in the Crimea in 1346 and Europe in 1347, where it became the Black Death of chapter 16; ancient DNA has traced the strain to Kyrgyzstan around 1338.[^7] Whether it reached China in the same decades and contributed to the Yuan's collapse is argued and the Chinese sources are ambiguous. The Pax Mongolica ended with the plague and the khanates' breakup, and the land route closed; when Europe went looking for Asia again in the fifteenth century, it went by sea (chapter 18), because the road Marco Polo took was gone.

:::story The kamikaze
In the summer of 1281 two Mongol fleets, one from Korea and one from the conquered Song coast, converged on Hakata Bay in Kyushu with an army drawn from Mongols, Chinese, and Koreans. The Japanese, warned by the failed invasion of 1274, had built a stone wall along the bay and fought the landings for seven weeks. On 15 August a typhoon struck; the fleets, crowded into the bay, were driven onto each other and the shore, and the Japanese sources say most of the ships were lost and the survivors on the beaches were killed. Underwater archaeology since 1980 has recovered anchors, weapons, and hull timbers, some of the ships badly built or riverine, from the bay floor.[^8] Japan concluded that the gods protected it, an idea it would revisit in 1945 when it named its suicide pilots for the wind. The Kamakura shogunate, which had organized the defense, was bankrupted by it, had no conquered land to reward its warriors with, and fell in 1333; and Japan, alone among the settled societies on the Mongol frontier, was never conquered, which is one reason its history diverged from Korea's and China's.
:::

:::history How we got here
The Mongols were the barbarians of every settled civilization's history: Persian, Arab, Russian, Chinese, and European chroniclers agreed on the destruction and little else, and the eighteenth-century Enlightenment made them the type of the Asiatic destroyer. In the twentieth century the picture was revised in two directions. Soviet and then Mongolian scholarship, and Western historians such as Thomas Allsen and David Morgan, reconstructed the empire from its own sources, above all the *Secret History of the Mongols*, and from Persian and Chinese administrative records, and found a state that governed, taxed, adjudicated, and promoted trade and religious toleration with sophistication. Jack Weatherford's popular *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World* (2004) went further and made the Mongols the founders of globalization, free trade, and religious freedom, which most specialists think overstates it as much as the older view understated it. The current picture holds both: an empire that killed on a scale that was, for its time, unmatched, and that then governed a connected Eurasia for a century with more competence than its subjects credited. In Mongolia itself, Chinggis Khan, suppressed under Soviet influence, has been the national founder since 1990, and his face is on the currency.[^9]
:::

:::key
The Mongols did three things to East Asia and one to the world. They ended the Song and with it whatever trajectory Song China was on. They ruled China for the first time as a foreign dynasty and set the precedent the Manchus would follow. They tested Japan and failed, and Japan's escape is one root of its distinct path. And they joined Eurasia into one space for a century, moving technology, ideas, merchants, and plague across it, so that Europe learned of China's wealth, lost a third of its people to a Central Asian bacterium, and, when the road closed, went to sea to find the way back. The world of chapter 18 was made by the road of chapter 30.
:::

Can increased trade along Mongol-controlled routes coexist with catastrophic destruction from conquest?

:::answer One way to reason it through
Yes. The effects differ by place, group, and phase of rule. Secure routes for some merchants can emerge after campaigns that devastate other communities. A useful account does not turn benefits to one group into compensation for another's losses; it tracks both with their timing.

## Summary

- Chinggis Khan united the steppe by 1206 with a decimal army, promotion by merit, written law, a post system, and terror as policy; his descendants built the largest contiguous empire in history, from Korea to Hungary.
- The conquests killed tens of millions and depopulated Persia and northern China; the figures are uncertain, the scale is not.
- Khubilai ruled China as the Yuan from Beijing, the first foreign dynasty over all of China, tolerated all religions, ranked Mongols above Chinese, and failed twice against Japan.
- The Pax Mongolica joined Eurasia for a century, moving gunpowder, printing, the compass, and Marco Polo's report west and Persian science east; it also carried the Black Death.
- Mongol expansion had limits: campaigns against Japan, Vietnam, and Java failed. The Yuan fell in 1368, but Mongol power continued beyond China. The later disruption of overland routes was one influence on European efforts to reach Asian markets by sea.
