## Recap

Chapter 26 ended with Qin's conquest of the last rival state in 221 BCE. This chapter covers the two dynasties that made the Chinese empire: the Qin, which lasted fifteen years and built everything, and the Han, which lasted four hundred and decided what the empire would mean. The Han were contemporaries of the Roman Republic and Empire, knew of each other dimly through the Silk Road, and never met; the comparison runs through the chapter because it is the best way to see what was distinctive about each.

## The First Emperor

Ying Zheng, king of Qin from the age of thirteen, completed the conquest at thirty-eight and took a new title, **Shi Huangdi**, First Emperor, using words that had meant gods and sage-kings. In eleven years he and his chancellor Li Si, a Legalist, imposed on the whole of China what Qin had done to itself. The old states were abolished and replaced by thirty-six commanderies under appointed governors, with no hereditary lords. Weights, measures, axle widths, coinage, and, above all, the **script** were standardized: the small-seal characters of Qin became the writing of the empire, so that a document written in Canton could be read in Beijing, whatever the dialects, and could be for the next two thousand years. Roads radiated from the capital; canals joined river systems; existing frontier walls were joined into a barrier against the steppe that later dynasties would rebuild as the Great Wall; 300,000 men were sent north against the Xiongnu and 500,000 south to conquer what is now Guangdong and northern Vietnam.[^1] Laws were uniform, punishments were severe, and in 213 BCE, at Li Si's urging, the emperor ordered the burning of all histories except Qin's and all philosophical works in private hands, and, tradition says, the execution of 460 scholars. The Confucian tradition never forgave him, and he is the type of the tyrant in Chinese memory.

He also built his tomb. Some 700,000 laborers worked on a complex near modern Xi'an whose central mound, never excavated, is described by the historian Sima Qian as containing a model of the empire with rivers of mercury (soil tests show elevated mercury) and crossbows set to fire at intruders. In 1974 farmers digging a well found the **Terracotta Army**: some 8,000 life-size soldiers, each with an individual face, with horses, chariots, and bronze weapons, buried in pits to guard him.[^2] The emperor sought immortality through alchemists' elixirs and died at forty-nine in 210 BCE, probably of the mercury in them, on a tour of the provinces; his ministers hid the death, installed a weak son, and the empire rose against the Qin within a year. In 206 BCE the capital was sacked and the dynasty was gone. It had lasted fifteen years and had made the Chinese state.

## The Han

The winner of the civil war that followed was Liu Bang, a peasant who had been a minor police official, the first commoner to found a Chinese dynasty. The **Han** (206 BCE–220 CE, with a fifteen-year interruption) kept everything of Qin that worked, the commanderies, the standardized script and measures, the appointed bureaucracy, the legal code, and dropped what had made Qin hated: they lowered taxes, softened punishments, and, from the reign of Emperor Wu (141–87 BCE), adopted **Confucianism** as the state's ideology. An imperial academy was founded in 124 BCE to teach the Five Classics; officials were to be recommended for learning and virtue; and the Legalist machine was given a Confucian face.[^3] The synthesis, Legalist in structure and Confucian in justification, a bureaucracy of scholars administering a code of laws in the name of a virtuous emperor, is the Chinese imperial state, and it lasted with modifications until 1911.

Emperor Wu was the Han's Augustus: fifty-four years on the throne, wars on every frontier, the conquest of Korea's northwest, Yunnan, and the Tarim basin, and a state monopoly on salt and iron to pay for it. His envoy Zhang Qian, sent west in 138 BCE to find allies against the Xiongnu, came back thirteen years later with news of Central Asia, Persia, and, at second hand, Rome, and the trade that followed along the oases of the Tarim, carrying silk west and horses, glass, and, later, Buddhism east, is what a nineteenth-century German geographer named the **Silk Road**.[^4] Roman senators wore Chinese silk by the first century CE and Pliny complained of the drain of gold to pay for it; Chinese sources describe Da Qin, "Great Qin," a western empire of walled cities and honest merchants; neither ever quite reached the other.

The Han also produced the historian. **Sima Qian** (c. 145–86 BCE), the court astrologer, wrote the *Records of the Grand Historian*, 130 chapters covering everything from the Yellow Emperor to his own day: annals of rulers, tables, treatises on rites, music, the calendar, and the economy, and biographies of generals, ministers, assassins, merchants, and philosophers, with sources compared and the author's own judgments appended. He finished it after being castrated for defending a disgraced general, choosing the punishment over suicide so the book could be completed.[^5] It is the model of every later Chinese history, and China's dynastic histories, compiled by each dynasty for its predecessor, are the longest continuous historical record in the world.

## The Xiongnu, and the steppe problem

The Han's great enemy was the **Xiongnu**, a confederation of steppe nomads north of the Wall, united around 209 BCE under a ruler called the *chanyu*, whose mounted archers could raid a thousand miles of frontier and vanish. The first Han emperor lost a battle to them and paid tribute for sixty years; Emperor Wu sent armies of 100,000 cavalry into the steppe and broke their power at enormous cost; the Han settled the remnants inside the frontier as allies. Ancient DNA from Xiongnu cemeteries in Mongolia has shown a people of extraordinary genetic diversity even within single families, an empire built from many populations and held together by elite kinship and, notably, by high-status women.[^6] The relationship, a settled agrarian empire that could not conquer the steppe and a steppe power that could not hold the farmland, alternating between war, tribute, trade, and marriage alliance, is the structural fact of Chinese history for two thousand years, and the Xiongnu are its first instance. The Great Wall is its monument, and the Mongols and Manchus, who did conquer China, are its exceptions.

## What Han China was like

The census of 2 CE counted 57.7 million people, about the same as the Roman empire at the same date, in twelve million households. Most were free peasant farmers on small holdings, taxed on land and person and liable to a month's labor service a year and two years of military service; slavery existed but was marginal, perhaps 1 percent, against 10 to 15 percent across the Roman empire and far more in Italy.[^7] The capital, Chang'an, held perhaps 250,000 people within walls twenty-five kilometers around; Luoyang, the Later Han capital, was similar. Iron tools and the ox-drawn plow were universal; paper was invented, or at least perfected, by a court eunuch, Cai Lun, in 105 CE; the wheelbarrow, the seismograph, the stern-post rudder, and cast-iron production on an industrial scale were Han. Confucian scholars ran the government; great landowning families dominated the provinces; eunuchs and the emperor's in-laws fought for control of the court; and Daoism, in the late Han, became a popular religion with a church, priests, and millenarian rebellions.

:::story The Salt and Iron debates
In 81 BCE the Han court convened a debate between imperial officials and Confucian scholars on whether the state should keep its monopolies on salt, iron, and liquor, and its policy of buying grain cheap and selling dear to stabilize prices. The officials argued that the monopolies paid for the frontier armies and that the state must manage the economy. The scholars argued that the state should not compete with its people, that monopolies enriched officials and produced bad iron at high prices, that a virtuous ruler needed no such revenues because he would not fight such wars, and that farming, not trade, was the basis of a good society. The record, the *Discourses on Salt and Iron*, is the oldest surviving debate on economic policy in any civilization, and every position in it, state versus market, revenue versus virtue, frontier security versus domestic welfare, has been argued in China ever since.[^8] The scholars won the argument and the officials kept the monopolies. That, too, has recurred.
:::

## The fall

The Han fell slowly, from causes that Chinese historians made into the pattern of dynastic decline. Great families accumulated land and the peasants who worked it fell off the tax rolls, so revenue fell while the burden on those still registered rose. Child emperors were controlled by their mothers' families, who were destroyed by eunuchs, who were destroyed by generals. In 184 CE the **Yellow Turbans**, a Daoist millenarian movement, rose across eastern China; the generals who suppressed them kept their armies; and by 190 the emperor was a puppet of whichever warlord held him. In 220 the last Han emperor abdicated to the son of the warlord Cao Cao, and China divided into three kingdoms, the beginning of a division that would last, with one brief interruption, for 370 years.[^9] The parallel with Rome's third century is close and the outcome different: Rome's West never reunited, and China did.

:::history How we got here
The comparison between Han China and Rome was made in the eighteenth century by Europeans who admired China, and it has been made ever since with the answer shifting. Nineteenth-century Europe saw a despotic, static China against a dynamic Rome whose heirs conquered the world; Max Weber made China's bureaucracy and Confucian ethic the reason it did not develop capitalism. Since about 1980 comparative historians (Scheidel, Lewis, Mutschler) have set the two empires side by side as the two great agrarian empires of antiquity, alike in size, population, administrative reach, and reliance on peasant taxation, and different in what mattered: China had a unified script and a scholar-bureaucracy chosen for learning, Rome had law and a citizenship that expanded; China's elite were officials, Rome's were landowners and soldiers; China had almost no slavery and Rome had a great deal; and when both fell to similar pressures, China's memory of unity, kept in its script and its classics, let it reunite, and Rome's did not. Why China reunited and Europe never did is, for comparative history, the central question of the two millennia that followed.[^10]
:::

:::key
The Qin built the state in fifteen years and were destroyed by the hatred it earned; the Han kept the state and gave it a Confucian conscience, and the combination, a centralized bureaucracy administering uniform law and taxation across a continent in the name of a virtuous emperor and the classics, is what "China" has meant as a polity ever since. Its recurring problems were set in the Han too: the steppe frontier that could be bought or fought but never solved, the great families who swallowed the land and the tax base, and the court in which eunuchs, in-laws, and generals fought for a child emperor. Every later dynasty faced all three.
:::

## Summary

- The First Emperor abolished the old states and standardized script, law, measures, and administration across China in eleven years; his dynasty fell within four years of his death, hated for its severity.
- The Han kept Qin's structure, softened its punishments, and adopted Confucianism as state ideology from 124 BCE, creating the scholar-bureaucracy that ran China until 1911.
- Emperor Wu expanded the empire, fought the Xiongnu, and opened the Silk Road; Sima Qian wrote the model for all later Chinese history.
- Han China had 58 million people, little slavery, a free peasantry, paper, and an industrial iron economy; it debated state versus market in 81 BCE in terms still current.
- It fell to land concentration, court factions, and warlord generals after 184 CE, and, unlike Rome, would reunite.
