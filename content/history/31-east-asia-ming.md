## Recap

The Mongols fell in China in 1368 and their century's connections closed. This chapter covers the three centuries in which each East Asian society rebuilt on its own terms: Ming China as a centralized, inward, Confucian agrarian empire that was also, in its later years, the world's largest silver importer; Joseon Korea as the most thoroughly Neo-Confucian state that ever existed; and Japan as a century of civil war ending in unification. It ends in 1644, when the Ming fall and the Manchus take Beijing, and 1600, when Japan's last unifier wins the battle that will give it 250 years of peace.

## The Ming founder

Zhu Yuanzhang, born to a destitute peasant family in 1328, orphaned by famine and plague at sixteen, a beggar and Buddhist novice, joined a millenarian rebel army at twenty-four, commanded it within a few years, defeated every rival, and took Beijing from the Yuan in 1368. He ruled for thirty years as the **Hongwu** emperor, and he ruled from the peasant's view of the world: the countryside was virtue, the city and the merchant were corruption, the official was a thief until proven otherwise, and the emperor's job was to protect the peasant from all of them. He abolished the office of chief minister and ran the government himself through a secretariat, so that the Ming emperor was the most personally powerful ruler China had had; he registered every household and every field in the empire in the Yellow Registers and Fish-Scale Maps; he fixed the land tax in grain and labor service, forbade foreign trade except through official tribute missions, and organized villages into self-policing groups of 110 households. He also executed tens of thousands of officials in purges, flogged others in open court, and issued instructions to his descendants that they were never to change any of it.[^1] They mostly did not.

His son, the **Yongle** emperor (1402–1424), who took the throne by civil war, was the exception: he moved the capital to Beijing and built the Forbidden City, compiled the classics in an encyclopedia of 11,000 volumes, and sent the eunuch admiral **Zheng He** on seven voyages (1405–1433) with fleets of up to 300 ships and 27,000 men, the largest wooden ships ever built, to Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf, and East Africa. They carried tribute and porcelain, brought back giraffes and ambassadors, and demonstrated a naval reach no European power would have for a century. Then, after Yongle's death, the Confucian officials, who thought the voyages a eunuch's extravagance, stopped them, the ships rotted, the records were destroyed, and China's maritime withdrawal was complete by 1500, sixty years before the Portuguese reached Canton.[^2] Why is asked as often as "why Europe," and the answers are the same in reverse: a unified empire with no rival to fear and nothing it needed from outside had no reason to keep paying for the fleet.

## The Ming state and society

Ming China was the largest and most populous state in the world for its whole existence: perhaps 60 to 85 million people in 1400 and 150 to 200 million by 1600, when Europe had about 80 million. It was governed by some 20,000 civil officials, recruited by examinations that were now the only route, in a system so stable that its structure of six ministries, provinces, prefectures, and counties barely changed until 1911. The court, in the Forbidden City, had thousands of eunuchs who ran the emperor's household, his secret police, and, when the emperor was a child or a recluse, the state; several Ming emperors did not meet their ministers for years, and the eunuch dictatorships of the 1440s, 1500s, and 1620s are the dynasty's disgrace in Confucian historiography.[^3] The Great Wall, as tourists see it today, is Ming: rebuilt in brick and stone from the 1470s after the Mongols captured an emperor in 1449, at ruinous cost, and never entirely effective.

Hongwu's agrarian vision did not hold. From the sixteenth century, the Yangzi delta became a commercial economy of cotton and silk textiles, porcelain from Jingdezhen's kilns, tea, and market towns, and the whole system ran on **silver**, which the Ming state adopted as its unit of tax from the 1570s and which China did not produce. Japan's mines and, above all, Spanish America supplied it: perhaps a third of all the silver mined in the Americas between 1500 and 1800 ended in China, carried across the Pacific to Manila and traded for silk and porcelain (chapter 18).[^4] Late Ming China was as commercial, urban, and literate as any society on Earth, with a publishing industry, vernacular novels (*Journey to the West*, *The Plum in the Golden Vase*), a philosophy of individual moral intuition (Wang Yangming) that challenged Zhu Xi's orthodoxy, and Jesuit missionaries at court, led by Matteo Ricci, who learned Chinese, dressed as scholars, and brought European astronomy, mathematics, and maps, while sending back the reports that made Enlightenment Europe admire China.[^5] It was also a state whose fixed tax quotas could not capture the new wealth, whose emperors were often absent, and whose frontier was about to break.

## Joseon Korea

In 1392 a Goryeo general, Yi Seong-gye, took the throne and founded the **Joseon** dynasty, which would last 518 years, longer than any Chinese dynasty. Its founders were Neo-Confucian reformers who saw Goryeo's Buddhism as corruption, and they built the most thoroughly Confucian state in history: Buddhism was disestablished and its monasteries stripped; the *yangban* aristocracy of scholar-officials monopolized the examinations and the land; ancestor rites, patrilineal descent, and the seclusion of upper-class women were enforced by law; and the king ruled with, and was constantly lectured by, a bureaucracy of Confucian censors whose duty was to tell him when he was wrong.[^6] Korea was a tributary of the Ming, sent embassies to Beijing several times a year, adopted the Ming calendar and ritual, and regarded itself as the truest guardian of Confucian civilization, a conviction that would outlast the Ming.

Its greatest king, **Sejong** (1418–1450), sponsored the invention of the Korean alphabet, **Hangul**, promulgated in 1446: twenty-eight letters (twenty-four in modern use) whose shapes depict the position of the tongue and lips in making the sound, arranged in syllable blocks, learnable, the preface said, by a clever man in a morning and a fool in ten days. Linguists regard it as the most scientifically designed script in use anywhere.[^7] The yangban despised it as a script for women and commoners and went on writing Chinese for four centuries; it became Korea's national script only in the twentieth. Joseon also produced the world's first standardized rain gauge (1441), a sundial and water clock, movable-type printing on a large scale, and a Buddhist-turned-Confucian court culture of great austerity. In 1592 it was invaded by Japan.

## Japan: from Kamakura to Sekigahara

The Kamakura shogunate fell in 1333, bankrupted by the Mongol defense. The **Ashikaga** shoguns who replaced it ruled from Kyoto's Muromachi district for two centuries and controlled little beyond it; power lay with provincial lords, and after the Onin War (1467–1477) destroyed Kyoto and the shogunate's authority, Japan entered the **Sengoku**, the Warring States, a century in which some 250 domain lords (**daimyo**) fought each other without any central authority at all. The daimyo built castles, fielded armies of peasant foot-soldiers with pikes, and ruled their domains as small states with their own laws and surveys; the samurai became a professional warrior class; and the Zen Buddhism of the Kamakura period, with its tea ceremony, ink painting, and Noh theater, flourished in the ruins.[^8]

In 1543 a Chinese junk with Portuguese aboard was blown onto the island of Tanegashima, and the Japanese saw the matchlock gun. Within a decade Japanese smiths were producing them by the thousand, and within a generation Japan had more guns than any European country. Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries followed; Francis Xavier arrived in 1549, and by 1600 there were perhaps 300,000 Japanese Christians, including several daimyo. Three men then unified the country. **Oda Nobunaga**, a minor daimyo of genius and ferocity, used massed arquebuses to destroy a cavalry army at Nagashino in 1575, burned the militant Buddhist monasteries that had defied every government, and controlled a third of Japan when a vassal assassinated him in 1582. His general **Toyotomi Hideyoshi**, a peasant's son, finished the conquest by 1590, disarmed the peasantry, froze the class structure, surveyed the land, and then, in 1592 and 1597, invaded Korea with 160,000 men in the largest overseas expedition Japan had mounted, meaning to conquer China. The Japanese took Seoul and Pyongyang, Ming armies came south, the Korean admiral Yi Sun-sin destroyed the Japanese fleets with his iron-covered turtle ships, and the war devastated Korea for seven years until Hideyoshi died in 1598 and the Japanese withdrew.[^9] **Tokugawa Ieyasu**, Hideyoshi's ally, won the battle of Sekigahara in 1600 against the coalition loyal to Hideyoshi's heir, became shogun in 1603, and founded a regime that lasted until 1868. His settlement is chapter 32.

## The Ming fall

The Ming died of the causes Chinese historians had catalogued since the Han. Fiscal: the land tax was fixed in 1580 and could not be raised, while the Korean war, the Great Wall, and a court of 100,000 eunuchs and dependents consumed it. Climatic: the **Little Ice Age** brought, in the 1630s and 1640s, the worst droughts in five centuries, with a precursor megadrought in the 1580s now visible in tree rings; harvests failed, famine spread, and epidemics followed. Political: the Wanli emperor did not hold an audience for some twenty-five years; the eunuch Wei Zhongxian terrorized the bureaucracy in the 1620s; factions fought while the treasury emptied. Military: the **Manchus**, a Jurchen people of the northeast unified by Nurhaci from 1616, built a state on the Ming frontier with a Chinese-style administration and a Mongol-style army. In 1644 a peasant rebel, Li Zicheng, took Beijing; the last Ming emperor hanged himself on a hill behind the palace; a Ming general opened the Great Wall's gate to the Manchus to expel the rebel; and the Manchus, having expelled him, kept the capital and proclaimed the **Qing**.[^10] It took them forty years to conquer the south. They would rule China until 1912.

:::key
The Ming rebuilt China as the agrarian Confucian empire Hongwu wanted and then became, despite him, the most commercial society in the world, running on American silver and selling porcelain to Europe; the two Chinas, the fortress and the market, coexisted uneasily and the state never learned to tax the second. Korea became Confucianism's most faithful and austere state and invented the world's best alphabet, which its elite refused to use. Japan tore itself apart, took the gun from the Portuguese, and reassembled itself under three warlords into a regime that would seal the country. All three would face the West in the nineteenth century from positions fixed in the sixteenth.

## Summary

- Hongwu rebuilt China as a personally governed agrarian fortress, registered everything, and banned private foreign trade; Yongle built Beijing and sent Zheng He's fleets, which the officials then scrapped.
- Ming China, the world's largest state, became commercial and urban despite its founder, ran on American silver from the 1570s, produced great vernacular fiction, and received the Jesuits.
- Joseon Korea was the most thoroughly Neo-Confucian state in history, a Ming tributary, and the inventor of Hangul, the alphabet its elite refused to use.
- Japan collapsed into the Sengoku, adopted the Portuguese gun at once, was unified by Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu, and invaded Korea disastrously in the 1590s.
- The Ming fell in 1644 to fiscal rigidity, Little Ice Age drought, court paralysis, peasant revolt, and the Manchus, who founded the Qing.
