## Recap

The Ancient section ended with the Roman West broken into Germanic kingdoms and the Mediterranean about to split three ways. This chapter opens European history in the five centuries once called the Dark Ages: the rise of the Franks, the arrival of Islam, the conversion of the pagan north, Charlemagne's empire and its collapse, the Viking raids, and the slow emergence, by 1000, of the kingdoms that Europe still has. The name "Europe" as a place with a shared identity, rather than a Greek geographical term, dates from this period, and it was coined to mean Latin Christendom.

## The world of 600

Picture Western Europe around 600. Rome, a few tens of thousands of people among ruins built for a million. No roads maintained, no coinage in gold in most places, no long-distance trade to speak of except in luxuries for kings and relics for churches. Perhaps 90 percent of people farmed, in a landscape where forest was reclaiming fields. Literacy was confined to the clergy, and books were copied by hand on animal skin, so that a Bible cost the hides of two hundred sheep. Kings ruled from wherever they happened to be, with a household of warriors bound by gifts and oaths, and law was custom, remembered rather than written, with compensation tariffs for every injury: so many shillings for a thumb, so many for a tooth.[^1]

Three things held it together. **The Church**, the one institution that survived from Rome, with its bishops in the old cities, its Latin, its monasteries, and its claim to a universal authority. **The kings**, Frankish, Visigothic, Lombard, Anglo-Saxon, who found that the Church could crown them, write for them, and make their rule legitimate to Roman and barbarian alike, and who converted, from Clovis of the Franks around 500 to the last Scandinavian kings around 1000. And **the memory of Rome**, which every one of them claimed in some form; the Roman empire was the only model of a large state anyone had.

## Islam arrives

In 632 the Prophet Muhammad died in Arabia, and within a century his followers ruled from Spain to the Indus. The story is Islamic history's and belongs to a future section; its effect on Europe was decisive. The Arabs took Syria and Egypt from Byzantium in the 630s and 640s, destroyed the Persian empire, swept across North Africa, and crossed into Spain in 711, destroying the Visigothic kingdom in a few years. A Frankish army under Charles Martel stopped a raiding force at Tours in 732, an event nineteenth-century historians made into the salvation of Christendom and modern ones regard as significant but not decisive; Muslim forces held Narbonne until 759, and the advance faltered less because of Tours than because Spain absorbed their energies and the Umayyad caliphate had troubles of its own.[^2] Muslim **al-Andalus** would last in some form until 1492, and Córdoba in the tenth century, with its libraries, its lamps, its paved streets, and, by cautious estimates, perhaps 100,000 people (older estimates run far higher), was the largest and most learned city in Western Europe, with no rival.

Islam's arrival did two things to Europe. It cut the Mediterranean in half, ending Rome's inland sea; the Belgian historian Henri Pirenne argued in the 1930s that this, not the Germanic invasions, ended the ancient world, by killing the trade that had sustained the cities, and though his thesis has been much qualified, the shift of Europe's center of gravity from the Mediterranean to the north is real and dates from this century.[^3] And it made Latin Christendom a frontier society with a rival that was richer, more literate, and more powerful, a condition that would shape it for eight hundred years.

## Monks

While kings fought, monks copied. **Monasticism** had come from Egypt (chapter 13), and in the West it took the form Benedict of Nursia gave it around 530: a community under an abbot, bound for life by a written Rule, dividing the day among prayer, reading, and manual work, and, as it happened, copying books.[^4] Irish monks, converted by Patrick's mission in the fifth century and cut off from the continent, developed a fierce ascetic Christianity and a scholarship of their own, founded monasteries across Britain and Europe (Iona, Lindisfarne, Luxeuil, Bobbio, St Gall), and produced the illuminated manuscripts, the Book of Kells among them, that are the period's great art. Anglo-Saxon England, converted by Roman and Irish missions in the seventh century, produced Bede, whose *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (731) is the first history of a European nation and the source of the AD dating system's general use.[^5]

Almost every classical Latin text that survives, survives because a monk copied it between 750 and 1000. The monasteries were also Europe's largest landowners, its hospitals, its schools, its guesthouses, and its agricultural innovators; the Cistercians of the twelfth century would be its first industrialists. When the phrase "Dark Ages" was coined by Renaissance humanists, they meant that the Latin was bad. The monks kept the Latin at all.

## Charlemagne

The **Franks**, who had taken Gaul in the fifth century, were the one Germanic kingdom that lasted and grew. In 751 the Carolingian family, who had run the kingdom as "mayors of the palace" for a century, deposed the last of the old Merovingian kings with the pope's blessing and took the crown; in 768 **Charles**, whom later ages called Charlemagne, Charles the Great, inherited it. In forty-six years of nearly annual campaigning he conquered the Lombard kingdom in Italy, the Saxons in Germany (a thirty-year war of forced conversion in which, one year, he had 4,500 prisoners beheaded), Bavaria, and the Avar khanate on the Danube, and pushed into Spain, where a rearguard action at Roncesvalles in 778 became the *Song of Roland*. By 800 he ruled everything from the Pyrenees to the Elbe and from the North Sea to Rome: most of Latin Christendom.[^6]

On Christmas Day 800, in St Peter's in Rome, Pope Leo III crowned him **emperor**. What each of them meant by it is argued: the Franks had a great king who ruled as much as any emperor; the pope had been rescued by Charles from his Roman enemies and needed a protector; Constantinople, which considered itself the only Roman empire, was at that moment ruled by a woman, which Westerners took as a vacancy. Charlemagne's biographer says he would not have entered the church had he known what the pope intended, which nobody believes.[^7] The consequences were large: the Western empire was revived as an idea, tied to the papacy and to Germany, and it would last as the Holy Roman Empire until 1806, and the coronation established that emperors were made by popes, a claim popes and emperors would fight over for the next five centuries.

Charlemagne's court at Aachen gathered the scholars of Europe, above all Alcuin of York, and set them to correcting the Bible, standardizing the liturgy, reforming the script (the clear Carolingian minuscule that became, via the Renaissance, the lowercase letters you are reading), and founding schools. This **Carolingian renaissance** was a rescue operation rather than a flowering, but it worked: the copying of classical texts peaked under Charlemagne and his son, and most of what we have passed through their scriptoria.[^8] He also issued laws, standardized coinage on a silver penny that Europe kept for centuries, and governed through counts supervised by traveling inspectors, the closest thing to administration the West had seen since Rome.

## Collapse and the Vikings

It did not last. Charlemagne's grandsons divided the empire in 843 into three kingdoms whose western and eastern parts are, roughly, France and Germany, and fought each other. At the same time the north, the east, and the south erupted. From Scandinavia came the **Vikings**, from about 790: pagan seafarers whose long ships could cross the North Atlantic and row up the Seine, who sacked monasteries (rich, undefended, on the coast), then cities, then extorted tribute, then conquered. They took half of England, settled Normandy, founded Dublin, took Kiev, and founded the Russian state (the "Rus" were Swedes), reached Constantinople and Baghdad as traders and Iceland, Greenland, and, briefly, Newfoundland as settlers, and sold slaves everywhere.[^9] From the east came the **Magyars**, steppe horsemen who raided Germany and Italy for fifty years before being defeated in 955 and settling as the Kingdom of Hungary. From the south, Muslim raiders based in Sicily and Provence sacked Rome's outskirts and held the Alpine passes.

The response to this second crisis shaped Europe more than Charlemagne did. Central authority, which could not defend anyone, dissolved toward whoever could: the local lord with a fortified hall and mounted warriors. Historians once called the result **feudalism**, a system in which land was held from a lord in exchange for military service, peasants were bound to the land as serfs, and public power was private property; recent scholarship prefers to describe the variety of local arrangements and doubts there was a system at all, but the shift from a public order to a patchwork of lordships is not in doubt.[^10] Castles, at first earth and timber, appeared across the landscape. The mounted armored warrior, the **knight**, became the military and then the social elite. And the Church, whose monasteries had been the raiders' first targets, became inseparable from the lords who protected it and from whom its bishops and abbots were drawn.

:::story The making of England
In 878 Alfred, king of Wessex, the last unconquered Anglo-Saxon kingdom, was hiding in the marshes of Somerset from a Danish army that had overrun everything else. He came out, beat the Danes at Edington, converted their king, and spent the rest of his reign building a system of fortified towns, a fleet, a rotating army, a law code, and a program of translating Latin books into English, some of them by his own hand, because, he wrote, learning had so decayed that few south of the Humber could understand their service books.[^11] His grandson Athelstan conquered the Danish north in 927 and was the first king of all England; by 1000 England had shires, sheriffs, a national coinage remade every few years, a land tax, and the most effective royal government in Europe, which the Normans would inherit in 1066 and which is why England's medieval history differs from the continent's. Alfred is the only native English king called "the Great." He earned it in a swamp.
:::

## The world of 1000

By 1000 the shape of Europe was set. France under the new Capetian kings (987), weak but durable. Germany under the Ottonian emperors, who had revived Charlemagne's title in 962 and ruled Italy as well as Germany. England unified. Spain divided between Muslim al-Andalus and the small Christian kingdoms of the north. Hungary newly a Christian kingdom (1000), Poland and Bohemia newly Christian principalities; Scandinavia converting; Kievan Rus Orthodox since 988, tied to Constantinople rather than Rome. The Church everywhere, with a papacy that was, at that moment, a plaything of Roman aristocratic families and about to be reformed. The population, which had fallen since 200, was growing again; forests were being cleared, the climate was warming, and the heavy plow, the horse collar, and the three-field rotation were raising yields.[^12] Europe was a poor, violent, rural, and very religious frontier society on the edge of the Islamic world, and it was about to grow very fast.

:::key
The five centuries after Rome were not empty. They produced the Frankish kingdom from which France and Germany descend, the English state, the Christianization of the whole continent, the monasteries that saved classical literature, the papacy as a European institution, and the lordship-and-castle order that would structure society until the French Revolution. They were also, for almost everyone, a time of poverty, insecurity, and short lives. The label "Dark Ages" is a Renaissance sneer about Latin style; the label "Early Middle Ages" is a modern historian's neutrality. Both describe a period in which the West was rebuilt from the ground, mostly by people who could not write, in a form no Roman would have recognized.
:::

## Summary

- Europe around 600 was rural, illiterate, poor, and held together by the Church, kings who converted, and the memory of Rome.
- Islam's arrival split the Mediterranean, made Latin Christendom a frontier society, and shifted Europe's center north; Córdoba was the West's greatest city.
- Monks under Benedict's Rule, and the Irish and Anglo-Saxon churches, preserved nearly all of classical Latin literature and were Europe's schools and landowners.
- Charlemagne united most of Latin Christendom, was crowned emperor in 800, and sponsored a rescue of learning; his empire split in 843.
- Viking, Magyar, and Muslim raids dissolved central power toward local lords, castles, and knights; England alone built a strong monarchy in response; by 1000 the map of Europe's kingdoms was recognizable.
