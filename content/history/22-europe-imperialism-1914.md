## Recap

Chapter 21 ended in 1871 with a Europe industrial, nationalist, and confident. This chapter follows that confidence outward, into the conquest of Africa and Asia, and inward, into the arms race and alliance system that made a continental war possible. It ends on the day the war began, and it tries to answer the question historians have asked ever since: how did the most prosperous, educated, and peaceful century Europe had known end in the worst war it had fought?

## The long peace and its prosperity

Between 1871 and 1914 no great power fought another in Europe. The **second industrial revolution**, steel, chemicals, electricity, the internal combustion engine, made Germany and the United States the world's industrial leaders and gave Europe a standard of living no society had known. Real wages in Britain nearly doubled; life expectancy passed fifty; universal primary education arrived nearly everywhere; the vote extended to most men (universal male suffrage in Germany from 1871 and France from 1848, and about two thirds of men in Britain by 1884) and, in Finland from 1906 and Norway from 1913, to women.[^1] The state took on new duties: Bismarck's Germany invented social insurance in the 1880s, against sickness, accident, and old age, to steal the socialists' thunder; Britain followed in 1908–1911. Cities got electric light, trams, department stores, telephones, cinemas, and mass newspapers. A middle-class European of 1900 could travel anywhere on the continent without a passport, and Norman Angell's *The Great Illusion* (1910) argued that war between industrial nations had become economically impossible. It sold well over a million copies.

The peace rested on Bismarck. Having made Germany, he spent nineteen years keeping it safe, by convincing everyone that it was satisfied, by allying with Austria (1879) and Italy (1882), by keeping Russia friendly, and by isolating France. In 1890 the young Kaiser Wilhelm II dismissed him, let the Russian tie lapse, and began building a battle fleet to challenge Britain. France and Russia allied in 1894; Britain, alarmed by the German navy, settled its differences with France (1904) and Russia (1907). By 1907 Europe was two armed camps, and the flexibility Bismarck had managed was gone.[^2]

## The new imperialism

In 1870 Europeans ruled about 10 percent of Africa, mostly coastal. In 1914 they ruled all of it except Ethiopia and Liberia. The **Scramble for Africa** was set off by Leopold II of Belgium's private seizure of the Congo basin and the French push inland from Senegal, formalized at the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, where fourteen European states agreed the rules for claiming African territory without an African present, and completed in twenty years of treaties, expeditions, and massacres.[^3] Britain took Egypt (1882), the Sudan, Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Kenya, Uganda, Rhodesia, and South Africa; France took most of the west and north; Germany, Italy, Portugal, and Belgium the rest. In Asia, Britain had India (ruled directly after the rebellion of 1857), Burma, Malaya; France took Indochina; the Dutch held Indonesia; Russia advanced across Central Asia to the Afghan border and across Siberia to the Pacific; and everyone took pieces of China (chapter 33). By 1914 Europeans and their settler descendants controlled about 84 percent of the world's land.

Why? The explanations, each with its historians: **economic** (Hobson and Lenin: surplus capital seeking investment, industry seeking raw materials and markets; the evidence is that most colonies lost money and most investment went to the Americas); **strategic** (Robinson and Gallagher: Britain took Egypt to guard the route to India and then Africa to guard Egypt, and each seizure required the next); **political** (colonies as prestige and as a distraction from class conflict at home, "social imperialism"); **technological** (quinine, steamboats, the telegraph, and above all the machine gun made conquest cheap: at Omdurman in 1898, British Maxim guns killed 10,000 Sudanese in a morning for 48 dead on their side); and **ideological**, the conviction, backed by a misreading of Darwin, that Europeans were racially superior and had a duty, "the white man's burden," to civilize.[^4] All of them operated, in different mixtures in different places, and the peripheral dynamic of men on the spot exceeding their instructions did the rest.

What it did to the colonized is the subject of their histories, and future sections of this guide. What it did in the worst cases can be stated: in Leopold's Congo, forced rubber collection enforced by mutilation and murder killed, by the most careful estimates, several million people between 1885 and 1908, half the population; in German South-West Africa, a war of annihilation against the Herero and Nama in 1904–1908, waged under an extermination order, killed most of the Herero and half the Nama and is usually counted as the twentieth century's first genocide; in India, famines under British rule killed, on Mike Davis's contested estimate, tens of millions while grain was exported.[^5] Most of the empire was not that, but that was the empire too, and the critics who said so at the time, Morel on the Congo, Hobson on the whole system, were right and were ignored.

## Cracks

Under the confidence there were cracks. **Nationalism**, which had made Italy and Germany, was dismembering Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire from within: Czechs, Poles, South Slavs, Romanians, Greeks, Bulgarians, and Serbs each wanted a state, and the Balkans, where the two empires met and Russia backed the Slavs, produced two wars in 1912–1913 that the great powers barely contained. **Socialism** had become a mass movement: the German Social Democrats were the largest party in the Reichstag by 1912, and the Second International pledged the workers of all countries to refuse war against each other. **Anti-Semitism** was becoming political: Dreyfus, a Jewish French officer, was framed for treason in 1894 and split France for a decade; in Vienna, the mayor was elected on an anti-Semitic platform; in Russia, pogroms killed thousands and drove two million Jews to emigrate, mostly to America.[^6] Women were demanding the vote and British suffragettes were breaking windows. Ireland was on the edge of civil war over Home Rule. Culture had turned: Nietzsche declared God dead, Freud found the unconscious, Einstein dissolved absolute time, Picasso broke the picture plane, and Stravinsky's *Rite of Spring* caused a riot in Paris in 1913. The generation of 1914 did not think it lived in a golden age. It thought it lived in a decadent one, and some of it wanted a war to cleanse it.

## The July Crisis

On 28 June 1914, in Sarajevo, a nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb named Gavrilo Princip, armed by Serbian military intelligence, shot the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, and his wife. Austria, wanting to crush Serbia before Serb nationalism dissolved the empire, secured Germany's backing (the "blank cheque" of 5 July), and on 23 July issued Serbia an ultimatum designed to be refused. Serbia accepted nearly all of it. Austria declared war on 28 July. Russia, Serbia's patron, mobilized; Germany, whose only war plan required beating France before Russia could mobilize fully, declared war on Russia on 1 August and France on 3 August, and on 4 August invaded neutral Belgium to get at France, which brought in Britain. In five weeks a Balkan assassination had become a European war, and Europe's empires made it a world war on the first day.[^7]

:::history How we got here
Who was to blame has been argued since 1914 and the argument has passed through four phases. At Versailles the victors wrote German guilt into the treaty. In the 1920s and 1930s "revisionists" spread the blame: the alliance system, the arms race, the mobilization timetables, the "slide" into war that nobody wanted, Lloyd George's "the nations slithered over the brink." In 1961 Fritz Fischer, a German historian working in German archives, argued that Germany's leaders had wanted a war of expansion and used the crisis to get it, and German historians fought over it for a decade. Since the 1990s the pendulum has swung back toward shared responsibility and contingency: Christopher Clark's *The Sleepwalkers* (2012) distributed the blame to Serbia, Austria, Russia, and Germany, and stressed the decision-makers' failures of imagination; others insist that Germany and Austria, who chose war, bear most of it. The current view: the war was not inevitable, the alliance system did not cause it but shaped it, and a small number of men in Vienna and Berlin made choices in July 1914 that they could have made differently and that other men in St Petersburg, Paris, and London then compounded. It is the clearest case in this guide of history that turns on a few individuals' decisions, and the clearest warning about what confidence, honor, and timetables can do.[^8]
:::

:::key
The forty years before 1914 were the richest, most peaceful, most progressive Europe had known, and they ended in the worst war it had fought, because the things that made the prosperity, nationalism, industrial power, and great-power competition, also made the alliances, the armies, and the sense that war was a tool. Europe ruled the world and believed it deserved to. The war would take that belief away, and the rest of the section is about what happened when it did.
:::

## Summary

- From 1871 to 1914 Europe enjoyed unprecedented peace and prosperity, extended the vote, invented social insurance, and believed war between industrial nations impossible.
- Bismarck's alliance system kept the peace; after his dismissal in 1890 Europe hardened into two armed camps.
- The new imperialism partitioned Africa in twenty years and brought 84 percent of the world's land under European control, for strategic, economic, political, technological, and racial reasons in varying mixtures; its worst cases were genocidal.
- Beneath the confidence, nationalism was dissolving the multinational empires, socialism was a mass movement, anti-Semitism was becoming political, and culture had turned against the age.
- The Sarajevo assassination became a world war in five weeks through choices in Vienna and Berlin, compounded elsewhere; blame has been argued for a century and now rests on contingency and a few men.
