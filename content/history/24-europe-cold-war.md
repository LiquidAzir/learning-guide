## Recap

Chapter 23 ended in May 1945 with Europe wrecked and occupied. This chapter follows the forty-six years in which it was divided: the Cold War as Europe experienced it, the West's recovery and integration, the East's stagnation and revolts, the end of the colonial empires, and the collapse of the Soviet system in 1989–1991. It is recent enough that most of its participants are documented to the minute and some are alive; its arguments are correspondingly fresh.

## Year zero

In 1945 some 40 million Europeans were displaced: refugees, forced laborers, camp survivors, and the 12 million Germans expelled from Eastern Europe in the largest forced migration in European history. Cities were rubble; Germany's industrial production was a third of 1938's; food rations in the British zone fell to 1,000 calories. The Nuremberg trials (1945–1946) established, for the first time, that leaders could be held criminally responsible for aggressive war and crimes against humanity, and hanged ten of them.[^1] The United Nations was founded; so, by 1948, was the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Europe's future was decided at Yalta by Roosevelt, Stalin, and Churchill, at Potsdam by Truman, Stalin, and Churchill and then Attlee, and then by facts on the ground: the Red Army occupied everything east of the Elbe and stayed.

## The division

The wartime alliance broke within two years. Stalin installed communist governments across Eastern Europe, by elections he rigged and coups he backed; Churchill spoke of an "iron curtain" in 1946; the United States, with the Truman Doctrine (1947) and the **Marshall Plan** (1948–1952, some $13 billion over four years, equal to about 5 percent of one year's American GDP, in aid to rebuild Western Europe), committed itself to containing Soviet power. In 1948 the Soviets blockaded West Berlin and the Allies flew supplies in for eleven months. In 1949 the West formed **NATO**, a military alliance binding the United States to Europe's defense, and Germany was divided into a Federal Republic in the west and a Democratic Republic in the east.[^2] The Soviet Union tested an atomic bomb the same year. The **Cold War**, a conflict between two nuclear superpowers that could not fight each other directly and so fought everywhere else, had its front line down the middle of Europe, and the line would not move for forty years.

Whose fault? The orthodox American account blamed Soviet expansionism; the revisionists of the 1960s blamed American economic imperialism and atomic diplomacy; the post-revisionists split the difference; and the opening of Soviet archives after 1991 showed a Stalin who was cautious, opportunistic, ideologically convinced that conflict with capitalism was inevitable, and determined to hold what the Red Army had taken. The current view credits both sides' security fears and Stalin's regime with most of the responsibility for the division itself.[^3]

## The West: the golden age

What happened in Western Europe between 1950 and 1973 had no precedent. Economies grew at 4 to 5 percent a year for a quarter of a century; West Germany's "economic miracle" took it from rubble to Europe's largest economy by 1960; Italy and France industrialized their south and countryside; real incomes roughly tripled. The **welfare state**, built on wartime solidarity and the memory of the 1930s, gave every citizen health care, pensions, unemployment insurance, and education; Britain's National Health Service (1948) was the model. Full employment lasted a generation. Peasants became workers, workers bought cars and televisions, and their children went to universities that quadrupled in size.[^4] Historians call it the golden age, and it was: the fastest rise in living standards for the largest number of people in European history, achieved by democracies with mixed economies and strong unions.

It was also the age of **integration**. Six countries, France, West Germany, Italy, and the Benelux three, pooled their coal and steel in 1951 and formed the **European Economic Community** in 1957, a common market with shared institutions, on the theory that economies too intertwined to separate could not go to war. Britain joined in 1973. The Community became the European Union in 1993 and is the only case in history of sovereign states voluntarily merging their economies and much of their law without conquest.[^5]

And it was the age of **decolonization**. Between 1947 (India) and 1975 (Portugal's African colonies), the European empires that had ruled 84 percent of the world in 1914 were gone, in some cases by negotiation and in others by wars the Europeans lost: France in Indochina (1954) and Algeria (1962), which brought down the Fourth Republic; the Netherlands in Indonesia; Britain, mostly, by orderly retreat, except in Kenya, Malaya, and Cyprus.[^6] The empires' end reduced Europe to a peninsula of middling powers dependent on America for defense, and its peoples adjusted with less trouble than anyone expected, partly because they were getting rich at home.

## The East

Eastern Europe under Soviet rule got the opposite: one-party states, planned economies, secret police, censorship, and a Red Army to guarantee it all. Living standards rose from a low base and then stalled; by 1980 East Germans, the bloc's richest, had a third the income of West Germans, and everyone could see it on West German television. The system's legitimacy rested on the memory of 1945 and on force, and every time the force wavered the peoples moved. East Berlin rose in 1953 and was crushed by tanks. Hungary rose in 1956, declared neutrality, and was invaded; 2,500 died and 200,000 fled. Czechoslovakia tried "socialism with a human face" in 1968 and was invaded by half a million Warsaw Pact troops. Poland's **Solidarity** union, born in the Gdańsk shipyard in 1980, had ten million members within a year and was suppressed by martial law in 1981.[^7] The Berlin Wall, built in 1961 to stop East Germans leaving (2.7 million had), was the division made concrete, and about 140 people died trying to cross it.

## 1989

Mikhail Gorbachev became Soviet leader in 1985 with an economy that could not feed its people or match American arms spending, and tried to reform it: *glasnost* (openness) and *perestroika* (restructuring). He also let it be known that the Soviet Union would no longer send tanks to save its allies. That was enough. In 1989 Poland held semi-free elections in June and Solidarity won all but one of the seats it could contest; Hungary opened its border with Austria in September and East Germans poured through; demonstrations in Leipzig grew from thousands to hundreds of thousands; on 9 November an East German official misread a new travel rule at a press conference, crowds gathered at the Wall, the guards had no orders, and they opened the gates. Czechoslovakia's communists resigned within weeks after the **Velvet Revolution**; Bulgaria's fell; Romania's Ceaușescu was shot on Christmas Day. In one autumn, without a war, the Soviet empire in Europe ended.[^8] Germany reunified in October 1990. In August 1991 hardliners tried a coup in Moscow and failed; on 25 December 1991 the Soviet Union dissolved itself into fifteen states.

:::history How we got here
Why did it end, and why so peacefully? Four explanations compete and combine. The **triumphalist** account credits Reagan's arms buildup and pressure, which the Soviet economy could not match. The **economic** account stresses the planned economy's inability to deliver consumer goods or keep up with the information age, and the oil price collapse of 1986 that emptied Soviet coffers. The **Gorbachev** account credits one man's decision not to use force, which nothing compelled him to make; his predecessors had used it every time. The **society** account credits the peoples of Eastern Europe, whose dissidents (Havel, Michnik, Sakharov) had kept an alternative alive and whose crowds in 1989 supplied the pressure. Most historians now hold that the system was rotting, that Gorbachev's refusal to shoot was decisive and personal, and that the crowds did the rest; the Western contribution was to exist and be visibly richer. Nobody predicted it. The failure of every intelligence agency and nearly every scholar to foresee 1989 is the standing warning to anyone who claims to know where history is going.[^9]
:::

:::story A press conference
On the evening of 9 November 1989 Günter Schabowski, an East German politburo member, read out a new regulation at a televised press conference. It allowed citizens to apply for travel permits, and it was meant to take effect the next day, in an orderly way, through the usual offices. Asked when it came into force, he shuffled his papers and said "as far as I know, immediately, without delay." West German television reported that the Wall was open. Within an hour thousands of East Berliners were at the checkpoints demanding to cross; the border guards, who had received no orders and whose commander could reach no one who would give any, faced a crowd that would not leave. At 11:30 p.m. the officer at Bornholmer Strasse gave up and raised the barrier. By midnight every crossing in Berlin was open and people were dancing on the Wall.[^10] Twenty-eight years of the Cold War's most concrete fact ended because a functionary misread his brief and a lieutenant colonel decided not to shoot. History is often like that, and this is the case where we can watch it on film.
:::

:::key
Divided Europe was two experiments run side by side for forty years on the same people, and the results were unambiguous: the democratic, mixed-economy, integrated West produced the greatest rise in prosperity and freedom in the continent's history, and the one-party planned East produced stagnation, walls, and, whenever the tanks were absent, revolt. The Cold War ended because the East's rulers stopped believing in their own system enough to kill for it, and its peoples never had. The lesson Western Europe drew, that integration and welfare democracy had abolished war and want, would be tested in the next chapter.
:::

## Summary

- Europe in 1945 was wrecked, displaced, and occupied; within four years it was divided between an American-backed West and a Soviet-controlled East, with the line down the middle of Germany.
- Western Europe's golden age (1950–1973) tripled incomes, built the welfare state, integrated six economies into the EEC, and gave up its empires.
- Eastern Europe stagnated under one-party rule and rose whenever it could: 1953, 1956, 1968, 1980; each rising was crushed until 1989.
- Gorbachev's refusal to use force let the peoples of Eastern Europe end Soviet rule in one autumn; the Wall opened by accident on 9 November 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991.
- Why it ended, and why nobody predicted it, remains the period's central historiographical question.
