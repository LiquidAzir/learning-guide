## How did ocean voyages become conquest and a transformed world?

Chapters 16 and 17 have mentioned 1492 and American silver as if they were background. They were not; they were the beginning of Europe's relation to the rest of the world, and this chapter tells that story from the first Portuguese voyages down the African coast to the point, around 1700, when Europeans controlled the world's oceans and were arguing about what that meant. It also asks the question every historian of the period asks: why did a poor, quarrelsome peninsula on the edge of Eurasia, and not the far richer Chinese or Islamic worlds, do this?

## Portugal first

In 1415 Portugal, a small kingdom with a long coast and nothing to do with Spain blocking it, took Ceuta in Morocco. Over the next eighty years, under the patronage of Prince Henry ("the Navigator," who never navigated), Portuguese captains worked their way down the west coast of Africa in **caravels**, small ships with lateen sails that could beat against the wind, learning to use the Atlantic wind systems, and bringing back gold, pepper, and, from 1441, slaves.[^1] Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488; Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498 and came home with a cargo worth sixty times the voyage's cost. Within fifteen years Portugal had seized Goa, Malacca, and Hormuz, blown Arab and Indian fleets out of the water with ship-mounted cannon nobody in the Indian Ocean had faced before, and taken over the spice trade that had reached Europe through Venice and Egypt for a thousand years. Portuguese ships reached China in 1513 and Japan in 1543 (chapter 31). It was an empire of forts and ships, not of land, run by a nation of barely a million people.

## Columbus, and what he found

Christopher Columbus, a Genoese who had sailed for Portugal, believed the Earth was smaller than it is (educated opinion knew better; nobody thought it flat) and that Japan lay 2,400 miles west of the Canaries. Portugal turned him down; Spain, in the year it took Granada, said yes. On 12 October 1492, after thirty-three days, he landed in the Bahamas and thought he was near Japan. He died in 1506 still insisting he had reached Asia.[^2]

What he had reached was a hemisphere of perhaps 50 to 60 million people (estimates range from 8 to 100 million), with two empires larger than any in Europe. The **Aztec** empire in Mexico, whose capital Tenochtitlan, a city on a lake with 200,000 people, causeways, aqueducts, and floating gardens, was larger than any city in Spain. The **Inca** empire in the Andes, 2,500 miles long, with 10 million subjects, a road system to rival Rome's, and a state that stored grain against famine and moved whole populations (chapter 11).[^3] In 1519 Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico with about 500 men; in 1521 Tenochtitlan fell. In 1532 Francisco Pizarro with 168 men captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa by treachery at Cajamarca and, by 1533, had his empire.

How? The conquistadors' own accounts credit courage and God. The real answers: steel swords and armor against obsidian and cotton; horses, which the Americas had not seen; tens of thousands of native allies who hated the Aztecs, without whom Cortés would have died on the causeways; civil war in the Inca empire; and, above all, **disease**. Smallpox reached Mexico in 1520 and killed perhaps half of Tenochtitlan before Cortés returned to it; it reached Peru before Pizarro did and killed the emperor whose sons' war Pizarro exploited.[^4] The Americas had none of the Old World's crowd diseases, because they had none of its domestic animals (chapter 2), and their peoples had no immunity. Over the next century smallpox, measles, typhus, and influenza, with war, enslavement, and famine, killed perhaps 90 percent of the indigenous population of the Americas: the largest demographic catastrophe in human history, so complete that reforested land may have measurably lowered global carbon dioxide.[^5]

## The Columbian exchange

The joining of the hemispheres moved everything. From the Americas to the Old World: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, chili peppers, cacao, tobacco, vanilla, beans, squash, turkeys, and, in silver, a mountain of it. Potatoes and maize fed the population growth of Europe, China, and Africa for the next three centuries; Ireland's population tripled on potatoes and then starved when they failed. From the Old World to the Americas: wheat, sugar, coffee, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, the diseases, and, in chains, some 12.5 million Africans.[^6]

Silver was the engine. The mines of Potosí in Bolivia (from 1545) and Zacatecas in Mexico produced perhaps 150,000 tons of silver over three centuries, three quarters of the world's supply. It paid for Spain's wars, flowed through Europe causing the century-long **price revolution**, and flowed on to China, which demanded silver for taxes and sold silk and porcelain for it (chapter 31); a Spanish galleon crossed the Pacific from Acapulco to Manila every year from 1565 to 1815.[^7] The first global economy ran on American silver dug by Andean forced labor and spent in China.

## Slavery

Sugar needed labor in quantity, in a climate that killed Europeans, on islands whose native populations were dead. From about 1520 the Portuguese and then everyone else bought captives on the West African coast from African rulers and merchants who had long sold slaves north across the Sahara, and shipped them to Brazil and the Caribbean. The **Atlantic slave trade** carried about 12.5 million people between 1500 and 1867, of whom 10.7 million survived the crossing; the Middle Passage killed the rest, and plantation life killed the survivors fast enough that the islands needed constant replacement.[^8] Brazil took over 40 percent, the British, French, and Spanish Caribbean and mainland colonies most of the rest, and what became the United States about 4 percent. It was the largest forced migration in history, and it was, for two centuries, an ordinary business, financed in Amsterdam and London, insured, and taxed, and defended by everyone from popes to Locke. Its abolition began in the 1780s with Quaker and evangelical campaigns in Britain: Britain banned the trade in 1807 and slavery in its colonies in 1833, France for good in 1848, the United States after its civil war in 1865, and Brazil, last, in 1888.

## The Dutch and the English

Spain and Portugal had the sixteenth century; the seventeenth was Dutch and then English. The **Dutch Republic**, at war with Spain from 1568 (chapter 17), built the most efficient commercial economy in the world: the Amsterdam Exchange Bank (1609), the first stock exchange, and the **Dutch East India Company** (VOC, 1602), a joint-stock company with a state charter, its own army and fleet, and the right to make war and treaties, which seized the spice islands from Portugal, founded Batavia (Jakarta) and Cape Town, and was the only European presence Japan tolerated.[^9] The English followed with their own East India Company (1600), colonies in Virginia (1607) and Massachusetts (1620), Caribbean sugar islands, and, after three wars with the Dutch, the world's largest navy. France took Canada and the Mississippi. By 1700 the Atlantic was a European lake, the Indian Ocean was patrolled by European companies, and the Americas, except for the Andes and the interior, spoke Spanish, Portuguese, English, or French.

:::history How we got here
Why Europe? The question was asked as soon as the answer seemed obvious, and the obvious answers were racial or religious: Europeans were braver, or Christian, or white. Those are gone. The serious candidates since 1950: geography (Europe's fragmented coastline made many competing states, none able to stop exploration, whereas one Chinese emperor could and in 1433 did end Zheng He's voyages, which had reached East Africa in ships four times the size of Columbus's); disease (Europeans carried germs the Americas lacked, so conquest was cheap); technology (the compass and gunpowder were Chinese, but Europeans put cannon on ships and built ships for the Atlantic); institutions (the joint-stock company and state-chartered violence spread risk); poverty and need (Europe wanted Asian goods and had nothing to sell but silver, so it had to go and get the silver); and plain luck (the Atlantic wind system happens to loop from Europe to the Caribbean and back).[^10] The consensus, if there is one, is that Europe's fragmentation drove competition, its position on the Atlantic made the Americas reachable, and the Americas' epidemiological isolation made them conquerable; China's unity and self-sufficiency removed the incentive. None of it required Europeans to be superior, and the fifteenth-century Chinese would have found the suggestion absurd.
:::

## What it did to Europe

The Atlantic economy shifted Europe's center of gravity from the Mediterranean to the north and west, from Venice and Genoa to Antwerp, Amsterdam, and London. It made Spain briefly the greatest power on Earth and then ruined it, because silver paid for wars instead of industries. It enriched a merchant class whose capital and institutions would later finance industry (chapter 21). It brought Europeans face to face with peoples their books had not mentioned and provoked the first arguments about human rights: in 1550–1551 the Spanish crown convened a debate at Valladolid on whether the conquest of the Indians was just, and the Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas, who had seen it, argued it was not; no verdict was given and the conquests went on, but the argument was had, and the laws Spain passed to protect the Indians, though ignored, exist.[^11] And it gave Europe an image of itself as the center of a world it had mapped, which it would keep for four hundred years.

:::key
The European expansion was made possible by things Europeans had not invented (the compass, gunpowder, the lateen sail) and could not control (the Atlantic winds, Old World germs), and it was driven by poverty rather than strength: Europe went to sea because it wanted Asia's goods and had nothing Asia wanted but silver. Its results were the destruction of the American civilizations, the enslavement of twelve million Africans, the first global economy, and the shift of Europe's center to the Atlantic. Whether it should be told as achievement or atrocity is a question the participants asked at Valladolid in 1550, and the answer, then as now, is that it was both, done by the same people, for reasons they thought sufficient.
:::

## Summary

- Portugal opened the Atlantic and Indian Ocean routes with caravels and ship-mounted cannon, and took the spice trade from Venice and the Arabs by 1515.
- European conquest depended on Indigenous allies as well as weapons and horses. Introduced diseases, warfare, enslavement, and famine devastated Indigenous populations; estimates vary by place and period, and losses in some regions approached 90 percent.
- The Columbian exchange moved crops, animals, and diseases across the world; American silver, dug by forced labor, made the first global economy and flowed to China.
- The Atlantic slave trade carried 12.5 million Africans to the plantations of Brazil and the Caribbean as ordinary, financed, insured business.
- The Dutch and English replaced Spain and Portugal with chartered companies, stock exchanges, and navies; historians explain "why Europe" by fragmentation, geography, germs, and need rather than superiority.
