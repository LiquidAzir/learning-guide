## Why did the end of the Cold War leave so many conflicts unresolved?

Chapter 24 ended with the Soviet Union dissolving on Christmas Day 1991 and Western Europe believing that integration and democracy had won. This chapter takes the story to the present. It is the hardest chapter in the section to write, because the events are not over and the historian has no hindsight; it therefore stays close to what is documented, marks what is contested, and stops at the point where history becomes news.

## The 1990s: a new Europe

The decade after 1989 remade the map. Germany reunified and moved its capital to Berlin. The **European Union** was created by the Maastricht Treaty (1992), which added a common foreign policy, common citizenship, and a commitment to a single currency to the common market; the **euro** was launched in 1999 and its notes and coins in 2002, and by 2026 twenty-one states used it, Croatia joining in 2023 and Bulgaria in 2026. Eastern Europe's new democracies applied to join both the EU and NATO, and in 2004 ten states, eight of them formerly communist, entered the EU at once; Romania and Bulgaria followed in 2007, Croatia in 2013.[^1] The former Soviet republics of the Baltic, occupied since 1940, were in NATO by 2004. Within fifteen years of the Wall's fall, the division of Europe had been reversed, and the states that had been behind the Iron Curtain were, by the standards of their own history, freer and richer than they had ever been.

Russia did not follow the same path. Yeltsin's shock-therapy privatization of the 1990s produced a collapse in output of about 40 percent, hyperinflation, an oligarchy that acquired state assets for a fraction of their value, a fall in male life expectancy to 58, and a default in 1998. Vladimir Putin, appointed prime minister in 1999 and elected president in 2000, restored order, prosperity (on oil at $100 a barrel), and central control, and dismantled, step by step, the independent media, the opposition, and the electoral competition that the 1990s had created.[^2] Whether Russia's turn from democracy was the result of Western policy (NATO's eastward expansion, the humiliation of the 1990s), of Russia's own history, or of Putin's choices is the most consequential of the period's arguments and is unsettled.

## Yugoslavia

The exception to the peaceful 1990s was the Balkans. Yugoslavia, a federation of six republics held together since 1945 by Tito and the Communist party, came apart when both were gone and its politicians, above all Serbia's Slobodan Milošević, discovered nationalism as a route to power. Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in 1991; the Yugoslav army, effectively Serbian, fought a short war in Slovenia and a long one in Croatia. Bosnia, with a Muslim plurality and large Serb and Croat minorities, declared independence in 1992 and was torn apart in a three-year war in which Serb forces besieged Sarajevo for 1,425 days, the longest siege of a capital in modern history, and carried out **ethnic cleansing**, the term the war made current, of Muslim populations; at Srebrenica in July 1995 they murdered some 8,000 Muslim men and boys in a UN "safe area" while Dutch peacekeepers stood by.[^3] NATO bombing and a Croat-Bosnian offensive ended the war; the Dayton Agreement (1995) froze Bosnia as a state of two entities that barely functions. In 1999 NATO bombed Serbia for 78 days to stop the expulsion of Kosovo's Albanians; Kosovo declared independence in 2008. Some 130,000 people died in the Yugoslav wars, the first in Europe since 1945, and the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, sitting at The Hague, convicted 90 people, Milošević dying in his cell before verdict. Europe, which had believed its institutions had abolished war, had watched a genocide on its television screens for three years and done little, and the memory shaped the next generation's interventions and inhibitions alike.

## The crises

The euro's first decade was a success. Its second was not. The **global financial crisis** of 2008 ([the finance chapter of the economics guide](#/economics/finance)) exposed the flaw that economists had pointed out in 1992: a single currency without a single treasury, so that a member state in trouble could neither devalue nor be bailed out. Greece, whose debt turned out to be far larger than reported, was rescued three times from 2010 on terms that cut its economy by a quarter and its pensions by half; Ireland, Portugal, Spain, and Cyprus needed help; Italy nearly did; and the crisis was contained only when the European Central Bank's president promised in 2012 to do "whatever it takes."[^4] The **eurozone crisis** left southern Europe with a lost decade, youth unemployment above 50 percent in Greece and Spain, and a resentment of Brussels and Berlin that fed the politics that followed.

In 2015 more than a million refugees and migrants, mostly from Syria's civil war, arrived in Europe in one year; Germany took the largest share. The **migration crisis** split the EU between states that admitted and states that refused, and became the central issue of a new politics: nationalist, populist, hostile to immigration and to the EU, and successful. Such parties entered government in Hungary (Orbán, from 2010, who dismantled his country's liberal institutions while remaining in the EU), Poland (2015–2023), Italy (2018 and 2022), Czechia (2017 and 2025), Austria, the Netherlands, and elsewhere, and reached the second round of France's presidential election in 2017 and 2022.[^5]

In June 2016 the United Kingdom voted, 52 to 48, to leave the EU, and left in January 2020. **Brexit** was the first reversal of integration in the Union's history; its causes, immigration, sovereignty, the eurozone's troubles, the divide between metropolitan and provincial England, are as argued as its consequences, which by 2026 were measurable in trade and investment and disputed in everything else.[^6]

## Ukraine

Ukraine had voted for independence in 1991 and spent two decades between Russia and the West. In 2013–2014 its president abandoned an EU association agreement under Russian pressure; protests in Kyiv's Maidan square turned into a revolution; he fled; and Russia seized Crimea and armed a separatist war in the eastern Donbas that killed 14,000 people over eight years. On 24 February 2022 Russia invaded Ukraine on three fronts with some 190,000 troops, expecting to take Kyiv in days. It did not. Ukraine, armed increasingly by the West, held the capital, retook Kharkiv and Kherson in 2022, and settled into a war of attrition along a 1,000-kilometer front that by 2026 had killed or wounded, by Western estimates, well over a million soldiers on both sides and displaced some 10 million Ukrainians.[^7] Russia struck Ukrainian cities and power stations; Ukraine struck Russian refineries and, with drones, ships and bombers. American-brokered talks in 2025 and 2026 produced short truces that collapsed within days and left the sides divided over territory and security guarantees; about a fifth of Ukraine remained under Russian occupation. The largest war in Europe since 1945 was, as this guide was written, unresolved, and this chapter does not pretend to know its outcome.

Its effects on Europe were already historical. Finland and Sweden, neutral for seventy-five years, joined NATO in 2023 and 2024. Germany announced the end of its post-1945 military restraint and Europe's largest rearmament since the Cold War; the EU committed to defense spending on a scale it had never contemplated; Europe cut itself off from Russian gas, on which Germany had built its industry. The postwar settlement, in which Europe was a civilian power under American protection, was, by 2026, being renegotiated, with the United States drawing down its forces in Europe even as it reaffirmed the alliance's mutual-defense clause at the 2026 Ankara summit.[^8]

## What the last thirty-five years look like from here

Some things are clear. The reunification of Europe after 1989 was a success on a scale that its planners hoped for and did not expect: the ten states that joined the EU in 2004 doubled their real incomes in twenty years, and Poland, no richer than Ukraine in 1990, was by 2026 a major European economy. Democracy, the rule of law, and open borders became normal across a continent where, in 1989, half the population had never voted in a free election. Europe fought no wars between its states after 1999 except the ones Russia started, in Georgia in 2008 and in Ukraine from 2014, both outside the Union.

Other things are contested. Whether the euro was a mistake or a success that survived its test. Whether NATO's expansion provoked Russia or protected the states that joined (every one of which asked to). Whether the EU's response to the crises showed strength (it survived all of them) or weakness (each took years). Whether the populist wave is a passing reaction to the crises or a permanent realignment. Whether Europe can defend itself. Historians in 2050 will have answers; this chapter has the questions.

:::history How we got here
The story Europe told itself in 1991 was the "end of history": liberal democracy and market economics had defeated their last rival, and the rest was administration. Francis Fukuyama's essay of that title (1989) was subtler than its slogan, and he warned that boredom and the desire for struggle might undo the settlement, but the slogan is what stuck.[^9] By 2016 the story had been replaced by its opposite, a narrative of democratic recession, populist revolt, and the return of great-power war. Neither story is history yet. The historian's caution is that the events of a single generation almost never look, fifty years later, the way they looked to the people living them: the 1920s did not know they were the interwar years, and 1989 did not know what it was the beginning of. This chapter has been written knowing that.
:::

:::key
Europe after 1989 reunited, enlarged, and enriched itself, and believed it had abolished the two things that had defined its history, war and want. The Yugoslav wars showed that war had not been abolished, only exported to the margins; the eurozone crisis showed that want had not; the migration crisis and Brexit showed that the Union's own peoples were not agreed on what it was for; and Russia's invasion of Ukraine showed that the security on which all of it rested had been borrowed from America and could be recalled. The postwar settlement of chapter 24 is being remade, and nobody knows into what.
:::

## Summary

- The 1990s reversed the division of Europe: the EU deepened at Maastricht, launched the euro, and with NATO absorbed the former Soviet bloc by 2004–2007; Russia went from chaotic democracy to Putin's centralized state.
- Yugoslavia's collapse brought Europe's first wars since 1945, ethnic cleansing, the Srebrenica genocide, and NATO's first combat, and exposed the limits of Europe's institutions.
- The 2008 crisis exposed the euro's design flaw and cost southern Europe a decade; the 2015 migration crisis and Brexit fueled a populist and nationalist politics that reached government across the continent.
- Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 began the largest European war since 1945 and forced the rearmament of Europe and the end of its dependence on Russian energy; its outcome is unknown.
- The reunification of Europe succeeded beyond expectation; the postwar settlement it rested on is being remade, and the historian does not yet know into what.
