## How did universal rights coexist with exclusion and empire?

The seventeenth century ended with Newton's universe, Louis XIV's monarchy, and England's Parliament. The eighteenth applied Newton's confidence to Louis's kind of state and asked why it should exist. The answer, given first in books and then in the streets of Paris, is the subject of this chapter, and it is the hinge of European history: everything before 1789 is the old regime and everything after is modern politics.

## The Enlightenment

The **Enlightenment** was a movement of writers, mostly French, British, and German, between about 1690 and 1790, who believed that reason, applied to society as Newton had applied it to nature, could reform it. Its founding texts were Locke's argument that government rests on consent and the mind on experience, and Newton's demonstration that the universe obeyed laws. Its method was criticism: of revealed religion (Voltaire's lifelong war on the Church's cruelties, Hume's demolition of miracles), of absolute monarchy (Montesquieu's argument in 1748 that liberty requires the separation of powers), of torture and cruel punishment (Beccaria, 1764), of slavery, of censorship, of every institution that rested on custom rather than utility.[^1] Its great collective work was Diderot's *Encyclopédie* (1751–1772), twenty-eight volumes that set out to describe all knowledge and, between the lines, to undermine every authority that could not justify itself. Its social base was the **public sphere**: coffeehouses, salons run by aristocratic women, Masonic lodges, reading societies, and a book trade that smuggled forbidden works across every border.[^2]

It was not one thing. Voltaire wanted a reformed monarchy and despised the mob; Rousseau, in the *Social Contract* (1762), argued that legitimate authority came only from the general will of the people and that a citizen who dissented was to be "forced to be free," which both democrats and dictators have quoted since. Adam Smith, in 1776, argued that markets left alone produced wealth, and founded economics ([the history chapter of the economics guide](#/economics/history)). Kant, in 1784, defined the whole movement as humanity's emergence from self-imposed immaturity and its motto as "dare to know." The Scottish, French, German, and American Enlightenments differed, and historians now speak of Enlightenments in the plural. What they shared was the conviction that human beings could understand and improve their condition without waiting for God or king, and that the test of any institution was whether it served human happiness.[^3]

Some monarchs agreed. Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Great of Russia, and Joseph II of Austria, the **enlightened despots**, abolished torture, tolerated religions, codified laws, built schools, and freed (in Joseph's case) the serfs, all by decree and none by consent; Frederick hosted Voltaire and Catherine corresponded with him while partitioning Poland. Reform from above was the Enlightenment's first political form. Revolution was its second.

## The American precedent

Britain's thirteen American colonies, taxed after 1763 to pay for the war that had won Canada from France, and unrepresented in the Parliament that taxed them, argued from Locke that this was tyranny, declared independence in 1776 in a document Jefferson wrote from Enlightenment premises, won a war with French help by 1783, and in 1787 wrote a constitution with separated powers, a federal structure, and, in 1791, a bill of rights.[^4] It was the first Enlightenment state, and it proved to Europeans that a large republic was possible. It also bankrupted the French monarchy that had paid for it.

## The French Revolution

France in 1788 was Europe's richest and most populous country and its government could not pay its debts. Half the budget went to interest; the nobles and clergy, who owned most of the land, paid almost no tax; and every attempt at reform had been blocked by the courts the nobles controlled. Louis XVI, well-meaning and weak, called the **Estates-General**, which had not met since 1614, for May 1789. The Third Estate, the commoners, who were 97 percent of the nation and had one vote in three, refused to be outvoted, declared itself the National Assembly, and swore not to disperse until France had a constitution. On 14 July a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille to seize its gunpowder. In August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and issued the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen**: men are born free and equal in rights; sovereignty resides in the nation; law is the expression of the general will; liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression are natural rights.[^5] In a summer, a thousand years of legal inequality were declared void.

The revolution then radicalized in stages that every later revolution has repeated. The Church's land was confiscated and the clergy made state employees, splitting Catholic France. The king tried to flee in 1791 and was caught. War with Austria and Prussia began in 1792, went badly, and the Paris crowd stormed the palace, deposed the king, and massacred 1,400 prisoners. A republic was declared in September 1792; Louis was tried and guillotined in January 1793. The **Jacobins**, led by Robespierre, took power, and to fight invasion abroad and civil war in the Vendée at home ran the **Terror** (1793–1794): revolutionary tribunals, some 17,000 official executions and perhaps 40,000 deaths, a campaign of dechristianization that closed churches and substituted first a Cult of Reason and then Robespierre's Cult of the Supreme Being, a new calendar, the metric system, and total mobilization of the nation for war, the **levée en masse**, which put 800,000 men under arms and invented the citizen army.[^6] In July 1794 Robespierre's colleagues, fearing they were next, sent him to the guillotine. A conservative republic followed, corrupt and unstable, and in November 1799 a general it had relied on took power in a coup.

:::history How we got here
For most of the twentieth century the standard account of the Revolution was Marxist: a bourgeois revolution in which a rising capitalist class overthrew a feudal aristocracy, the necessary step from feudalism to capitalism, of which the Terror was the radical phase and Napoleon the consolidation. It made sense of the class language the revolutionaries themselves used. From the 1950s, Alfred Cobban and then François Furet dismantled it: the revolutionary leaders were lawyers and officials, not capitalists; the nobility was not feudal and was itself investing in trade; the economic results were, if anything, to delay French industrialization. Furet argued the Revolution was about politics and ideology, the invention of a new kind of politics in which the sovereign people could be spoken for by whoever claimed to know its will, and that the Terror was inherent in that idea, not an accident of war. Since then, cultural and gender historians have asked what the Revolution meant for women (who got divorce and inheritance rights and then lost the vote they had never had), for slaves (freed in 1794, re-enslaved by Napoleon), and for the provinces. The Marxist frame is gone; whether the Terror was the Revolution's logic or its perversion is still the question.[^7]
:::

## Napoleon

Napoleon Bonaparte, born in Corsica in 1769 to minor nobility, was a general at twenty-four, conqueror of Italy at twenty-seven, ruler of France at thirty, and emperor at thirty-five. He kept the Revolution's principles where they served him, legal equality, careers open to talent, the abolition of feudalism, religious toleration, and a state built on merit and centralized administration, and discarded the rest: elections were plebiscites, the press was censored, and the Senate did what it was told. His **Civil Code** (1804) codified the Revolution's legal gains and is still the law of France and the model for half the world's legal systems.[^8]

He fought for fifteen years and won almost everything until 1812. Austerlitz (1805), Jena (1806), Wagram (1809); Austria, Prussia, and Russia beaten in turn; the Holy Roman Empire abolished in 1806 after a thousand years; his brothers made kings of Holland, Naples, Spain, and Westphalia. Everywhere his armies went they brought the Code, the end of serfdom and guilds, the confiscation of church lands, and the idea of the nation, which the conquered turned against him. Britain alone he could not reach: Nelson destroyed his fleet at Trafalgar in 1805, and Britain financed every coalition against him. Spain, invaded in 1808, fought a guerrilla war that bled him. In 1812 he marched 600,000 men into Russia; 400,000 died there, of cold, hunger, typhus, and Cossacks, and the retreat from Moscow was the largest military catastrophe in European history to that point.[^9] Europe rose. He was beaten at Leipzig in 1813, abdicated in 1814, escaped from Elba, ruled for a hundred days, was beaten at Waterloo on 18 June 1815 by Wellington and Blücher, and died a British prisoner on St Helena in 1821.

The **Congress of Vienna** (1814–1815) put the map back with adjustments, restored the Bourbons to France, and built a system of great-power management, the Concert of Europe, that kept the continent free of general war for a century. It also tried to put back the old regime, and could not, because Napoleon had spread the Revolution's substance even while betraying its politics, and every European government after 1815 was, whether it admitted it or not, a modern state.

:::story Toussaint Louverture
The Declaration of the Rights of Man said men were born free. In the French colony of Saint-Domingue, the richest in the world, 500,000 enslaved Africans heard about it. In August 1791 they rose, burned the plantations, and fought France, Spain, and Britain for twelve years under a former slave named Toussaint Louverture, who read Raynal and the Enlightenment, made himself governor of the colony, and forced revolutionary France to abolish slavery in 1794. Napoleon reimposed it, sent an army, captured Toussaint by treachery, and let him die in a French prison in 1803. The army died of yellow fever and the ex-slaves' resistance; on 1 January 1804 Haiti declared itself independent, the first state founded by a successful slave revolt and the second republic in the Americas.[^10] France recognized it in 1825 in exchange for an indemnity that Haiti finished paying, with interest, in 1947. No event tested the Revolution's principles more directly, and the Revolution failed the test. Haiti passed it, and was made to pay for it for a century.

:::

:::key
The Enlightenment argued that institutions must justify themselves to reason; the French Revolution acted on the argument and discovered that the sovereign people, once invoked, could be claimed by anyone and could kill. Its permanent achievements, legal equality, the end of feudal privilege, the nation as the source of authority, the citizen army, the metric system, the Civil Code, were spread across Europe by the man who ended its politics. Modern politics, left and right, liberal and authoritarian, nationalist and universalist, was invented between 1789 and 1799, and every political argument since has used its vocabulary.
:::

A revolutionary declaration announces equality while slavery continues. How can a historian explain the contradiction without ignoring either part?

Examine who the authors counted as rights-bearing, who challenged that boundary, and which interests sustained exclusion. The declaration could supply tools for later demands while its authors denied those demands. Ideals matter historically both through their limits and through uses their originators did not control.

## Summary

- The Enlightenment applied reason to society: consent as the basis of government, separation of powers, toleration, the critique of cruelty, and the test of utility; its base was a new public sphere of print and conversation, and its first political form was reform by enlightened despots.
- The American Revolution proved an Enlightenment republic possible and bankrupted the French monarchy that funded it.
- France's fiscal crisis produced the Estates-General, the National Assembly, the Declaration of Rights, and, under war and civil war, the republic, the king's execution, the Terror, and the citizen army.
- Napoleon kept the Revolution's legal and administrative substance, discarded its politics, and spread both across Europe by conquest until Russia, Spain, and Britain broke him; Vienna restored the map but not the old regime.
- Haiti's slaves took the Declaration literally and won the only successful slave revolution in history, which France punished for a century.
