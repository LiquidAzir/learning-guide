## How do population change, economic power, and security shape East Asia today?

Chapter 36 ended in 2000 with China entering the world economy, Japan stalled, and Korea and Taiwan democratic. This chapter takes the story to the present, with the same warning as chapter 25: these events are not over, the historian has no hindsight, and the chapter stays with what is documented, marks what is argued, and stops where history becomes news.

## China's rise

China's entry to the World Trade Organization in 2001 began the phase of its growth that changed the world. Exports rose from about $250 billion in 2000 to over $2 trillion by 2012; China became the largest trading nation, the largest manufacturer, the largest emitter of carbon dioxide, the largest holder of American debt, and, in 2010, the second-largest economy, passing Japan. Its GDP per person, about $1,000 in 2000, passed $12,000 by 2021, the World Bank's threshold for high income being near.[^1] Some 400 million people moved from countryside to city in a generation, the largest migration in history; China built more high-speed rail than the rest of the world combined, more housing than had existed, and, from 2013, the Belt and Road Initiative's ports, railways, and power plants across Asia, Africa, and Europe. The party's bargain, growth for acquiescence, held.

Under **Xi Jinping**, general secretary from 2012, the terms changed. He centralized power in himself to a degree not seen since Mao, abolished the two-term limit on the presidency in 2018, purged rivals through an anti-corruption campaign that disciplined over a million officials, tightened control over the internet, universities, private business, and civil society, and put the party back at the center of everything. In Xinjiang, from 2017, the state interned somewhere over a million Uyghurs and other Muslims in "re-education" camps, a program the United States and several parliaments have called genocide and China calls counter-terrorism and vocational training; in Hong Kong, after mass protests in 2019, a national security law in 2020 ended the autonomy promised until 2047.[^2] Abroad, China built the world's largest navy by number of ships, fortified islands in the South China Sea, and pressed Taiwan with daily military flights. The relationship with the United States, which had been managed as engagement since Nixon, became, by the late 2010s, open rivalry: tariffs, technology controls on semiconductors, and mutual descriptions of the other as the century's central threat. Whether this is a new Cold War, a great-power transition of the kind that has usually ended in war, or something without precedent is the argument of the moment, and the historian's contribution is to note that every previous case of a rising power meeting an established one has been studied for exactly this reason.[^3]

## The demographic turn

One of East Asia’s most consequential changes is demographic. China, Japan, and South Korea all face aging populations and population decline, shaped by long-running changes in fertility, longevity, migration, and public policy.

Japan's population peaked at 128 million in 2008 and is falling by about half a million a year; nearly 30 percent of Japanese are over sixty-five, the highest share in the world, and the fertility rate is around 1.2 children per woman, against the 2.1 needed to hold a population steady. South Korea's fertility rate, the lowest ever recorded for any country, fell to 0.72 in 2023 before a slight recovery, which means each generation is roughly a third the size of the one before; its population peaked in 2020. And China, which had enforced a **one-child policy** from 1980 to 2015 to slow its growth, found when it lifted the policy that the growth had already stopped: the population fell for the first time in 2022, and by the end of 2025 had declined for four straight years to about 1,405 million, with 7.9 million births against 11.3 million deaths and a fertility rate near 1.0.[^4] India passed China as the most populous country in 2023. The consequences, shrinking workforces, pension systems built for a pyramid supporting a column, emptying villages and schools, and, for China, the prospect of growing old before it grows rich, are the region's structural condition for the rest of the century, and no government has found a policy that changes the trend.

## Japan and Korea

Japan in the 2000s and 2010s was a stable, wealthy, aging democracy managing decline: the LDP returned to power in 2012 under Shinzo Abe, the longest-serving prime minister in Japanese history, whose "Abenomics" of monetary expansion and fiscal stimulus ended deflation without restoring growth, and whose reinterpretation of Article 9 in 2015 allowed Japan's Self-Defense Forces to fight alongside allies. The earthquake, tsunami, and Fukushima nuclear disaster of March 2011 killed about 20,000 people and shut down Japan's nuclear power for years. Abe was assassinated in 2022. Japan's foreign policy, alarmed by China and North Korea, moved toward rearmament, with a 2022 commitment to double defense spending and the acquisition of long-range missiles that would have been unthinkable in 1990.[^5]

South Korea became, in the same decades, a cultural exporter whose films (*Parasite*, the first non-English film to win the Best Picture Oscar, 2020), television (*Squid Game*), and music (BTS) reached audiences that no Asian popular culture had, and a technological power (Samsung, Hyundai, the world's most wired society) with a per capita income that passed Japan's on some measures in the 2020s. Its politics remained turbulent: two presidents were impeached and removed (2017 and 2025), one after declaring martial law in December 2024 in an attempt that the National Assembly and the streets reversed within hours, a test of the 1987 democracy that it passed.[^6] North Korea tested its first nuclear weapon in 2006 and by the 2020s had an arsenal estimated at fifty or more warheads and missiles that could reach the United States; two summits and a border meeting between Kim Jong Un and Donald Trump in 2018–2019 produced nothing; and the regime, under its third Kim, remained what it had been.

## Taiwan

Taiwan in the 2020s was a democracy of 23 million people with the world's most advanced semiconductor industry (TSMC makes some 90 percent of the most advanced chips, on which every economy depends), a distinct national identity (most Taiwanese now identify as Taiwanese rather than Chinese, a reversal since 1990), and a neighbor that regards its status as unfinished business from 1949 and has said it will use force if necessary. The Democratic Progressive Party, which leans toward formal independence, won the presidency in 2016, 2020, and 2024; China responded with military exercises, economic pressure, and the daily flights; the United States, whose policy since 1979 has been deliberate ambiguity about whether it would defend the island, sold weapons and sent ships through the strait. The Taiwan question is the single most likely cause of a war between nuclear powers in the world today, and the reader should know that the historians and strategists who study it disagree about whether one is coming.[^7]

## Memory

East Asia's past is East Asian politics. Japan's wartime record, the Nanjing massacre, the comfort women, forced labor, the Yasukuni shrine where war criminals are enshrined and prime ministers have prayed, is the subject of recurring crises with China and Korea over textbooks, apologies (Japan has issued many; its neighbors judge them insufficient and its politicians sometimes undercut them), court judgments, and statues. China's Communist Party has made the "century of humiliation" and the war against Japan the core of its national narrative, while forbidding discussion of the famine, the Cultural Revolution, and Tiananmen, whose anniversary is scrubbed from the Chinese internet each June. South Korea's politics divide over how to judge the colonial period, the Korean War's civilian massacres (investigated by a Truth and Reconciliation Commission from 2005), and the dictatorship. Taiwan has its own reckoning with the 1947 massacre and martial law. The historian's observation is that every one of these societies is using the past to define what it is, that the arguments are about identity rather than evidence, and that the evidence, in archives opened since the 1990s in Japan, Taiwan, Russia, and to a lesser extent China, has more often complicated the national stories than confirmed them.[^8]

:::history How we got here
The story of East Asia since 2000 has been told, in the West, as the story of China's rise, and the frame has shifted three times in twenty years. In the 2000s the expectation was convergence: trade and prosperity would liberalize China, as they had South Korea and Taiwan, and China would become a "responsible stakeholder." In the 2010s, as Xi centralized and the economy kept growing, the frame became challenge: China as a peer competitor with an alternative model of authoritarian capitalism that might outperform democracy. In the 2020s, with growth slowing to about 5 percent by official count and less by independent estimates, a property bubble deflating, youth unemployment high, the population shrinking, and foreign investment retreating, a third frame appeared: "peak China," a power that has plateaued and may be more dangerous for it. Each frame was held with confidence and each was overtaken; the historian's advice is the same as chapter 25's. The Chinese government's own frame, of national rejuvenation after a century of humiliation, reaching its goal at the People's Republic's centenary in 2049, has not changed, and is worth knowing because Beijing acts on it.[^9]
:::

:::key
East Asia in the 2020s is the region where the twenty-first century's largest questions are being decided: whether a one-party state can sustain a modern economy and a peer rivalry with the United States; whether the most rapidly aging societies in history can remain prosperous; whether a democratic island can survive beside a great power that claims it; and whether societies that share a script, a philosophy, and two thousand years of history can agree on what happened in the twentieth century. The section began with the oracle bones and ends with Taiwan's semiconductors; the continuity is that the Chinese state, its neighbors, and the argument about how to govern, which chapter 26 introduced, are all still there.
:::

## Summary

- China became the world's second economy and largest trader after 2001, moved 400 million people to cities, and under Xi Jinping recentralized power, interned Uyghurs, ended Hong Kong's autonomy, and entered open rivalry with the United States.
- Japan, South Korea, and China all face aging populations and population decline. The timing and policy responses differ; low fertility, longer lives, and migration together shape the pressure on workers, families, and public services.
- Japan managed decline and began rearming; South Korea became a cultural and technological power and passed a test of its democracy in 2024–2025; North Korea built a nuclear arsenal.
- Taiwan became a democracy with a Taiwanese identity and the world's most important semiconductor industry, beside a China that claims it; the strait is the likeliest flashpoint for great-power war.
- The region's politics run on contested memory of the twentieth century; the Western frame for China has shifted from convergence to challenge to "peak China," and each frame has been overtaken.
