## Recap

Chapter 20 covered the political revolution. This chapter covers the economic one that was happening at the same time in Britain, and the century of its consequences: the industrial city, the working class, the ideologies invented to explain it, the failed revolutions of 1848, and the unification of Italy and Germany. It ends in 1871 with a new Europe: industrial, urbanizing, nationalist, and about to conquer the rest of the world.

## The Industrial Revolution

Between about 1760 and 1840, in Britain, the way goods were made changed. Cotton cloth, spun and woven by hand in cottages, came to be spun on machines, in mills, powered first by water and then by steam. Iron, smelted with charcoal in small quantities, came to be smelted with coke in blast furnaces by the thousands of tons. Coal, dug for heating, came to power everything. The **steam engine**, made efficient by James Watt in the 1770s and put on rails by George Stephenson in the 1820s, freed production from rivers and transport from horses. The **factory**, a building where hundreds of workers tended machines to a schedule set by a clock and a manager, replaced the workshop and the home.[^1] By 1850 Britain produced two thirds of the world's coal, half its iron, and half its cotton cloth, and half its population lived in towns, the first society in history for which that was true.

Why Britain? Historians have argued for two centuries and the leading current answers are: cheap coal and expensive labor, which made it pay to replace workers with coal-burning machines (Allen); an agricultural revolution that freed labor from the land; a large internal market with no tolls, a stable government that protected property, patents, and a cheap national debt (chapter 19); a culture of practical tinkering among artisans and a Scientific Revolution that had made mechanism respectable (Mokyr); colonies and slavery that supplied cotton and markets (Williams, and the recent "new history of capitalism"); and the accident of having iron and coal in the same places. None is sufficient alone and the weights are disputed.[^2] What is not disputed is that it spread: to Belgium and France by 1830, Germany and the United States by 1850, and everywhere with coal and capital by 1900.

The results for the people who lived through it were, for the first two generations, arguably worse. Real wages in Britain barely rose between 1780 and 1840 while output doubled; hours lengthened to twelve or fourteen a day, six days a week; children of six worked in mills and mines; Manchester's population went from 25,000 in 1770 to 300,000 in 1850, in housing without drains, and its life expectancy at birth fell to the mid-twenties.[^3] Then, from about 1850, wages rose, hours fell, and the gains of two generations of growth began to reach the people who had produced them. The "standard of living debate," about whether industrialization made workers better or worse off and when, is one of economic history's longest and its resolution, roughly, is: worse first, much better later, and the "first" lasted a working lifetime.

:::key
The Industrial Revolution is the most important event in economic history and, after the invention of agriculture, in human history. Before it, sustained growth in income per person did not happen anywhere; every gain was eaten by population. After it, in the countries that industrialized, income per person has grown roughly fifteenfold, life expectancy has doubled, and the share of people who farm has fallen from 80 percent to 2. It was made by coal, machines, factories, and a legal and financial system that rewarded their combination, and it was paid for, in its first two generations, by the workers who ran the machines. Everything in the rest of this section, from socialism to imperialism to the world wars, is a consequence of some countries having it before others.
:::

## The ideologies

The Revolution had given Europe its political vocabulary; industrialization gave it something to argue about, and the nineteenth century produced the "isms" that still structure politics.

**Liberalism** was the creed of the commercial and professional middle class: constitutional government, civil liberties, free trade, careers open to talent, a franchise for men of property, and the state kept out of the economy. It won in Britain (the Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to the middle class; the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 gave it free trade) and in the revolutions of 1830 in France and Belgium.[^4] **Conservatism**, articulated by Burke against the French Revolution, defended tradition, hierarchy, religion, and gradual change against rationalist schemes, and was the creed of the restored monarchies and their chief minister, Metternich, whose system suppressed liberal and national movements across Europe from 1815 to 1848. **Nationalism**, born in the Revolution's idea that the nation was sovereign and spread by resistance to Napoleon, held that every people with a common language and culture should have its own state, which was a program for uniting Italy and Germany and dismembering Austria, Russia, and the Ottomans.[^5]

**Socialism** was the response to the factory. Its early forms were utopian: Owen's model mills, Fourier's communes, Saint-Simon's technocracy. Then in 1848 Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published the *Communist Manifesto*, which argued that history was the history of class struggle, that capitalism had created a proletariat with nothing to sell but its labor, that capitalism's own contradictions would destroy it, and that the proletariat would inherit the earth. Marx's *Capital* (1867) gave the argument a theory of value and exploitation. Whether or not his predictions held ([the history chapter of the economics guide](#/economics/history)), his analysis of capitalism as a system with its own dynamics, and his insistence that ideas served material interests, shaped every social science and half the world's politics for a century.[^6]

## 1848

In February 1848 a Paris crowd overthrew the July Monarchy and declared a republic. Within weeks there were revolutions in Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Venice, Budapest, Prague, and most of the German and Italian states: liberals demanding constitutions, nationalists demanding unification or independence, workers demanding bread and work, all at once and all, briefly, winning. Metternich fled. The Austrian emperor promised a constitution. A German national assembly met at Frankfurt to write one for a united Germany.

Within eighteen months it had all failed. The liberals wanted constitutions, not social revolution, and when the Paris workers rose in June 1848 the republic shot them down, thousands dead. The nationalists wanted their own nations and not each other's: Germans would not give Bohemia to Czechs, Hungarians would not give Croatia to Croats, and the Habsburgs used each against the rest. The armies stayed loyal. The peasants, freed from the remaining feudal dues, went home. By 1849 the Habsburgs had retaken Vienna and Budapest with Russian help, the Prussian king had refused the German crown the Frankfurt assembly offered him, and France had elected Napoleon's nephew president, who made himself emperor in 1852.[^7] 1848 was "the turning point at which modern history failed to turn," in the historian's phrase, and its lesson, learned by conservatives and radicals alike, was that liberal idealism without armed force achieves nothing. The next generation applied the lesson.

## Blood and iron

Italy and Germany were unified not by liberals but by monarchies that used nationalism for their own ends. **Italy**: the kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, under Cavour, allied with Napoleon III to drive Austria from Lombardy in 1859; Garibaldi, a romantic republican, conquered Sicily and Naples with a thousand volunteers in 1860 and handed them to the Piedmontese king; Venice came in 1866 and Rome in 1870. The result was a constitutional monarchy that most Italians had not asked for and that took Piedmont's laws and taxes south; "we have made Italy," said one of its makers, "now we must make Italians."[^8]

**Germany**: Otto von Bismarck, Prussian prime minister from 1862, declared that the great questions of the day would be settled by blood and iron, and settled them in three wars. He beat Denmark (1864), then Austria (1866, in seven weeks, with railways and breech-loading rifles), which excluded Austria from Germany, and then France (1870–1871), whose emperor he captured at Sedan and whose capital he besieged. In January 1871, at Versailles, the German princes proclaimed the Prussian king German Emperor; France lost Alsace-Lorraine and paid five billion francs.[^9] The **German Empire** was a federal state with a parliament elected by universal male suffrage, a chancellor responsible only to the emperor, and an army responsible to nobody but him. It was the most powerful state in Europe on the day it was born, and the balance of power that had held since 1815 was over.

:::story Cholera and the map
In August 1854 cholera broke out in Soho, London, and killed some 500 people in ten days and over 600 in all. The medical consensus held that it spread by bad air. John Snow, a physician who had doubted this since the epidemic of 1849, went door to door recording deaths and marked each on a street map. They clustered around the water pump on Broad Street; the workhouse nearby, which had its own well, was spared, as was the brewery, whose men drank beer. He persuaded the parish to remove the pump handle. The epidemic, already waning, ended.[^10] It took another thirty years and the identification of the bacterium for the medical establishment to accept that cholera came from water, but Snow's map was the beginning of epidemiology, and the sewers, water supplies, and public health boards that London and every industrial city built over the next generation were the reason the industrial city stopped killing its inhabitants faster than it could recruit them. Life expectancy in Manchester passed forty in the 1880s. The Industrial Revolution's second phase was not steam but drains.
:::

## The world of 1871

By 1871 Europe had two new great powers, Germany and Italy, and one very old one, Austria, weakened and reorganized as Austria-Hungary in 1867. Russia had freed its serfs (1861), twenty-two million of them, in the largest emancipation in history, and was beginning to industrialize. Britain was the workshop of the world and about to be overtaken by Germany and the United States. France was a republic again, bitter and bent on revenge for Alsace-Lorraine. The Ottoman Empire was losing the Balkans piece by piece. Railways covered the continent; the telegraph linked it; steamships were shrinking the Atlantic to ten days. Cities were growing faster than ever and were, for the first time, becoming safe to live in. Most Europeans could read. And the confidence of the age, in progress, science, nation, and Europe's right to lead the world, was about to be turned outward, on Africa and Asia, in the chapter that follows.

## Summary

- The Industrial Revolution, in Britain from about 1760, replaced hand production with steam-powered factories and made sustained growth in income per person possible for the first time in history; its causes are disputed (coal, wages, institutions, empire) and its first two generations of workers paid for it.
- Liberalism, conservatism, nationalism, and socialism were invented to explain and direct the new world; Marx gave socialism its theory in 1848 and 1867.
- The revolutions of 1848 briefly won everywhere and failed everywhere, because liberals, nationalists, and workers wanted different things and the armies stayed loyal.
- Cavour and Bismarck unified Italy and Germany from above, by war and diplomacy; the German Empire of 1871 ended the balance of 1815.
- Public health, beginning with Snow's cholera map, made the industrial city livable; by 1871 Europe was industrial, literate, nationalist, and confident.
