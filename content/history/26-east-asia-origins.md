## Recap

The Ancient section touched China only in passing, because its story is continuous enough to deserve its own telling. This section follows East Asia, meaning China, Korea, and Japan, from the first farmers to the present, and it begins here with the two and a half millennia in which China, in the sense of a shared civilization with a shared script and a shared argument about how humans should live, came into being. Japan and Korea enter in chapter 28, when the record allows.

## Farmers and the first cities

Farming began in China independently of the Fertile Crescent (chapter 2): rice in the Yangzi valley and millet in the Yellow River valley, both by about 8000 BCE. By 5000 BCE the Yangshao culture of the middle Yellow River was making painted pottery in large villages; by 3300 the Liangzhu culture near modern Hangzhou had built a city with a hydraulic system of dams and canals and carved jade in forms that would stay sacred in China for three thousand years; by 2300 the walled stone city of Shimao on the Loess Plateau, with its sacrificial pits of young women's skulls, and the Longshan cultures of the lower Yellow River show something like states.[^1] Liangzhu collapsed around 2300 BCE, and cave records now point to catastrophic flooding. None of these cultures wrote, and until the 1980s Chinese history was assumed to begin on the Yellow River; the archaeology of the last forty years has shown a dozen regional cultures, from Sichuan to the coast, interacting to make what became China.

Tradition names the first dynasty the **Xia** (c. 2070–1600 BCE), founded by Yu, who tamed the floods. Whether it existed is argued: a Bronze Age culture at Erlitou fits its time and place, but nothing there names itself Xia, and the earliest texts to mention it are a thousand years later.[^2] Chinese archaeology treats Erlitou as Xia; most Western scholars treat Xia as a later dynasty's legitimating memory of an earlier one. The argument is about what counts as evidence, and it is the same argument as the one over David's kingdom in chapter 5.

## The Shang: writing to the ancestors

The **Shang** (c. 1600–1046 BCE) is the first Chinese dynasty attested by its own writing, and the writing was found by accident. In 1899 a scholar in Beijing noticed inscriptions on "dragon bones" sold by apothecaries as medicine; the bones were traced to Anyang in Henan, the last Shang capital, where excavations from 1928 and a century of collecting have yielded some 150,000 fragments of **oracle bones**: turtle shells and ox scapulae on which the king's diviners had written questions to the royal ancestors, applied heat until the bone cracked, read the answer in the cracks, and often recorded the outcome.[^3] Will it rain? Will the harvest be good? Will the queen's childbirth be lucky? (It was not; it was a girl.) Should we attack the Qiang? The script is recognizably the ancestor of modern Chinese characters, and a literate Chinese today can pick out words on a bone from 1200 BCE. No other civilization's writing has that continuity.

The bones show a kingdom centered on a king who was the sole intermediary with powerful ancestors, a bronze industry that produced ritual vessels of a technical sophistication nobody has surpassed, a calendar, an army with chariots (which arrived from the steppe around 1200), and human sacrifice on a large scale: the royal tombs at Anyang hold hundreds of victims each, and the bones record the sacrifice of thousands of war captives to the ancestors.[^4] At Sanxingdui in Sichuan, a contemporary culture with no writing made bronze masks and figures unlike anything Shang, and smashed and buried them in pits whose excavation, resumed in 2020, is still recovering thousands of objects; China in 1200 BCE was several civilizations, not one.

## The Zhou: the Mandate of Heaven

Around 1046 BCE the **Zhou**, a people from the Wei valley to the west, overthrew the Shang. They needed to explain why, and their explanation became the theory of Chinese politics for three thousand years. Heaven (*tian*), an impersonal moral order rather than a personal god, gives the right to rule, the **Mandate of Heaven**, to a virtuous house, and withdraws it when the house becomes corrupt; the Shang had lost the Mandate by their cruelty and debauchery, and the Zhou had received it.[^5] The theory justified every subsequent dynasty's founding and every subsequent rebellion, made the ruler's virtue the measure of his legitimacy, made floods and famines evidence against him, and gave the Chinese state a religious foundation that was ethical rather than theological. The Zhou also replaced most human sacrifice with ritual, and the early Zhou texts, the *Book of Documents* and the *Book of Odes* (305 poems, some of them love songs, some of them complaints about tax), are the beginning of Chinese literature.

The Zhou ruled through kinsmen granted territories, and the kinsmen's descendants became independent. In 771 BCE the capital fell to a coalition of nomads and rebels, the court moved east to Luoyang, and the **Eastern Zhou** (770–256 BCE) was a king without power over lords who fought each other for five centuries. Historians divide it into the **Spring and Autumn** period (770–476), named for the chronicle of the state of Lu, with about 150 states shrinking to a dozen, and the **Warring States** (475–221), with seven great powers fighting wars of annihilation with armies of hundreds of thousands of conscript infantry, iron weapons, crossbows, and, from about 300, cavalry copied from the steppe.[^6] It was the most violent period in Chinese history before the twentieth century, and it was, like the Greek fifth century, the most creative.

## The Hundred Schools

Between about 550 and 250 BCE, teachers wandered from court to court offering rulers advice on how to survive, and the surviving advice is the foundation of Chinese thought. Tradition calls them the **Hundred Schools**; four matter most.

**Confucius** (Kong Qiu, c. 551–479 BCE), a minor official from Lu who never held real power, taught that social order comes from moral cultivation: a ruler who is humane (*ren*), observes ritual propriety (*li*), and governs by example rather than force will have subjects who are the same, because people imitate their superiors. The family is the model: filial piety toward parents and ancestors, extended upward, is loyalty to the ruler, and the ruler is a father to the people. Government is the business of the educated and virtuous, the **junzi** or gentleman, a term Confucius redefined from birth to character. He was not much interested in gods or the afterlife ("respect the spirits but keep them at a distance"), he claimed to be transmitting the ways of the early Zhou rather than inventing, and his sayings were collected by his students as the *Analects*.[^7] His followers Mencius (who argued human nature is good) and Xunzi (who argued it is bad and needs ritual to shape it) elaborated the system, and it became, from the Han onward (chapter 27), the ideology of the Chinese state and the curriculum of its officials for two thousand years.

**Daoism**, in the *Daodejing* attributed to Laozi (a figure who may not have existed; the text is probably fourth-century) and the *Zhuangzi*, replied that Confucian striving was the problem. The **Dao**, the Way, is the natural course of things; the sage does nothing (*wuwei*, non-action) and everything gets done; the ruler who governs least governs best; names, categories, and moral distinctions are artificial; and the best life is one that flows with the Way like water, which is soft and overcomes everything.[^8] Daoism became the counterweight to Confucianism in Chinese culture, the philosophy of the recluse, the poet, and the official out of office, and later a religion with gods, alchemy, and a search for immortality.

**Mozi** (c. 470–391) argued for universal love without the Confucian gradations of family, for utility as the test of policy, against aggressive war, and against expensive rituals and music. His school was a major rival of the Confucians for two centuries and then vanished.

**Legalism** (*fajia*), in the writings of Shang Yang and Han Feizi, told rulers what they wanted to hear: people are selfish, virtue is unreliable, and the state should be run by clear laws (*fa*), harsh and certain punishments, rich rewards, a bureaucracy of officials appointed for competence and watched for loyalty, and an economy organized for war.[^9] The state of Qin adopted it under Shang Yang from 356 BCE, and it worked: Qin abolished the aristocracy's privileges, registered every household, rewarded farmers and soldiers, punished everyone else, and became the most efficient military machine of the Warring States. In 221 BCE it conquered the last of its rivals, and its king declared himself the First Emperor. That is chapter 27.

:::history How we got here
For two thousand years Chinese scholars treated the Confucian classics as the record of a golden age of sage-kings and read the Warring States as its decline. Modern historiography inverted this: the Warring States became China's Axial Age (chapter 12), and its violence the condition of its creativity, because rulers competing for survival had to hire talent and listen to advice. The twentieth century's greatest change was archaeological. The oracle bones proved the Shang real and the traditional texts partly reliable; tombs since the 1970s have yielded manuscripts on bamboo and silk (Mawangdui, Guodian, and the Tsinghua slips, still being published) that give versions of the classics older than any known, texts nobody had heard of, and the Legalist and military writings that the Confucian tradition had preserved poorly.[^10] Confucius himself has been argued over: a conservative transmitter, a radical who redefined nobility as virtue, a political failure whose students made him, and, in the People's Republic, a feudal reactionary (1970s) and a national sage (2010s). What the reader should take from this is that the Chinese past has been the property of the Chinese present continuously for three thousand years, and that every generation has re-read it.
:::

:::key
Three things made China between 1200 and 221 BCE. A **script** that has been read continuously since the oracle bones, so that the past has never been lost or foreign in the way Sumer was to Rome. A **theory of legitimacy**, the Mandate of Heaven, that made the ruler's virtue the measure of his right to rule and gave rebellion a justification. And an **argument** about how to govern, between the Confucian claim that good rulers make good people and the Legalist claim that good laws make obedient ones, that the Qin settled one way, the Han settled the other, and every Chinese government since, including the present one, has conducted in some form. The section that follows is that argument's history.
:::

## Summary

- Farming began in China independently around 8000 BCE; a dozen regional Neolithic cultures, Liangzhu and Shimao among them, produced cities and proto-states before any writing.
- The Shang (c. 1600–1046) is the first dynasty attested by its own writing, the oracle bones, whose script is the direct ancestor of modern Chinese; it was a kingdom of bronze, ancestors, and human sacrifice.
- The Zhou justified their conquest with the Mandate of Heaven, the theory that made virtue the basis of legitimacy for three thousand years, and then lost control to their lords for five centuries of Spring and Autumn and Warring States.
- The Hundred Schools, Confucians, Daoists, Mohists, and Legalists, argued about how to govern; Confucius made moral cultivation the basis of order, Laozi made non-action, and the Legalists made law and punishment.
- Qin adopted Legalism and conquered everything by 221 BCE; archaeology since 1899 has both confirmed and rewritten the traditional account.
