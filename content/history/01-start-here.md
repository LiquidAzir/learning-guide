## A body in the ice

In September 1991 two hikers in the Ötztal Alps found a corpse melting out of a glacier. The police assumed a lost climber. The corpse turned out to be about 5,300 years old: a man in his forties, with a copper axe, a bow he had not finished making, a flint knife, a coat of goat and sheep hide, sixty-one tattoos, and an arrowhead in his left shoulder. Over the next thirty years, scientists read his last days from his body. His stomach held ibex meat and einkorn wheat eaten an hour before death; pollen in his gut placed his final climb in late spring; the arrow had cut an artery and killed him within minutes; a wound on his hand, days old, said he had been fighting before.[^1] No text mentions him. No one wrote his name. And yet we know how he died, what he ate, where he grew up, and that someone wanted him dead, with more confidence than we know most of what happened in the year 1000.

That is the first lesson of history as a discipline: the past is not what was written down. It is everything that left a trace, and the craft is in reading the traces. The second lesson is that the traces are partial, biased, and often lying, and that the same evidence supports different stories depending on who is telling it. This guide is about both: what happened, as best we can tell, and how we can tell.

## What history is, and is not

**History** is the study of the human past from evidence, and the argument about what the evidence means. It is not the past itself, which is gone; it is not memory, which is what individuals and groups carry and which is reliably wrong in known ways; and it is not a fixed body of facts, though it contains many. It is a set of claims, each resting on sources, each open to revision when new sources appear or old ones are reread. Every chapter in this guide ends with a list of what its claims rest on, and several contain a section on how the standard story changed and why.

Two words need separating at the start. **Prehistory** is the human past before writing, known only from objects, bones, and now genes: the first two and a half million years of the story, and in most of the world the first several thousand years after farming. **History** in the narrow sense begins with written records, around 3200 BCE in Mesopotamia, and arrives at different times in different places: 1200 BCE in China, the first century CE in much of northern Europe, and never, before European contact, in Australia. The line between them has blurred as archaeology and genetics have made the unwritten past legible, and this guide crosses it freely.

## The sources

Everything a historian says rests on **sources**, and knowing what kind of source is behind a claim tells you how much to trust it.

**Written sources** come in two kinds. **Primary sources** were produced at the time or by participants: a law code carved in stone, a letter, a tax record, a chronicle written by someone who was there. **Secondary sources** are later accounts built from them, including every history book, including this one. A primary source is closer to events and not therefore truthful: Julius Caesar's account of his wars in Gaul is a primary source written by a general explaining to Roman voters why his wars were necessary.[^2] The historian's first question of any document is not "is it true?" but "who wrote this, for whom, and what did they want the reader to believe?"

**Material sources** are the physical remains: buildings, tools, pottery, bones, coins, garbage. **Archaeology** recovers and interprets them, and it has two great advantages over texts. It is far more abundant, since everyone left rubbish and few left writing; and it does not lie in the way documents do, though it can be misread. A city's trash heaps tell you what people actually ate; its inscriptions tell you what its rulers wanted remembered.

**Scientific sources** are the newest and have transformed the field in the last twenty years. **Radiocarbon dating**, which measures the decay of carbon-14 in anything that was once alive, gives dates to within a few decades for the last 50,000 years.[^3] **Dendrochronology**, tree-ring dating, gives exact years and a record of climate. Ice cores, lake sediments, and cave deposits record volcanic eruptions, droughts, and temperature. And **ancient DNA**, recovered from bones and teeth and sequenced in bulk since about 2010, has rewritten the story of who moved where: it showed, for instance, that the people who built Stonehenge were almost entirely replaced, genetically, by newcomers from the continent within a few centuries, a migration no text records.[^4] Chapter 38 surveys what these methods have changed.

:::howto Reading a primary source
The questions historians ask of any document, letter, inscription, or image, in order:
1. **What is it?** A law, a private letter, a public monument, a story? Each genre has its own conventions about truth. A king's inscription claiming victory is a genre in which no king ever lost.
2. **Who made it, when, and for whom?** A chronicle written a century after the events it describes is a different object from one written the same year. An audience of gods, subjects, posterity, or one friend changes everything.
3. **What did the author want?** Persuasion, record-keeping, self-justification, piety, entertainment. The wanting shapes the telling.
4. **What does it take for granted?** The most valuable information in a source is often what it does not bother to explain, because everyone knew it: prices, distances, what counts as an insult.
5. **What else confirms or contradicts it?** A claim resting on one source is a claim. Independent sources agreeing, especially hostile ones, is evidence.
6. **What is missing?** Who does not appear, and why? Most sources were written by literate men with property about other literate men with property.

*Example.* The Roman historian Tacitus reports that the emperor Nero blamed Rome's great fire of 64 CE on Christians and executed them cruelly. Tacitus wrote fifty years later, loathed Nero, and used the episode to display Nero's viciousness rather than to defend Christians, whom he also disliked. The passage is therefore good evidence that Christians existed in Rome in 64 and were persecuted, since Tacitus had no motive to invent that, and weak evidence for the details of the fire's cause, which served his portrait of Nero.[^5]
:::

## Dating, and the problem of calendars

Dates in this guide are given as BCE (before the common era) and CE (common era), which are the same years as BC and AD without the theological claim; there is no year zero, so 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE. "The fifth century BCE" means 500 to 401 BCE, counting toward the present. Ancient dates are often approximate, marked "c." for *circa*, and for anything before the first millennium BCE, uncertainties of a century or more are normal. Every society kept its own calendar, counting from the reign of a king, the founding of a city, or a religious event; converting them is a technical field of its own, and the dates here are the current scholarly consensus, which for some periods (the Egyptian Old Kingdom, the early Chinese dynasties) has shifted by decades within living memory.

## What historians argue about

If the facts are the facts, why does history keep being rewritten? Four reasons, and understanding them is understanding the discipline.

**New evidence.** Ancient DNA, deciphered scripts, opened archives, excavated cities. The Herculaneum scrolls, carbonized by Vesuvius in 79 CE and unreadable for two millennia, began to yield their text to artificial intelligence in 2023.[^6] Chapter 38 is about this.

**New questions.** For most of its history, history was the deeds of rulers and armies. Since about 1900, and decisively since 1960, historians have asked about peasants, women, slaves, prices, disease, climate, and the texture of ordinary life, and the sources to answer them (parish registers, tax rolls, bones) were always there. This guide tries to do both.

**Interpretation.** The same facts bear different stories. Rome "fell" in 476, or transformed gradually, or never fell in the East, where it lasted until 1453; each version is defensible from the same evidence and each has been the consensus at some point.[^7] Where such arguments are live, this guide presents the sides and says which the current weight of scholarship favors, without pretending the matter is closed.

**Present concerns.** Each generation asks the past the questions that trouble it, which is why histories of empire were written differently in 1900, 1960, and 2020. This is not a defect to be eliminated; it is why history stays alive. But it means every history, including this one, is written from somewhere, and the reader should know where. This guide was written in 2026, tries to give each society's own account of itself before judging it, and treats "why did intelligent people believe this?" as a more useful question than "were they right?"

:::key
History is an argument from evidence, not a list of facts. A good historical claim names its sources, weighs them against each other, admits what is uncertain, and says what would change the conclusion. A bad one, however confidently stated, does none of those things. The single most useful habit this guide can teach is to ask, of any statement about the past, "how do we know that?" Most of the time there is a good answer. Sometimes there is not, and knowing which is which is the skill.
:::

## How this guide is organized

History is written here in **sections**, each a self-contained course, and more sections will be added over time. The three so far:

Each section tells its story in chronological order, so its chapters build. The sections can be read independently, and they cross-reference where the stories touch: the Mongols appear in both European and East Asian history; Buddhism is born in the Ancient section and reshapes East Asia; the Opium Wars are a European and an East Asian event. Chapters 38 to 40 serve the whole subject: what new methods are finding, who the historians and actors were, and every term and date in one place.

:::howto A useful first pass
Choose the ancient, European, or East Asian route and read its chapters in order. Keep the timeline nearby, and distinguish what a source records from what a historian infers.

At the end of a core chapter, try the question before opening “Show the reasoning” or “One way to reason it through.” Explain your answer in a sentence or work the calculation; then compare the reasoning, not just the result. Reading-time estimates exclude time spent practicing.
:::

## The conventions

- **Bold terms** are defined where they appear and collected in the glossary (chapter 40).
- Each chapter opens with a scene, because history is people doing things, and closes with a summary. **Dates to hold onto** boxes give the handful of dates worth remembering; you do not need the rest.
- **How we got here** boxes tell how historians' understanding of the topic changed: what the old story was, why it made sense, and what overturned it.
- **The story** boxes tell one episode in detail, because a single well-documented event teaches more than a paragraph of generalization.
- **Where it stands today** boxes mark live arguments.
- Key factual claims point to a numbered source at the bottom of the chapter, with a link where the source is free to read. For antiquity, many primary sources are free online in translation and the footnotes point to them; go and read a page of Herodotus, or the Analects, or the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and you will understand the period better than any summary can manage.
- Names are given in the forms most used in English scholarship (Confucius, not Kong Fuzi; Beijing, not Peking), with the alternative on first use where it helps.

:::try
Before reading on, write down three things you believe about the past. Examples: the Middle Ages were a dark age. Rome fell to barbarian invasion. China was isolated and unchanging for two thousand years. Farming was an improvement on hunting. Each is a claim historians have tested, and each turns out to be wrong or much more complicated. See which survive.
:::
