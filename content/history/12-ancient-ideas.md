## Recap

The political chapters have mentioned religion as it came up: the temple economy of Sumer, the Egyptian afterlife, the Hebrew covenant, Greek philosophy, the Buddha, Christianity. This chapter steps back to look at the whole pattern, because the ancient world produced, in the span of about six centuries, nearly every religion and ethical system that people live by today, and the coincidence needs explaining. It also lays out what each of them actually taught, since later chapters assume the reader knows.

## The old religions

Before about 800 BCE, religion nearly everywhere looked alike in its essentials. There were many **gods**, each responsible for a domain (storms, grain, the city, childbirth) and each requiring **sacrifice**: food, drink, animals, occasionally people, offered at altars by priests or kings. The gods were powerful, not good; they wanted service, not love, and they punished neglect with drought, plague, and defeat. Religion was public and collective, the city's business rather than the individual's, and its aim was to keep the world running: the Nile rising, the rain falling, the enemy losing. There was usually an afterlife, and it was usually dim. The Mesopotamian dead ate dust in a house of darkness; the Homeric dead were witless shades; only Egypt, with its mummies and judgment, promised anything better, and only to those who could afford it.[^1]

This religion had no creeds, no conversion, and no heresy. You worshipped the gods of your city because you were born there; when you conquered another city, you added its gods to yours. It also had no separation between religion and everything else: the king was the gods' steward, the law was their gift, the calendar was their festivals.

## The turn

Between about 800 and 200 BCE, in four regions with little or no contact, something changed. In Israel, the prophets; in Greece, the philosophers; in India, the Upanishads, the Buddha, and Mahavira; in China, Confucius, Laozi, and the Hundred Schools (chapter 26). The German philosopher Karl Jaspers called this the **Axial Age** in 1949, and the label has stuck though the explanation has not.[^2] What the movements shared: a turn from ritual to ethics, from what the gods want to how one should live; a concern with the individual's inner state rather than the community's outward performance; universal claims, about all humans rather than one city's people; a transcendent standard, God, Heaven, *dharma*, the Good, by which kings and customs could be judged and found wanting; and a class of teachers, prophets, sages, and philosophers, who were not priests and who criticized the powerful.

Why then, and why everywhere? Proposed answers: iron-age prosperity produced literate classes with leisure to think; the collapse of the Bronze Age palaces broke the old link between king and cult; new empires (Assyria, Persia, Qin) made local gods seem small and demanded a bigger frame; growing cities and markets made individuals who were not defined by lineage; writing let arguments accumulate. None is sufficient and the coincidence may be partly an artifact of what survived.[^3] But the change was real, and everything that follows in this guide, in every region, happens in the world it made.

## What they taught

Six traditions matter for the rest of this guide. Here is each in a paragraph, stated as its adherents would recognize it.

**Zoroastrianism.** The prophet Zoroaster (Zarathustra), somewhere in eastern Iran, sometime between 1500 and 600 BCE (the range is that uncertain), taught that there is one supreme god, Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord; that the world is a battlefield between truth and the Lie; that every person must choose sides by good thoughts, words, and deeds; that the dead are judged, the good rewarded in heaven and the wicked punished; and that history will end with a final judgment and the renewal of the world.[^4] It was the religion of the Persian empire (chapter 5) and of Iran until Islam; roughly 100,000 to 200,000 people, in India, Iran, and a diaspora, keep it today. Its ideas, one god, a cosmic struggle with evil, judgment, heaven and hell, a savior, and an end of days, appear in Judaism after the Exile, when the Jews lived under Persian rule, and through Judaism in Christianity and Islam. The debt is disputed in detail and clear in outline.

**Judaism.** The Hebrew Bible, edited into shape between about 600 and 200 BCE from older material (chapter 5), tells of one God who made the world, chose one people, gave them a law, and judges them by whether they keep it. The distinctive claims: there is only one God and he alone may be worshipped; he is a moral agent who demands justice, above all for the poor, the widow, and the stranger; he acts in history, so history has a direction and meaning; and his people are bound to him by covenant, a contract with obligations on both sides. The **prophets**, Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the rest, denounced kings and priests in God's name for oppressing the poor and trusting in ritual, and that a religion should judge its own establishment was new.[^5] After the Babylonian exile and the temple's second destruction by Rome in 70 CE, Judaism became a religion of the book, the synagogue, and the rabbi, portable anywhere, and it survived two thousand years without a state. Ethical monotheism, the idea that the one God cares how you treat people, is its gift to everything downstream.

**Greek philosophy.** Chapter 6 covered the thinkers; the point here is the method. The Greeks asked the Axial questions without a god to answer them: what is the world made of, what is knowledge, what is justice, how should one live, and they answered by argument, in public, with the expectation of being contradicted. Plato's forms, Aristotle's virtues, the Stoics' providence, and Epicurus's atoms are answers; the practice of reasoning toward answers and defending them is the legacy, and it became the tool with which Christian, Jewish, and Muslim theologians would later think about their revelations.[^6]

**Hinduism.** The name is a later label for the family of traditions that grew from the Vedas and the Upanishads (chapter 10) and absorbed the local cults of a subcontinent. The shared frame: the soul (*atman*) passes through many lives; its condition is determined by *karma*, the moral consequence of action; the goal is release (*moksha*) from the cycle; and one's duty (*dharma*) depends on one's station and stage of life. Above the many gods is one reality, *brahman*, of which they are aspects, and the paths to it are knowledge, devotion, and dutiful action, the *Bhagavad Gita*'s three ways.[^7] It has no founder, no single scripture, no creed, and no central authority, and it has been the religion of most Indians for two thousand years.

**Buddhism.** The Buddha's teaching (chapter 10): life is unsatisfactory because everything changes and we crave permanence; there is no permanent self to be found; the craving can be extinguished by a disciplined path of ethics, meditation, and insight; the result is nirvana. It took the Hindu frame of karma and rebirth and removed the gods' importance, the priests' authority, and the self. In its Mahayana form it added compassionate saviors and celestial Buddhas and became the religion of East Asia; in its Theravada form it remains the religion of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.[^8] It is the first tradition to have spread across cultures by persuasion rather than conquest or birth.

**Christianity.** Jesus of Nazareth, a Jewish teacher executed around 30 CE (chapter 9), preached the coming kingdom of God, love of God and neighbor, and mercy for sinners. His followers believed he rose from the dead and was God's son and Messiah; Paul argued that his death atoned for humanity's sin and that faith in him, not the Jewish law, made one right with God. The councils of the fourth and fifth centuries fixed the doctrine that Jesus was fully God and fully human and that God is three persons in one.[^9] Christianity took Judaism's one moral God and made him available to everyone; it took Greek philosophy's tools and used them to define its God; and it took from the Persian inheritance the cosmic struggle, the judgment, and the end of days. The result was a religion of personal salvation, universal membership, an exclusive claim to truth, and an organized church, which is the combination that took over the Roman empire (chapter 13).

**Confucianism and Daoism** belong to chapter 26, where China's Axial Age is treated in its own setting. In brief: Confucius taught that a good society comes from good people, made by ritual, learning, and the cultivation of humaneness, and that the ruler's virtue, not his force, orders the state; the Daoists replied that the way to live is to stop striving and follow the natural course. Neither was much concerned with gods.

:::key
The old religions asked "what do the gods want?" and answered "sacrifice." The Axial traditions asked "how should I live?" and answered with an ethics, a discipline, and a standard above the king. That is the shift, and everything about it was new: the individual as the unit of religious concern, the universal claim, the criticism of power in the name of something higher, the teacher who was not a priest. Every religion practiced by most of humanity today is either an Axial tradition or, in Islam's case, its direct heir. The old religions did not die; they became "paganism," the thing the new ones defined themselves against.
:::

## How religions spread

Zoroastrianism spread with an empire and shrank with it. Judaism spread by diaspora and mostly did not seek converts. Greek philosophy spread with Greek education and never became a religion, though Stoicism came close. Hinduism spread by absorption, taking local gods into its pantheon and local peoples into its order. Buddhism spread by missionaries and monks along trade routes, with royal patronage (Ashoka, Kanishka, later the Chinese and Japanese courts) when it could get it. Christianity spread through the cities of an empire whose language and roads it used, by community and exclusivity, and then, after 312, by the state.

The pattern for the next two thousand years was set here. Religions that demand exclusive allegiance and organize their members outcompete religions that do not; religions carried by literate professionals travel; religions adopted by rulers become the religion of their peoples, in time, whatever the peoples thought at first. Chapter 13 shows the pattern working in the largest single case, the Christianization of Rome, and chapter 14 shows the second largest, the rise of Islam.

:::story A conversion in 386
In the 370s a young North African student, and then teacher, of rhetoric named Augustine, ambitious, brilliant, and sexually restless, was working his way through the available options. He had been raised by a Christian mother and found the Bible's Latin crude. He joined the Manichaeans, a Persian-born sect that explained evil as a rival power to God, and left when their astronomy proved wrong. He read the Skeptics and found he could not live doubting. He read Plato's followers and found God, but not a way to him. In a garden in Milan in 386 he heard a child's voice singing "take up and read," opened Paul's letters at random, and was converted. He wrote the story down in the *Confessions* around 400, the first autobiography in the modern sense, addressed to God and asking how a will can be divided against itself.[^10] Augustine's life is the Axial Age in one man: the old public religion irrelevant, a market of rival universal claims, and the question, which he answered as Bishop of Hippo in thirty-five years of writing, of what the soul owes to the city of men and what to the city of God.
:::

## Summary

- Before about 800 BCE religion nearly everywhere was the collective service of many gods through sacrifice, with no creeds, conversion, or personal ethics.
- Between 800 and 200 BCE, in Israel, Greece, India, and China independently, teachers who were not priests turned religion toward ethics, the individual, universal claims, and a standard above kings: the Axial Age, unexplained but real.
- Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Greek philosophy, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Christianity each answered "how should I live?" in a distinct way; this chapter stated each as its adherents would.
- Judaism gave ethical monotheism; Greece gave the method of argument; Persia gave judgment and the end of days; Christianity combined them into a universal, exclusive, organized religion of personal salvation.
- Religions that are exclusive, organized, carried by literate professionals, and adopted by rulers spread; the pattern set here runs through both later sections.
