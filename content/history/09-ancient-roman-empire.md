## How did Rome turn conquest into a durable imperial system?

Augustus ended the republic in 27 BCE while pretending not to. This chapter is about the empire he founded at its height, the two and a half centuries historians call the **Principate**, from *princeps*, "first citizen," the title Augustus preferred to king. It is less about emperors, who are the least representative Romans, than about the empire as a system: how it was governed, who lived in it, what it built, and the two things born in it, Roman law and Christianity, that outlasted it by two thousand years.

## Augustus's settlement

Augustus ruled for forty years (27 BCE–14 CE), long enough that when he died almost no one remembered the republic working. His genius was to keep every republican form and drain it of power. Consuls were still elected; he decided who. The Senate still met; it did what he wished. He held no office called emperor, only a bundle of republican powers granted by the Senate: the authority of a tribune, command of the frontier provinces and therefore the army, and the vague, immense *auctoritas* of the man who had ended the civil wars.[^1] He paid the army from his own vast wealth and settled the veterans on land, so that the legions were his. He gave Rome peace, grain, games, and marble, and wrote his own account of his reign to be carved on his tomb. His one failure was succession: a monarchy that pretended not to be one had no rule for choosing the next monarch, and every dynasty that followed would fight over it.

The dynasties are a familiar roll: the Julio-Claudians (Tiberius, the mad or maligned Caligula, Claudius, Nero, who killed his mother, and lost the throne to a revolt in 68); a year of civil war and four emperors; the Flavians (Vespasian, who built the Colosseum, Titus, Domitian, murdered in 96); and the "five good emperors" (Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius, 96–180), each adopting his successor until Marcus left the throne to his son Commodus, who was a disaster and was strangled in 192.[^2] The writers who are our sources, Tacitus, Suetonius, Cassius Dio, were senators or courtiers who saw the emperors up close and mostly loathed them, and the lurid portraits of Tiberius and Nero are as much senatorial revenge as reportage. What matters more than any emperor is that the machine ran regardless of who sat on top of it.

## How the empire worked

At its height under Trajan (98–117), the empire ran from Scotland to the Sahara and from Portugal to Iraq: some 5 million square kilometers and perhaps 60 million people, a quarter of humanity. It was governed by a staff that would embarrass a modern city. Perhaps 150 senior officials, senators and members of the equestrian order (the wealthy class just below the Senate), administered forty-odd provinces, and the entire salaried imperial bureaucracy numbered in the low thousands.[^3] The trick was that Rome did not govern most people directly. It ruled through **cities**: about 2,000 self-governing towns whose local elites collected the taxes, kept order, maintained the roads and aqueducts, and competed to build temples and baths in the Roman manner in exchange for Roman citizenship and a place in the imperial system. The provincial elites became Romans because it paid, and their grandsons became senators and emperors. Trajan was Spanish; Septimius Severus was African; Philip the Arab was Arab.

**The army** held the frontiers: about 30 legions of 5,000 citizen infantry plus an equal number of auxiliaries recruited from the provinces, some 300,000 to 400,000 men, one of the largest standing armies of the pre-modern world, stationed on the Rhine, the Danube, in Syria, and in Britain, and almost never inside Italy.[^4] It was also the empire's engineering corps and its integrating institution: a Syrian served twenty-five years on the Danube, learned Latin, and retired a citizen. **Taxes**, a land tax and a poll tax assessed by census plus customs and sales duties, took perhaps 5 to 10 percent of the economy, most of it spent on the army.[^5] **Roads**, some 80,000 kilometers of them, paved and surveyed, carried the army, the imperial post, and trade; a letter could go from Rome to Alexandria in a fortnight. **Law** was uniform across the empire and administered by governors; a Roman citizen could appeal to the emperor, as Paul of Tarsus did. And the **Pax Romana**, the Roman peace, meant that from Augustus to Marcus Aurelius the interior of the empire saw almost no war: two centuries in which a merchant could sail from Spain to Syria and never leave one jurisdiction, and in which the Mediterranean was, for the only time in its history, an inland sea belonging to one state.

## What it was like

The empire was a slave society, an urban society, and a deeply unequal one, and all three at once.

**Slaves** were perhaps 10 to 15 percent of the population, higher in Italy; they were war captives, purchased from beyond the frontiers, or born to slave mothers, and they did everything from farming and mining to teaching, medicine, and running the imperial treasury. Roman slavery was not racial, manumission was common, and freed slaves' children were full citizens; it was also brutal, legally absolute, and questioned as an institution by almost no one whose writing survives, Stoic or Christian (Gregory of Nyssa in the fourth century is the rare exception).[^6]

**Cities** were the point. Rome itself held about a million people, a size no European city would match again until London around 1800, fed by a state grain dole to some 200,000 citizens and by eleven aqueducts delivering a volume of water per person that few modern cities match. It had apartment blocks six stories high, public baths for tens of thousands, and the Colosseum, seating 50,000 for the killing of animals and men.[^7] Pompeii, buried by Vesuvius in 79 CE and excavated since 1748, shows a provincial town of 12,000 in the middle of its life: bakeries with the bread in the ovens, taverns, brothels, election graffiti, a dog on a leash, and a wall scrawled with "Aufidius was here."

**Inequality** was extreme. The elite, perhaps 1.5 percent of the population, took about a fifth of all income, and at its apex stood a few hundred senatorial families; a senator's minimum fortune was a million sesterces, and a legionary earned 900 a year.[^8] Most people were peasants who never saw a city, lived at subsistence, and died before fifty; infant mortality was around 30 percent, and average life expectancy at birth in the mid-twenties. The Roman empire was, for most of its inhabitants, a place of hard work, early death, and taxes collected under threat, and for a small minority a civilization of a comfort not seen again in Europe for a thousand years. Both things are true and neither cancels the other.

:::story A day in 79 CE
On the morning of the eruption, in the town of Herculaneum a few miles from Pompeii, someone was reading in a villa's library. The library, some 1,800 papyrus rolls of Greek philosophy, mostly by the Epicurean Philodemus, was carbonized by the pyroclastic surge, the avalanche of hot gas and ash, that killed the town and preserved as blackened lumps that crumbled if unrolled. In 2023 a competition using X-ray scans and machine learning read the first words from inside a rolled scroll, in 2025 the title of one, Philodemus, *On Vices*, and in June 2026 an entire scroll, read from beginning to end.[^9] It is the only library of papyrus books to survive from the classical world, and the reason we have any Epicurus at all beyond fragments is that a rich Roman collected him and a volcano sealed the collection. The same eruption killed the elder Pliny, the admiral and naturalist, who sailed toward it to rescue friends, and was described by his nephew in two letters to Tacitus that are the first eyewitness account of a volcanic eruption and the reason that kind of eruption is called Plinian.[^10]
:::

## Christianity

Around 30 CE a Jewish preacher from Galilee named Jesus was executed by the Roman governor of Judea for sedition, in the reign of Tiberius, at the request of the Jerusalem priesthood, by crucifixion, the Roman punishment for slaves and rebels. His followers, who believed he had risen from the dead, were a Jewish sect for a generation. Then Paul of Tarsus, a Greek-speaking Jew and, according to Acts, a Roman citizen, who had never met Jesus, argued that the message was for everyone and that non-Jews need not keep the Jewish law, and carried it through the cities of the eastern empire in letters written in the common Greek.[^11] The four Gospels were written between about 70 and 100. By 100 there were Christian communities in most major cities; by 200 perhaps 200,000 Christians in an empire of 60 million; by 300, several million.[^12]

Why did it grow? Historians offer several answers that are not exclusive. It was a religion for the cities, where uprooted people from everywhere lacked the local cults of home. It offered a community that cared for its poor, widows, and sick, at a time when nothing else did. It was open to women, slaves, and the poor as full members. It made exclusive claims, unlike the tolerant polytheism around it, so converts did not drift back. And it offered a personal God, a moral code, and life after death, which the philosophies offered only to the educated.[^13] The empire's peace, roads, common language, and urban network were its medium; Paul traveled as a Roman citizen on Roman roads and wrote in the Greek Alexander had spread.

Rome persecuted Christians sporadically, not for their beliefs, which it did not much care about, but for refusing to sacrifice to the emperor's genius and the gods of the state, which looked like treason. Nero blamed them for the fire of 64 (chapter 1); Trajan told a governor not to hunt them but to execute those who would not recant; the real empire-wide persecutions came in the third-century crisis and under Diocletian in 303.[^14] They failed, and what happened next belongs to chapter 13.

## Law

The Romans' most durable creation was **law**, and it grew from the Republic's jurists into an imperial science. By the second and third centuries CE, professional legal scholars (Gaius, Papinian, Ulpian) were writing systematic treatises on property, contract, inheritance, family, and delict (civil wrongs); the emperor answered legal questions by rescript, a written ruling; and the principles they worked out, that a contract binds because the parties consented, that ownership is distinct from possession, that the accused is presumed innocent, that no one should be judge in his own case, became Justinian's *Digest* in 533 and, through the medieval universities, the civil law of continental Europe and its former colonies.[^15] English common law developed separately, but its vocabulary and much of its equity is Roman too. A lawyer in Buenos Aires, Paris, or Tokyo argues today in categories Ulpian defined.

:::key
The Roman empire governed a quarter of humanity for two centuries with a few thousand officials, by making the local elites of two thousand cities into Romans and letting them do the governing. Its peace was real and its costs were real: it rested on a professional army, a slave economy, and taxation of a peasantry that saw none of the marble. What it produced that lasted was not the emperors but the frame: a common law, a common language in each half (Latin west, Greek east), a road network, a citizenship anyone could join, and a new religion that grew in its cities because the cities were connected. Chapter 13 is about how the frame broke and what came through the breach.
:::

## Summary

- Augustus kept the republic's forms and took its powers; the Principate was a monarchy without a succession rule, and every dynasty fought over it.
- The empire governed 60 million people through about 2,000 self-governing cities whose elites became Romans, a 300,000-man frontier army, uniform law, and 80,000 kilometers of road; the interior saw two centuries of peace.
- It was a slave society (10–15 percent), an urban civilization (Rome held a million people), and extremely unequal; most inhabitants were peasants who died young.
- Christianity grew from a Jewish sect to several million by 300 through the empire's cities, roads, and common Greek, offering community, exclusivity, and salvation to people the old cults did not serve.
- Roman jurists made law a science; through Justinian's *Digest* it became the civil law of most of the world.
