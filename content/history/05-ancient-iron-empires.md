## How did empires govern far more people than they could know?

The Bronze Age system fell around 1150 BCE. This chapter follows the Near East through the next eight centuries, in which empires returned at a scale never seen before and, on the margins of those empires, three small peoples, Phoenicians, Israelites, and Greeks, produced the alphabet, the Bible, and the city-state. The Greeks get their own chapter next. This one is about the empires, and about the two inventions that made empires of that size possible: iron, and administration.

## Assyria: the first machine of conquest

Assyria, in northern Iraq, had been a middling power for a thousand years. From about 900 BCE it became something new. Over two centuries its kings conquered everything from the Persian Gulf to Egypt, and they did it with a professional standing army, iron weapons, cavalry, siege engineering, a road and messenger system, and a policy of **deportation**: conquered populations, by the hundreds of thousands, were marched to other parts of the empire and replaced with strangers, so that no province kept a people who could rebel together.[^1] The palace reliefs of Nineveh and Nimrud show it in stone: cities under siege, prisoners impaled, heads counted in piles, the king hunting lions. The Assyrians were not more cruel than their neighbors; they were more systematic, and they advertised it, because terror was policy.

They also collected. Ashurbanipal (reigned 668–c. 631) assembled at Nineveh a library of some 30,000 tablets, gathering every text in Mesopotamia's two-thousand-year tradition: the *Gilgamesh* epic in its standard version, omens, medical texts, dictionaries between Sumerian and Akkadian.[^2] When Nineveh was sacked in 612 BCE by a coalition of Babylonians and Medes, the library was buried under the rubble and preserved; its excavation from 1849 recovered Mesopotamian literature for the modern world. The empire that had terrorized the Near East for two centuries collapsed in less than twenty years, and the prophet Nahum's "Woe to the bloody city" was one of many celebrations.

## Babylon, briefly

The **Neo-Babylonian** empire (626–539 BCE) took Assyria's place. Nebuchadnezzar II rebuilt Babylon into the largest city in the world, with the Ishtar Gate, the ziggurat that became the Tower of Babel in the Hebrew Bible, and possibly the Hanging Gardens, if they existed and were not at Nineveh.[^3] In 587 he destroyed Jerusalem and deported its elite to Babylon, the event that shaped the Hebrew Bible more than any other. Babylonian scholars in this period recorded the positions of planets night after night with a precision that let them predict eclipses, and they did so for seven centuries; their **astronomical diaries** are the longest continuous scientific record in history, and the zodiac is theirs.[^4] In 539 the city fell without a fight to Cyrus of Persia.

## Persia: the empire that ruled by leaving alone

In fifty years, from 550 to 500 BCE, the Persians, a people from the highlands of Iran, built the largest empire the world had seen: from the Indus to the Aegean, from Egypt to Central Asia, perhaps a third of humanity. Cyrus the Great (reigned 559–530) took Media, Lydia, and Babylon; Cambyses took Egypt; Darius I (522–486) organized the whole.[^5]

The Persians ruled differently from Assyria. Cyrus entered Babylon as its liberator, restored its gods, and, the book of Ezra records, allowed the Jewish exiles to return and rebuild their temple; the Hebrew Bible calls him the Lord's anointed, the only non-Jew so named.[^6] The **Achaemenid** empire (after the dynasty's founder) was divided into about twenty provinces under governors called **satraps**, each paying a fixed tribute; local laws, languages, and religions were left in place; the imperial language was Aramaic, not Persian, because it was already the Near East's commercial tongue. A royal road with relay stations carried messages from Susa to the Aegean in a week. Darius standardized coinage, weights, and a calendar, dug a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea, and built Persepolis, whose reliefs show the empire's peoples bringing tribute in their national dress, each named, each distinct, all subordinate.[^7]

The Persian kings worshipped Ahura Mazda, the god of the prophet Zoroaster (chapter 12), and their inscriptions frame kingship as the upholding of truth against the Lie. They also called themselves king of kings and expected prostration. Greek writers, who fought them, made "Persian" a byword for despotism and luxury, and that picture dominated Western history until the twentieth century, when the Persian sources, above all the thousands of administrative tablets from Persepolis recording rations for workers including women and children, gave a different view: an empire that was bureaucratic, tolerant by the standards of the age, and durable.[^8] It lasted two centuries and fell only to Alexander (chapter 7).

:::history How we got here
For two thousand years the Persian empire was known almost entirely through its enemies. Herodotus, Xenophon, and the Bible were the sources, and they gave a Persia that was either the tyrant against which Greek freedom defined itself or the instrument of God's plan for Israel. The nineteenth-century decipherment of Old Persian cuneiform (Behistun inscription, 1830s–40s), the excavation of Persepolis (1930s), and the publication of the Persepolis Fortification tablets (from 1969) gave the Persians their own voice, and the picture shifted from despotism to what historians now call an empire of accommodation, which governed a vast diversity by demanding tribute and loyalty and little else.[^9] The lesson generalizes: when a people is known only from its enemies' books, expect the account to be revised when its own turn up.
:::

## Iron and administration

Two technologies underlie all of this. **Iron** ore is common where tin is not, and once smiths learned to work it (a harder trick than bronze, requiring higher heat and the addition of carbon to harden it), it made tools and weapons cheap. A Bronze Age army was an aristocracy in chariots; an Iron Age army could be tens of thousands of infantry with iron-tipped spears. Empires got bigger because armies did.

**Administration** kept them together. The Assyrians built the model: provinces, governors, garrisons, roads, relay messengers, a census, taxation in kind and labor, and archives in which every order and report was filed. The Persians took the model and scaled it. Ruling a large territory from one center requires knowing what is in it, moving information faster than rebellion, and paying the people who do the knowing and moving. Every later empire, Rome, the Han, the Caliphate, the Mongols, is a variation on the Assyrian-Persian template, and the template's requirements, literacy, standardized measures, and a professional class loyal to the center rather than to their home province, are the reason empires and writing go together.

## The Phoenicians and the alphabet

On the Lebanese coast, the cities of Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos, heirs of Bronze Age Canaan, survived the collapse and became the traders of the Iron Age Mediterranean, sailing to Spain for silver, to Britain (perhaps) for tin, and founding colonies from Cyprus to Cadiz, the greatest of them Carthage in Tunisia, around 814 BCE (chapter 11).[^10] The Greeks called them Phoenicians, "purple people," after the dye they sold.

Their lasting export was the **alphabet**. Cuneiform and hieroglyphs had hundreds of signs and took years to learn. Around 1800 BCE, Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt had begun using a few dozen Egyptian signs to stand for the consonant sounds of their own language, and the Phoenicians standardized this into 22 letters, all consonants.[^11] A child could learn it in weeks. The Greeks borrowed it around 800 BCE, added vowels, and produced the first alphabet in the full sense; the Etruscans passed the Greek version to Rome, whose letters you are reading, and the Aramaic version spread east to become the scripts of Hebrew, Arabic, and, through a long chain, much of India and Southeast Asia. Writing stopped being a profession and became a skill, and a great deal of what follows in this guide, Greek democracy, the Hebrew Bible as a people's book, the letters of Paul, depends on that.

## Israel and Judah

Among the small kingdoms of the Iron Age Levant, two matter out of all proportion to their size, because they produced a book. **Israel** in the north and **Judah** in the south, hill-country states that emerged around 1000 BCE, were minor players squeezed between Egypt and the Mesopotamian empires. Israel was destroyed by Assyria in 722 BCE and its people deported; Judah was destroyed by Babylon in 587, its temple burned and its elite exiled. Under Persia the exiles returned, rebuilt the temple, and completed the work of editing their traditions into the **Hebrew Bible**.[^12]

That work is the beginning of a new kind of religion, which chapter 12 takes up: a people bound to a single god by a covenant, with a written law, whose history is understood as God's judgment on their conduct. What matters here is the historian's problem it poses. The Bible is a primary source for the Iron Age Levant and also a theological work compiled centuries after most of the events it describes. Archaeology confirms the existence of the two kingdoms, the destruction of Jerusalem in 587, kings named in Assyrian records (Ahab, Jehu, Hezekiah), and a "House of David" in a ninth-century inscription; it does not confirm, and in some cases contradicts, the Exodus from Egypt, the conquest of Canaan, and the extent of Solomon's kingdom.[^13] How to weigh text against dirt is the discipline's oldest argument, and this is where it is fought hardest.

It may preserve evidence of violence, but it was also a performance of royal power meant to intimidate and legitimize. Compare its claims with archaeology and other texts. Taking propaganda seriously as evidence includes asking what its author wanted an audience to believe.

## Summary

- Assyria built the first systematic conquest state: standing army, iron, roads, deportation, terror as policy, and a library that preserved Mesopotamian literature.
- Babylon's brief empire destroyed Jerusalem and produced the astronomical diaries, the longest scientific record in history.
- Persia ruled a third of humanity for two centuries by accommodation: satraps, fixed tribute, local autonomy, Aramaic as the imperial language; its reputation for despotism is its enemies' account, revised when its own records were read.
- Iron made armies large and administration held empires together; the Assyrian-Persian template underlies every later empire.
- The Phoenicians spread the alphabet, which made literacy a skill rather than a profession; Israel and Judah, minor kingdoms crushed by the empires, produced the Hebrew Bible, the text that most tests how historians weigh documents against archaeology.
