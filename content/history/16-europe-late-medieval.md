## Recap

Chapter 15 ended in 1300 with Europe's population pressing against what its farmland could feed. This chapter is about what happened when the pressure broke: famine, the worst pandemic in recorded history, a century of Anglo-French war, peasant revolts, a papacy split in three, and, at the same time, the rebirth of learning in Italy and the invention that would make it permanent. The two centuries are called the "waning" of the Middle Ages, and they were a crisis from which the modern world was made.

## The great famine and the Black Death

The Medieval Warm Period ended around 1300. Cold, wet years followed, and in 1315–1317 the harvest failed across northern Europe two years running; perhaps 10 percent of the population died and the rest were weakened.[^1] Then came the plague.

The **Black Death** arrived at Messina in Sicily in October 1347 on Genoese ships from the Black Sea, where the Mongol trade routes (chapter 30) had carried it from Central Asia; ancient DNA has traced its origin to the Tian Shan mountains of Kyrgyzstan around 1338.[^2] It was *Yersinia pestis*, bubonic plague, spread by fleas on rats and, in its pneumonic form, from person to person, and it killed most of those infected within days. In four years it crossed all of Europe from Sicily to Norway. The death toll is estimated at between a third and a half of the population, perhaps 25 million people in Europe alone, though the regional variation was enormous and pollen evidence suggests some regions, Poland, parts of Iberia, were less affected than the chronicles imply.[^3] Florence lost perhaps 60 percent; Boccaccio, who was there, described the dead left in the streets and families abandoning each other. The plague came back in 1361, 1369, 1374, and roughly every decade thereafter for three centuries; Europe's population did not recover its 1300 level until about 1550.

Nobody knew what caused it. The medical faculty of Paris blamed a conjunction of planets; the Church called for penance; the flagellants whipped themselves through the towns; and across Germany, Switzerland, and France Jews were accused of poisoning wells and massacred, whole communities burned, in the worst persecution before the twentieth century.[^4]

The consequences of losing a third of the population were, for the survivors, mixed. Labor became scarce and dear, land cheap; wages rose, rents fell, and serfdom, already weakening, collapsed in Western Europe because lords could not hold peasants who could walk to a better offer.[^5] Governments tried to freeze wages by law (England's Statute of Labourers, 1351) and provoked revolts: the French Jacquerie (1358), the English Peasants' Revolt (1381), which took London and killed the archbishop before it was crushed. The survivors ate more meat, wore better clothes, and had a bargaining power their grandparents had never dreamed of, and the culture that followed, with its dance of death, its memento mori, and its Flemish paintings of prosperous burghers in fur, is that of people who had watched the world end and then found themselves comfortably off.

:::frontier
The Black Death is where the new sciences of the past have done the most in the last decade. Ancient DNA from plague pits confirmed the pathogen in 2011, ending a century of debate about whether it was really bubonic plague; a 2022 study of cemeteries in Kyrgyzstan dated to 1338–1339 found the ancestral strain and located the origin; pollen cores from 261 sites across Europe showed that the abandonment of farmland, a proxy for mortality, varied enormously by region, so the "one in three everywhere" of the textbooks is too uniform.[^6] A 2022 claim that the plague left a detectable mark on European immune genes, selecting for variants that protect against plague and predispose to autoimmune disease, was challenged in 2025 on statistical grounds and is unresolved.[^7] The pandemic that most shaped Europe's demography is, seven centuries on, still being measured.
:::

## The Hundred Years' War

England and France fought, on and off, from 1337 to 1453. The causes were a disputed succession (Edward III of England claimed the French crown through his mother), the English king's lands in Gascony, and the wool trade with Flanders. The English won the famous battles, Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356), Agincourt (1415), with longbowmen who could shoot twelve arrows a minute through the French knights' charges, and Henry V was recognized as heir to France in 1420. Then a peasant girl from Lorraine named Joan, hearing voices, persuaded the French dauphin to let her lead his army, broke the siege of Orléans in 1429, saw him crowned at Reims, and was captured, tried by a Church court under English control, and burned at Rouen in 1431, aged nineteen. The French, with artillery, retook everything but Calais by 1453.[^8]

The war's effects outlasted its battles. It made England and France into nations, with national taxes, national armies, and, in each, a hatred of the other that became identity. It ended the dominance of the mounted knight, first by the longbow and then, decisively, by gunpowder, which reached Europe from China via the Mongols around 1320 and by 1450 could knock down any castle. It bankrupted Italian banks (the Bardi and Peruzzi of Florence failed in the 1340s when Edward III defaulted), and it gave France a standing army and a king, Louis XI, who by 1483 had made France the most powerful monarchy in Europe. In England, the returning nobles fought the Wars of the Roses (1455–1487) until Henry Tudor won at Bosworth in 1485.

## The Church in crisis

The papacy that had judged kings in 1200 was, in 1300, judged by them. Philip IV of France had Pope Boniface VIII kidnapped in 1303; from 1309 the popes lived at Avignon under French influence for seventy years. When they returned to Rome in 1377, a disputed election produced two popes, then three, each excommunicating the others, for forty years (the **Great Schism**, 1378–1417), until a council at Constance deposed all three and elected a fourth.[^9] Constance also burned Jan Hus, a Czech reformer who had argued that the Bible outranked the pope and that priests should be poor; his followers fought off five crusades in Bohemia. John Wycliffe in England had said much the same and translated the Bible into English; his followers, the Lollards, were hunted for a century. The Church's wealth, its taxation, its sale of offices and indulgences (remissions of punishment for sin, bought for cash), and its failure to reform itself were the common complaint of the age, and the Reformation of chapter 17 was a century in preparation.

## The Renaissance

While the north fought and the Church quarreled, the Italian cities, rich from trade and never fully feudal, produced a cultural movement whose participants believed they were reviving antiquity and whose historians named it the **Renaissance**, rebirth. Its core was **humanism**: the recovery, editing, and imitation of classical Latin and then Greek texts, and the conviction that the study of them (the *studia humanitatis*: grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry, moral philosophy) made better human beings and better citizens than scholastic logic did.[^10] Petrarch (1304–1374) hunted manuscripts and wrote letters to Cicero; Boccaccio wrote the *Decameron*; Lorenzo Valla proved the "Donation of Constantine," the document on which papal claims to temporal power rested, a forgery. Greek scholars fleeing the Turks brought Plato. The Medici bank paid for it.

In art, Giotto (c. 1300) painted figures with weight and feeling; Brunelleschi worked out linear perspective around 1415 and built the dome of Florence cathedral without scaffolding; Donatello made the first free-standing nude since antiquity; Masaccio, Botticelli, Leonardo, and, at the century's end, Michelangelo and Raphael made Florence and then Rome the capital of European art.[^11] The artists were craftsmen becoming celebrities, and their patrons were bankers, popes, and despots displaying magnificence. Politics was the Italian specialty too: the city-states, Florence, Venice, Milan, the Papal States, Naples, fought with mercenaries, negotiated through resident ambassadors (an Italian invention of the 1450s), and produced, in 1513, Machiavelli's *Prince*, the first work of political analysis to describe how power actually works without pretending it ought to work otherwise.

:::history How we got here
The Renaissance named itself. Petrarch called the centuries between Rome and himself dark; Vasari in 1550 wrote of art's rebirth; and in 1860 Jacob Burckhardt made the Italian Renaissance the birth of the modern individual and the modern state, a clean break with the medieval. For a century that was the story, and it made sense: the humanists said so, and the art speaks for itself. Then medievalists pointed out that the twelfth century had its own renaissance, that scholasticism was more rigorous than humanism, that the humanists were mostly civil servants writing for princes, and that the Renaissance was, statistically, a small elite in a few Italian cities while most of Europe went on as before. Feminist historians asked whether women had a Renaissance and answered no. The current view keeps the term for the cultural movement while denying it was a break: the Renaissance was a medieval phenomenon, one of several revivals of antiquity, distinguished by its self-consciousness, its art, and its timing, which put it in a position to be spread by print.[^12]
:::

## Print

Around 1450 in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith, combined existing technologies, the screw press, oil-based ink, paper (a Chinese invention that had reached Europe via the Arabs), and his own contribution, a mold for casting identical metal letters in quantity, into **movable-type printing**. His Bible of 1455 was the first major book printed in Europe.[^13] By 1500 there were presses in some 250 towns and perhaps 20 million books in print, more than all the manuscripts copied in the previous thousand years. A book that had cost a year's wages cost a week's. Within the next generation print would make the Reformation possible (Luther's pamphlets sold in the hundreds of thousands), standardize the vernacular languages, spread the humanists' texts across Europe, and create the reading public. It is the most consequential invention of the millennium, and it was made by a businessman who died in debt.

## 1492

In the year the last Muslim kingdom in Spain surrendered and its Jews were expelled, a Genoese sailor in the service of Spain reached the Caribbean. The world of the next chapter is about to open, and the two centuries of crisis had made Europe ready for it: nation-states with taxes and artillery, a commercial class with capital and double-entry books, a culture that had rediscovered geography and geometry, a press to publish the news, and a population that, having recovered from the plague, was about to grow again and needed somewhere to go.

:::key
The late Middle Ages show what a catastrophe does. The plague killed a third of Europe and, for those who lived, ended serfdom, raised wages, and broke the deference of centuries. War made nations. The Church's failure made the Reformation. The Italian cities' wealth made the Renaissance, and a goldsmith's mold made it permanent. None of it was planned and most of it was experienced as disaster. The transition from medieval to modern, which the humanists claimed as their own achievement, was mostly the work of bacteria, longbows, gunpowder, and lead type.
:::

## Summary

- Climate turned around 1300, famine struck in 1315, and the Black Death of 1347–1351 killed a third to a half of Europe; regional variation was large and is now measurable by pollen and DNA.
- The survivors gained wages and freedom; serfdom collapsed in the West, and attempts to reverse this provoked the great peasant revolts.
- The Hundred Years' War made England and France nations, ended the knight's dominance through the longbow and gunpowder, and bankrupted Italian banks.
- The papacy fell under French control, split three ways, and burned its reformers, preparing the Reformation.
- The Italian Renaissance revived antiquity in scholarship, art, and political thought; Gutenberg's press, around 1450, made it permanent and created the reading public.
