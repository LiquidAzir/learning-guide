## What changed when Greek culture spread through kingdoms?

Chapter 6 ended with Philip II of Macedon master of Greece in 338 BCE. Two years later he was assassinated at his daughter's wedding, and his son Alexander, aged twenty, took the throne. This chapter follows what Alexander did and, more importantly, what happened after, because the world his generals carved up was the world in which Rome grew, Judaism was rewritten, Buddhism reached the Greeks, and science first became a profession.

## The conquest

Macedon was a kingdom on Greece's northern edge, Greek-speaking but ruled by kings, considered by Athenians to be barely civilized. Philip had made its army the best in the world: a phalanx armed with eighteen-foot pikes, heavy cavalry, siege engineers, and the discipline of a professional force paid year-round.[^1] Alexander inherited it, along with his father's plan to invade Persia, in 336. He crossed into Asia in 334 with about 40,000 men, defeated a Persian provincial army at the Granicus, defeated the Persian king Darius III himself at Issus in 333 and again at Gaugamela in 331, took Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis (which he burned), pursued Darius until the king's own officers murdered him, and then kept going: into Central Asia for three years of guerrilla war, over the Hindu Kush into the Punjab in 326, where his army, after a hard-won battle against King Porus and his elephants, refused to march further east. He turned back, lost a large part of his army crossing the Gedrosian desert, reached Babylon, and died there in June 323, aged thirty-two, of fever, drink, poison, or grief for his friend Hephaestion, depending on the source.[^2]

Every history of Alexander is a history of the sources problem. He had official historians, and none of their work survives except in fragments. The five accounts we have were written between three and five centuries after his death, drawing on lost originals of very different reliability.[^3] The Alexander of Arrian is a great general and a fair-minded king; the Alexander of the Roman-era moralists is a young man ruined by success; the Alexander of medieval romance rode to the bottom of the sea in a glass barrel. What is beyond dispute: he was never defeated in battle, he founded some twenty cities named after himself, he adopted Persian dress and court ceremony and married two Persian princesses, he killed several close friends in fits of rage or suspicion, and he changed the map of the world.

## The successors

He left a pregnant wife, a mentally impaired half-brother, and no plan. His generals fought over the empire for forty years, killed his son, his mother, his wives, and each other, and by about 280 BCE had settled into three great kingdoms and several lesser ones.[^4] The **Ptolemies** ruled Egypt from Alexandria for nearly three centuries, ending with Cleopatra VII in 30 BCE. The **Seleucids** held the Asian core, from Syria to Iran, and lost it piece by piece, Iran to the Parthians in the second century and the rest to Rome. The **Antigonids** ruled Macedon and quarreled with the Greek cities until Rome ended them in 168. Historians call the whole era, from Alexander's death to Cleopatra's, **Hellenistic**, "Greek-ish," because its ruling class was Greek and Macedonian and its subjects mostly were not.

The kingdoms were something new: monarchies on a scale no Greek had governed, ruling Egyptians, Babylonians, Persians, and Jews through Greek officials in Greek-speaking cities founded for the purpose. The kings were worshipped as gods, taxed heavily, fought each other constantly with mercenary armies, and financed the most spectacular culture the ancient world had seen. Under them, Greek became the common language, the *koine*, from the Adriatic to the Indus, and stayed so under Rome; the New Testament is written in it.

## Alexandria

Ptolemy I founded the **Library of Alexandria** and its research institute, the Museum, around 300 BCE, and for the next two centuries most of what the ancient world knew was worked out there, by scholars on the royal payroll.[^5] Euclid wrote the *Elements* in Alexandria around 300, and it was the textbook of geometry until the twentieth century ([chapter 5 of the mathematics guide](#/mathematics/geometry)). Eratosthenes, the librarian, measured the circumference of the Earth around 240 BCE, from the angle of the noon sun at two points a known distance apart, and came remarkably close (how close depends on the length of his stadion, which is not known). Aristarchus argued that the Earth went round the Sun. Herophilus dissected human bodies and distinguished nerves from tendons and the brain from the heart as the seat of thought. Archimedes, who studied at Alexandria and worked at Syracuse, computed pi, found the law of the lever and of buoyancy, invented war machines, and was killed by a Roman soldier in 212 while, in the story, drawing a diagram in the sand.[^6] Hellenistic astronomers built the mathematical models of planetary motion that Ptolemy of Alexandria would codify in the second century CE and that Copernicus would still be arguing with in 1543.

This was **science as a profession**: salaried researchers, a library that tried to collect every book (the Ptolemies confiscated the scrolls from every ship in the harbor and returned copies), critical editions of Homer, and an audience of other specialists rather than the general public. It was also science without much application. Hero of Alexandria built a steam engine as a toy. The gap between Hellenistic theory and Hellenistic technology is one of the puzzles of ancient history, and the standard explanation, slaves made labor-saving pointless, is probably too simple.[^7]

:::story The Rosetta Stone
In 196 BCE the priests of Egypt issued a decree honoring the boy-king Ptolemy V and had it carved in three scripts: hieroglyphic, for the gods; demotic, the everyday Egyptian script; and Greek, for the rulers. It is a routine document about tax remissions and temple privileges. A fragment of one copy was found by French soldiers building a fort at Rashid (Rosetta) in 1799, taken by the British in 1801, and became the key to reading Egyptian, when Jean-François Champollion, working from the Greek and from his knowledge of Coptic (the late form of Egyptian still used in church), announced the decipherment in 1822.[^8] Everything in chapter 3 about Egypt from Egypt's own point of view depends on that stone, and the stone exists because a Greek dynasty needed to be understood by its Egyptian subjects and by the gods at once.
:::

## Greeks and others

The Hellenistic world was one of contact, and contact went both ways.

In Egypt, the Ptolemies ruled as pharaohs to the Egyptians and Greek kings to the Greeks, built Egyptian temples in Egyptian style, and ran a bureaucracy of astonishing thoroughness whose papyrus records, preserved in the dry sand, give the most detailed picture of any ancient economy: land surveys, tax receipts, contracts, petitions from peasants, a census.[^9] Ordinary Egyptians and Greeks lived in the same towns and mostly did not intermarry for the first century; by the end they did.

In **Judea**, Greek rule provoked one of the first conflicts fought explicitly over religious practice. The Seleucid king Antiochus IV, around 167 BCE, banned Jewish practice and installed a pagan altar in the Jerusalem temple, for reasons still argued (a tax revolt? a faction of hellenizing Jews?). The Maccabean revolt won Jewish independence for a century and left the festival of Hanukkah, the books of the Maccabees, and a permanent tension in Judaism between accommodation and resistance to the dominant culture.[^10] At the same time, in Alexandria, Jews translated their scriptures into Greek, the **Septuagint**, and Jewish thinkers began to describe their God in the language of Greek philosophy. Christianity would grow from that meeting.

In the east, the Greek kingdom of Bactria (Afghanistan) lasted two centuries and minted coins with Greek gods on one side and Indian ones on the other. The Mauryan emperor Ashoka (chapter 10) set up inscriptions in Greek for his Greek subjects and sent Buddhist missionaries to the Hellenistic kings; the Indo-Greek king Menander appears in a Buddhist text as a philosopher-king who converts.[^11] Greek sculptors, or Indians trained by them, made some of the first statues of the Buddha in the region of Gandhara, at about the same time as an independent Indian tradition at Mathura, and the draped robe he wears in many later images is a Greek garment.

## Philosophy for a bigger world

The polis had been small enough that a citizen's life and the city's were one. The Hellenistic kingdoms were vast and ruled by gods on thrones, and the philosophies that flourished were for individuals who could not control their world. **Epicurus** taught that the gods do not care, the universe is atoms, death is nothing to us, and the good life is modest pleasure and friendship in a garden. **Zeno of Citium**, teaching in the painted porch (*stoa*) at Athens, taught the opposite in one way and the same in another: the universe is rational and providential, virtue is the only good, everything else, wealth, health, reputation, even life, is indifferent, and the wise man is unshaken by fortune. **Stoicism** became the philosophy of the Roman ruling class and, in the writings of Seneca, Epictetus (a freed slave), and the emperor Marcus Aurelius, the most read ancient ethics today.[^12] The Cynics rejected society altogether; the Skeptics rejected knowledge. All of them asked the same question: how does one person live well in a world too large to change?

:::key
Alexander's empire lasted thirteen years and his world lasted three hundred. The Hellenistic age took the Greek language, the Greek city, and Greek learning and spread them over three continents, and in doing so it changed all three: the language became a common tongue, the city a royal instrument, and learning a profession. The Judaism that met Greek philosophy in Alexandria, the Buddhism that met Greek art in Gandhara, and the Rome that met Greek culture in the conquered East were all transformed by the meeting. Rome is the next chapter, but it should be understood as a Hellenistic power that happened to speak Latin.
:::

Greek inscriptions appear far from Greece after Alexander. Does that show the local population abandoned its earlier culture?

:::answer One way to reason it through
No. They show the use of Greek in those contexts, perhaps in government, trade, or elite display. Local languages, practices, and identities could persist and mix with new ones. Look for whose activity left the inscription and compare evidence from other parts of society.

## Summary

- Alexander conquered the Persian empire in a decade (334–323 BCE) with the army Philip built, and died without a plan; his generals divided the world into the Ptolemaic, Seleucid, and Antigonid kingdoms.
- The Hellenistic kingdoms were god-kings ruling non-Greek populations through Greek cities; Greek became the common language from the Adriatic to the Indus.
- Alexandria made science a salaried profession: Euclid, Eratosthenes, Archimedes, and the astronomers who set the terms for Copernicus.
- Contact ran both ways: Greek and Egyptian government fused, Judaism both resisted (the Maccabees) and absorbed (the Septuagint) Greek culture, and Greek art gave the Buddha his image.
- Stoicism and Epicureanism answered the question of how to live in a world too big to control, and Stoicism became Rome's philosophy.
