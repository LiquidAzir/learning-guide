## How do you find out which development policies work?

Chapter 10 asked why growth started and where it spread. **Development economics** asks the question from the other side: for the billion people still living in deep poverty, what works? It is the field where economics has been most humbled by evidence and where, since about 2000, it has changed its methods most completely.

## The scale of the problem, and the progress

The World Bank counts a person as extremely poor if they live on less than about \$3 a day, adjusted for local prices.[^1] By that standard about 840 million people were poor in 2022, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, roughly one person in ten. That number is a catastrophe and also a triumph: measured on the Bank's older, lower line of \$2.15 a day, the share of humanity in extreme poverty fell from 38 percent in 1990 (nearly two billion people) to under 9 percent in 2022.[^2] Nothing like that fall had happened before in human history. Almost all of it came from growth in Asia, above all China, and the mechanisms were those of chapter 10: investment, education, the movement of workers from farms to factories, and openness to trade.

That success is also the field's puzzle. Growth is what ends poverty, and economists know a great deal about what growth needs. What they have not found is a reliable way to make it start in a country that lacks the institutions to sustain it. The debates of the last seventy years are about what to do in the meantime.

## The big-push era and its lessons

After 1945 the dominant view was that poor countries were stuck in a trap. Individual investments did not pay because the roads, power, and skilled workers they needed were missing, and those were missing because nobody invested. Paul Rosenstein-Rodan's **big push** theory said a coordinated wave of investment, financed by foreign aid, could break the trap all at once.[^3] The idea had a distinguished modern revival in Jeffrey Sachs's argument that a doubling of aid could end extreme poverty by 2025.[^4]

The record was mixed to poor. Aid financed some clear successes, notably the eradication of smallpox, the near-elimination of polio, and the health programs behind a halving of child mortality since 1990. It also financed dams that displaced villages, factories that never ran, and governments that stayed in power by distributing it. William Easterly's assessment, after decades inside the aid system, was that the money had often bought "the white man's burden" rather than development, because planners in donor capitals could not know what worked in villages they had never seen.[^5] Cross-country studies found no reliable relationship between aid received and growth achieved.[^6] The disagreement between Sachs and Easterly framed a decade of argument that neither side could win, because the evidence was correlational and countries differed in a thousand ways.

## The randomized turn

The breakthrough was methodological. In the late 1990s a group of economists, led by Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo at MIT and Michael Kremer at Harvard, began doing to development programs what medicine had done to drugs: **randomized controlled trials**, assigning a program to some villages or schools by lottery and comparing outcomes with those that did not get it. Random assignment makes the groups comparable on average, helping isolate the program’s effect. Chance differences remain, so the estimate needs uncertainty bounds; attrition, spillovers, and implementation can also complicate the comparison. Chapter 16 has the method; this chapter has what it found. The three received the 2019 Nobel Prize.

The findings were often small, specific, and surprising, and they overturned intuitions on every side.

**Deworming works.** Kremer and Edward Miguel found in Kenya that a pill costing under a dollar, given to schoolchildren for intestinal worms, cut absenteeism by a quarter; children treated had higher earnings twenty years later.[^7] The finding has been fought over: a 2015 reanalysis found the spillover effects on untreated children smaller than first claimed, and medical reviews that exclude the long-run studies find little effect on average, so deworming is also the field's best case study in how a result is contested.[^15] Free textbooks, which seemed an obvious win, raised scores only for the strongest students in the same setting, because most pupils could not read them; school meals raised attendance but improved learning only where teachers were experienced.[^16]

**Microfinance mostly does not.** Small loans to poor entrepreneurs, celebrated with a Nobel Peace Prize in 2006, were subjected to six randomized trials across four continents. They found modest increases in business activity and no detectable effect on income, consumption, or poverty.[^8] The loans helped some people and were not the transformation promised.

**Cash works better than expected.** Giving poor households money with no strings attached, long assumed to be wasted on alcohol and idleness, was tested at scale in Kenya. Recipients bought livestock, roofs, and food, worked no less, and spent no more on temptation goods; a study of 653 villages found that a large one-time transfer raised consumption not only for recipients but for their neighbors, as local businesses sold more, with a local multiplier of about 2.5 and almost no inflation.[^9] A twelve-year experiment testing a universal basic income in Kenyan villages reports, in its early results, more businesses and higher revenues with no fall in work, and finds a lump sum outperforms a monthly stream of the same value.[^10]

**Incentives and information often beat infrastructure.** Small incentives raised vaccination rates more than better clinics did. Telling parents the actual returns to schooling raised attendance. Hiring extra teachers on short contracts improved test scores where hiring civil-service teachers did not.

:::key
The randomized revolution changed what counts as knowing something in development. Before it, the field ran on grand theories and country-level correlations that could not separate cause from coincidence. After it, a claim that a program works means a lottery was run and the treated group did better. The trials cannot answer every question, since you cannot randomize a country's institutions or its trade policy, but on the questions they can answer they have replaced argument with evidence, and the evidence has been humbling for everyone who thought they already knew.
:::

## Institutions, again

The trials tell you which programs work given a country's institutions. They do not tell you how to change the institutions, which chapter 10 argued is the deep determinant. The two literatures have had an uneasy relationship, and the most productive synthesis is that they answer different questions on different time scales: institutions set the ceiling on what a country can achieve, and well-tested programs determine how close to that ceiling its poor get to live in the meantime.

Two institutional findings recur. The **resource curse**: countries rich in oil or minerals often grow more slowly than those without, because the revenue funds rulers who need not tax or answer to citizens, and because a booming resource sector crowds out manufacturing.[^11] Norway and Botswana escaped it through unusually strong institutions before the discoveries; Nigeria, Venezuela, and the Democratic Republic of Congo did not. And **state capacity**: the ability of a government to collect taxes, enforce contracts, and deliver services is itself a form of wealth that poor countries lack and that aid has rarely built.

## Health, population, and human capital

Development is not only income. Since 1990 global child mortality has halved and life expectancy in low-income countries has risen by more than a decade, driven by vaccines, oral rehydration, insecticide-treated bed nets against malaria, and antiretroviral drugs for HIV.[^12] These are among the highest-return investments ever made, and many of them were funded by aid, which is the strongest reply to Easterly. Famine, the oldest face of poverty, turned out to be less about food than about rights: Amartya Sen showed in 1981 that the great famines of the twentieth century, including Bengal in 1943, struck when food was available but the poor lost the income or entitlements to buy it, and that no famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press.[^17]

Health feeds growth through **human capital**. Healthier children learn more; educated women have fewer children and invest more in each; and the resulting **demographic transition**, from high birth and death rates to low ones, produces a generation-long window in which a country has many workers and few dependents. East Asia rode that window in the 1970s and 1980s; sub-Saharan Africa, whose fertility is falling more slowly, is entering it now.

## Migration

On the estimates economists have made, the largest gains from any development policy would come from letting people move. A worker who moves from Haiti to the United States sees their earnings rise several-fold overnight, with the same skills, because they are now combined with rich-country capital and institutions.[^13] The estimates Michael Clemens surveyed suggest that removing all barriers to migration would raise world output by half or more, dwarfing the gains from removing all remaining trade barriers, which is why he called migration restrictions "trillion-dollar bills on the sidewalk." Migration is also the development policy rich-country voters resist most, and the evidence on its effects on native workers, though mostly reassuring, is contested (chapter 8 has the Mariel boatlift fight). A 2026 study using the US visa lottery for seasonal workers found that firms allowed more foreign workers produced and invested more and hired no fewer Americans.[^14]

## Where the field is now

Extreme poverty is now concentrated where growth has been weakest: fragile states, conflict zones, and much of sub-Saharan Africa, whose population will double by 2050. Progress against it stalled during the pandemic and has resumed slowly.[^2] Climate change hits the poorest hardest, through crop failure and heat, and the World Bank now treats poverty reduction and climate resilience as one problem. The randomized-trial toolkit has spread from development into rich-country policy, where it is testing everything from job training to tax compliance letters.

:::frontier
The open fights are about scale and about growth. Do the interventions that work in a trial of a hundred villages work when a government rolls them out to a nation, where the implementers are less careful and the politics intrude? Early evidence says effects shrink but often survive. And can anything be done deliberately to start growth where it has not started? The industrial-policy literature of chapter 13 offers the most hopeful recent answer; the record of the last seventy years offers the strongest caution.
:::

A randomized program raises average income in one district. Why should a government still investigate before applying it nationwide?

The estimate has uncertainty, and the larger program may face different participants, prices, staff capacity, or spillovers. A causal effect in the trial's setting does not automatically identify the effect at national scale. Ask what mechanism produced the gain and whether the new setting preserves it.

## Summary

- Extreme poverty, on the \$2.15-a-day line, fell from 38 percent of humanity in 1990 to under 9 percent, almost entirely through growth in Asia; about one person in ten still lives below the current \$3 line.
- Big-push aid had a mixed record; the Sachs–Easterly debate could not be settled with correlational evidence.
- Randomized trials (Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer) rebuilt the field on evidence: deworming and cash transfers work better than expected, microfinance worse, and small incentives often beat infrastructure.
- Institutions set the ceiling; programs determine life beneath it. The resource curse and weak state capacity are recurring traps.
- Health gains since 1990 are among the highest-return investments ever made and were largely aid-funded.
- On current estimates, migration would deliver the largest gains of any policy; it faces the strongest resistance.
