## The whole subject in one sentence

Economics is the study of how people choose when they cannot have everything, and what happens when millions of those choices meet.

The first half of that sentence is about **scarcity**: there is never enough time, money, land, or attention to do everything, so every choice gives something up. The second half is about **coordination**: nobody plans the world economy, yet bread appears in shops, wages get paid, and a phone assembled from parts made in forty countries arrives at your door. How eight billion people with different wants and private information manage that, and how badly it can go wrong, is the subject.

:::key
Economics is not the study of money. Money is one of its topics, the way telescopes are one of astronomy's. The subject is *choice under scarcity*, which is why economists have written about marriage, crime, sleep, climate, and the design of kidney-exchange programs. Anywhere people trade off one thing against another, the tools apply.
:::

## Why economics is hard

Physicists study electrons, and electrons do not read physics papers. Economists study people, who do, and who change their behavior when they learn what the model predicts. A theory that says a stock is underpriced makes people buy it until it is not. A policy that works because nobody expects it stops working once everyone does. This is one reason economic forecasts are humbler than physical ones, and it is built into the subject rather than being a failure of it.

Three other things make it hard. Economists usually cannot run controlled experiments on whole economies: there is one Great Depression, one 2008, and no control group. The things being measured, output, inflation, wellbeing, are themselves contested constructions. And the subject touches politics at every point, so its findings get recruited into arguments they may not support.

Given all that, the surprise is how much economics does know. It knows why prices coordinate better than planners; why some nations became rich and most stayed poor for millennia; why printing money causes inflation and roughly how much; why banks are fragile and how to make them less so; why trade makes countries richer on average and specific groups poorer; and, since about 1990, how to test claims like these against evidence rather than argument. This guide is about that knowledge, and equally about its edges.

## How economics makes progress

Economics moves by building **models**: deliberately simplified pictures of a situation that keep what matters and drop what does not. A model of supply and demand ignores almost everything about a market and still predicts what happens to the price of coffee after a frost in Brazil. The skill is knowing what to leave out. A model is a map, and a map that showed every pebble would be useless.

Models get overturned less often than they get bounded. Adam Smith's 1776 insight that self-interest, disciplined by competition, can serve the public good is still true; the twentieth century added a precise list of the conditions under which it fails (chapter 5). The classical belief that markets always clear was not so much refuted by the Great Depression as confined to the long run (chapter 12). Like the physics guide, this one presents each superseded idea with the reason intelligent people believed it, because most of them were right about something.

Since the 1990s the field has changed character. What its practitioners call the **credibility revolution** replaced arguments from theory with evidence from natural experiments, randomized trials, and enormous administrative datasets. Many long-standing beliefs survived the test; several did not. Chapter 16 explains the methods, and every applied chapter leans on them.

:::warning
"Economists disagree about everything" is a common claim and mostly false. Surveys of professional economists find overwhelming agreement on many questions: that rent control reduces housing quality and quantity, that tariffs make a country poorer on average, that carbon pricing is the cheapest way to cut emissions, that the 2009 stimulus reduced unemployment, that immigration has little effect on the average native worker's wage.[^1] The genuine disagreements, about the size of fiscal multipliers (how much total spending rises per dollar of government spending), the causes of inequality, the right inflation target, are real and this guide marks them. But the loud disagreements you hear are usually about values, not evidence, and economists have no more authority on values than anyone else.
:::

## The map of the territory

The subject splits into two halves that grew up separately and were stitched together in the twentieth century.

| Half | Question | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| **Microeconomics** | How do individuals, firms, and markets behave, and when do markets fail? | 4 to 8 |
| **Macroeconomics** | How does the whole economy behave: growth, unemployment, inflation, crises? | 9 to 12 |

Across both run three further fields. **International economics** (chapter 13) asks what changes when choices cross borders. **Development economics** (chapter 14) asks why some countries are poor and what works to change that. **Finance** (chapter 15) asks how societies move resources across time and handle risk. Underneath everything sits **econometrics**, the statistics of telling cause from correlation (chapter 16), and alongside it **behavioral economics** (chapter 6), which asks what happens when the people in the models act like actual people.

The chapters are ordered to build. Part II tells the story from Aristotle to the Marginal Revolution, then explains how markets work and where they break. Part III turns to people: how they choose, how they interact strategically, and how firms and labor markets sort them into winners and losers. Part IV assembles the whole economy. Part V takes it global. Part VI walks the edge.

## The scales economics covers

| Scale | Unit | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| One decision | A person choosing between two jobs | Does the higher salary cover the longer commute? |
| One market | The market for used cars in a city | Why do good used cars sell for less than they are worth? |
| One firm | Amazon, a corner shop | Why does this exist rather than a web of contracts? |
| One country | The United States, about \$29 trillion a year[^4] | Why is inflation 3% and not 2%? |
| The world | About \$110 trillion a year[^4] | Why did extreme poverty fall from 38% of humanity in 1990 to under 10%?[^2] |

The same tools apply at every scale, which is the subject's great economy of thought and, some critics argue, its great weakness: what is true of one household is not always true of a nation, a trap the next chapter names and chapter 12 spends much of its length avoiding.

## Who does economics, and how

Academic economists build theory and test it with data. Most of the profession works elsewhere: in central banks setting interest rates, finance ministries designing taxes, international agencies fighting poverty, companies pricing products, and courts assessing whether a merger will raise prices. Economics is unusual among social sciences in having a direct line to power. Most chairs of the Federal Reserve have been economists; so, often, is the head of the World Bank. That line runs both ways, and chapter 16 discusses what it has cost.

The field has also become empirical to a degree that would surprise its founders. A typical paper in a top journal today uses millions of tax records or a randomized trial in a hundred villages. The 2019 and 2021 Nobel Prizes went to methods for learning cause and effect from such data (tax, payroll, and benefit records collected by governments for other purposes are called **administrative data**), and the 2024 prize went to a finding, that institutions cause prosperity, that rested on them.[^3] The people chapter (17) names the individuals; keep in mind that modern findings are increasingly made by teams with access to data nobody had before.

:::howto A useful first pass
Begin with the graph and percentage tools in chapter 2, then read markets in chapter 4. Keep asking who makes the choice, what alternatives they have, and who bears the consequences.

At the end of a core chapter, try the question before opening “Show the reasoning” or “One way to reason it through.” Explain your answer in a sentence or work the calculation; then compare the reasoning, not just the result. Reading-time estimates exclude time spent practicing.
:::

## How to read this guide

The conventions match the physics and chemistry guides, and you need neither to start here.

- **Bold terms** are being defined where they appear. The glossary (chapter 18) collects them.
- Colored boxes mark six kinds of interruption: a **key idea** you should not skim, a **story** from the history, a **math box** that shows an equation and explains every symbol, a **think about it** prompt, a **common confusion** to avoid, and a **where it stands today** note on what is unsettled.
- Key factual claims point to a numbered source at the bottom of the chapter, with a link where the source is free to read.
- A separate **Latest research** section, reachable from the subject's front page, tracks recent results with a plain-English summary and an honest status label.
- The math is real and limited. Economics uses algebra, a little calculus (rates of change), and statistics. When an equation appears, it will be read aloud as a sentence with every symbol named.

:::try
Before reading on, write down three things you believe about the economy. (Examples: immigrants lower wages. A trade deficit means a country is losing. Raising the minimum wage costs jobs. Rich countries got rich by exploiting poor ones.) Each is a real claim with real evidence for and against, and each appears in this guide. See which survive.
:::

Read in order if you can. And carry one habit from the start: when you hear an economic claim, ask *compared to what?* Every cost is a cost relative to the alternative, and half of economics is just insisting that the alternative be named.
