## Why can equally productive workers earn different wages?

Markets coordinate through prices, but most economic activity happens *inside* organizations where prices are not used at all: nobody in a company bids for the use of the photocopier. And most people's income comes from one market, the market for their labor, whose behavior has generated the loudest arguments in the field. This chapter covers both, and then turns to the question they feed into: how unequal is the result, and why.

## Why do firms exist?

If markets are so good at coordination, why is so much of the economy organized as firms, islands of command in a sea of prices? (The image is Dennis Robertson's, from 1923.) Ronald Coase asked the question in 1937 and gave the answer that still stands: using the market has costs.[^1] Finding a supplier, negotiating a contract, checking the work, and going to court when it fails all take resources. These **transaction costs** can exceed the cost of simply hiring someone and telling them what to do. A firm exists wherever coordinating by command is cheaper than coordinating by contract, and it stops growing where the reverse becomes true: large organizations accumulate their own costs of bureaucracy and misaligned incentives.

Oliver Williamson filled in when command wins.[^2] When two parties must make investments specific to their relationship, a supplier who builds a factory next to one customer, they become vulnerable to each other's opportunism, and bringing the activity inside one firm removes the temptation. When quality is hard to measure in a contract, a manager who can observe effort directly beats a contract that cannot. Both Coase and Williamson won Nobel Prizes for explaining an institution economists had previously treated as a black box.

Inside the firm the problem does not vanish; it changes shape. Owners want profit; managers want salary, quiet, and empire; workers want pay and leisure. The **principal–agent problem** is the general name for one party (the principal) trying to get another (the agent) to act in the principal's interest when the agent knows more and cannot be fully monitored. Stock options for executives, piece rates for workers, and performance bonuses are all attempts at a solution, and each has produced its own pathologies: options that reward luck, piece rates that sacrifice quality, and bonuses that encourage the gaming of whatever metric is measured.

## What sets wages

The textbook answer is that a worker is paid the value of what the last worker hired adds to output, their **marginal product**. Firms hire until that value falls to the wage, so wages track productivity. Across countries and decades this is roughly true: countries with more productive workers pay more, and average wages grew with productivity for most of the twentieth century.

Since about 1980 the fit has loosened in the United States. Productivity has kept rising; the typical worker's pay has risen much more slowly, and the share of national income going to labor rather than capital has fallen several percentage points.[^3] Explanations include globalization, automation of routine work, weaker unions, rising market power of employers, and measurement differences; the evidence supports several of these at once, and the debate over their weights is live.

Two features of real labor markets matter for everything that follows. First, employers often have some power over wages. A textbook competitive employer must pay the going rate or lose everyone; a real employer in a town with two hospitals can pay nurses a bit less than their marginal product, because leaving means moving. This **monopsony** power (one buyer, by analogy with monopoly's one seller; Joan Robinson named it in 1933) has been measured directly in recent years and appears widespread, especially for low-wage work.[^4] Second, workers are not interchangeable. Wages depend heavily on **human capital**, the skills and knowledge a worker carries, a term Gary Becker and Jacob Mincer made central in the 1960s.[^5] The return to a year of schooling, the extra earnings it brings, has been estimated in hundreds of studies and averages about 9 percent per year worldwide, higher in poorer countries, partly for what is learned and partly (chapter 5) for what the credential signals.

## How much people work

The other side of the labor market is the worker deciding how much to work at all, and the answer follows the logic of chapter 2. A higher wage makes an hour of leisure more expensive, which pushes people to work more (the substitution effect), but it also makes them richer, which lets them afford more leisure (the income effect). The two pull in opposite directions, and for men already working full time they nearly cancel: higher wages barely change hours. For decisions about whether to work at all, especially for second earners and parents of young children, the response is much larger, which is why childcare costs and tax rules on second incomes have measurable effects on how many people hold jobs.[^22]

Over the long run the income effect has won. Working hours in rich countries fell from about 60 a week in 1870 to under 40 today, a fall spent almost entirely on leisure, retirement, and longer schooling, and the workers of Western Europe took more of their rising wages as free time than Americans did.[^23]

## Immigration

Immigration is a supply shift in the labor market, and the textbook prediction is that more workers lower wages. The evidence, one of the best-studied questions in the field, mostly says otherwise for the average native worker, while leaving room for effects on specific groups. The classic natural experiment is the Mariel boatlift of 1980, when 125,000 Cubans arrived in Miami over a few months and expanded its labor force by 7 percent; David Card found no detectable effect on the wages or unemployment of Miami's existing low-skilled workers compared with similar cities.[^24] George Borjas re-examined the episode in 2017 and found a sharp wage drop for the least-educated men; other researchers argued his sample was too small and the drop an artifact of how the survey changed.[^25] A 2017 review of the whole literature by the US National Academies concluded that the effect on the wages of native workers overall is small, that any negative effects fall on earlier immigrants and natives without a high-school diploma, and that immigrants' children are among the strongest fiscal contributors in the country.[^26] Why the textbook prediction fails is now understood: immigrants are also consumers, so demand rises with supply; firms respond by investing and expanding; and immigrants and natives with the same paper qualifications often do different jobs, so they complement each other more than they compete.

## The minimum wage, again

Chapter 4 introduced the fight. Here is where it stands. If labor markets were perfectly competitive, a minimum wage above the going rate would price some workers out of jobs, and the only question would be how many. The evidence since Card and Krueger's 1994 study is that moderate minimum wages, up to about 60 percent of the local median wage, have had employment effects close to zero, and the reason is monopsony: where employers have wage-setting power, a floor can raise pay without reducing hiring, because the employer was paying below the competitive wage to begin with.[^6] Employers respond in other ways, raising prices slightly, reducing turnover (which saves them money), or trimming hours.

The frontier of the debate is how high is too high. California's \$20 minimum for large fast-food chains, imposed in 2024, is the current test case. One team using government payroll counts finds fast-food employment fell about 3 percent relative to the rest of the country; another, using different data and comparison groups, finds pay rose 7 percent with essentially no job loss.[^7] Both are careful; they disagree; the Latest Research section tracks the dispute. That a policy this well studied can still divide serious researchers tells you something about how hard causal inference is even with the best modern methods.

## Discrimination

Economists study discrimination with the same tools as everything else, and the results have been uncomfortable for everyone. Gary Becker argued in 1957 that an employer who refuses to hire qualified workers of some group pays for the prejudice in lost profit, so competition should erode discrimination over time.[^8] It has eroded, but slowly and incompletely, and the reason is that not all discrimination is Becker's taste-based kind. **Statistical discrimination** occurs when an employer who cannot observe a candidate's ability uses group membership as a proxy, which can persist under competition because it is, from the employer's narrow view, informative.

The cleanest evidence comes from **audit studies**, which send out fictitious applications that differ only in a signal of group membership. A 2004 study sent 5,000 résumés to Boston and Chicago employers, identical except for names: Emily and Greg received 50 percent more callbacks than Lakisha and Jamal.[^9] A 2022 study that sent 83,000 fictitious applications to 108 of the largest US employers found the gap had narrowed to about 9 percent on average, with a small number of firms responsible for much of it.[^10] Gender gaps have a different structure. Claudia Goldin, who received the 2023 Nobel Prize for the work, showed that the remaining gap in rich countries opens mainly after the first child, as mothers move to jobs with more flexibility and less pay while fathers do not.[^11] Across 134 countries, this **child penalty** is small in poor economies and grows as countries develop.[^12]

## Measuring inequality

**Inequality** is the dispersion of income or wealth across people, and measuring it is harder than it sounds. The standard summary is the **Gini coefficient**, which runs from 0 (everyone has the same) to 1 (one person has everything). It is derived from the **Lorenz curve**, a plot of the share of total income received by the poorest fraction of the population.

{{fig:lorenz-curve|The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of people, poorest first. Perfect equality is the diagonal. The Gini coefficient is the shaded area between the diagonal and the curve, divided by the whole triangle beneath the diagonal.}}

Income Ginis run from about 0.25 in the Nordic countries to about 0.40 in the United States and above 0.55 in South Africa.[^13] Wealth is always far more unequal than income: the top 1 percent of US households hold roughly 30 percent of all wealth, a share the Federal Reserve tracks quarterly.[^14]

The most disputed number in the field is how much US inequality has risen since 1980. Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez, and Gabriel Zucman, using tax records, found the top 1 percent's share of pre-tax income roughly doubled, from about 10 percent to about 20 percent.[^15] Gerald Auten and David Splinter, using the same records but different assumptions about how to assign income that never appears on a tax return, such as employer health benefits and retirement accounts, find a much smaller rise, and after taxes and transfers barely any.[^16] The two teams have exchanged replies for several years. Both agree inequality rose; they disagree by a factor of several on how much, and the disagreement is entirely about how to handle the third of national income that individual tax returns do not capture. A reader who hears any single number for "the rise in inequality" should ask which of these it comes from.

## Why inequality rose

Several forces are well documented, and they are not mutually exclusive.

**Skill-biased technical change.** Computers raised the productivity of educated workers and replaced routine clerical and production work, widening the gap between college and non-college wages from the 1980s onward.[^17]

**Trade.** Competition from Chinese manufacturing after 2000 hit specific American regions hard, with employment and wage losses that persisted for a decade, even as the national gains from cheaper goods were real (chapter 13).[^27]

**Superstar firms and markets.** In markets where the best product can serve everyone, from software to entertainment, the top performer captures a share that would once have been split among many. Rising concentration of sales among a few firms in each industry appears to account for much of the fall in labor's share.[^18]

**Institutions.** Union membership in the United States fell from about a third of workers in the 1950s to a tenth;[^28] the real minimum wage fell from the 1970s to the 2000s; top marginal tax rates fell from 70 percent to under 40. Countries that kept these institutions saw smaller rises in top income shares even though the same technologies and trade reached them, which is the main evidence that institutions matter; how much of the gap is institutions and how much is differences in how income is measured across countries is still argued.[^29]

**Capital and inheritance.** Piketty's broader argument, in a 2014 book that sold two million copies, is that when the return on wealth exceeds the growth rate of the economy, inherited fortunes grow faster than earned incomes and inequality rises mechanically.[^19] The historical pattern fits; the mechanism is debated.

## Mobility

Inequality at a point in time matters less if people move between rungs. **Intergenerational mobility**, the extent to which children's incomes are independent of their parents', has been measured with unprecedented precision since Raj Chetty and colleagues gained access to anonymized tax records for tens of millions of Americans. Their findings: a child born in the bottom fifth has about a 7.5 percent chance of reaching the top fifth in the United States, against about 13 percent in Canada and 12 percent in Denmark; mobility varies enormously across American cities, with the Southeast lowest; and the neighborhood a child grows up in has a causal effect, since children who move to higher-mobility areas young do better as adults.[^20] The most recent work finds class gaps in outcomes widening while racial gaps among low-income children have narrowed, driven by changes in the employment rates of the adults around them.[^21]

:::key
Inequality is where economics meets politics most directly, and the discipline's contribution is to separate the questions. How much has inequality risen? That is a measurement question with a disputed but narrowing answer. Why? Several causes, each with evidence. Does it matter? That depends partly on mobility, which is measurable, and partly on values, which are not. Economists have standing on the first two questions and no special standing on the last.
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
Two workers with similar skills earn different wages in different towns. Name a reason other than one worker secretly being more productive.

:::answer Show the reasoning
Local employer competition, bargaining power, mobility costs, amenities, and the availability of alternative jobs can differ. Productivity matters, but wages emerge within institutions and markets. A fair comparison would also check hours, benefits, tasks, and working conditions.

## Summary

- Firms exist because using the market has transaction costs; they stop growing where command becomes costlier than contract. Inside them, principal–agent problems replace price problems.
- Wages track productivity in the long run and across countries, but employers have real wage-setting power and human capital drives most wage differences.
- Higher wages barely change the hours of full-time workers but strongly affect whether second earners work at all. Immigration has small effects on the average native wage; the losers, where there are any, are the least educated and earlier immigrants.
- Moderate minimum wages have small employment effects because of monopsony; very high ones are an open research fight.
- Discrimination is measured by audit studies and has narrowed but not vanished; the gender pay gap in rich countries is mostly a child penalty.
- The Gini coefficient summarizes inequality; how much US inequality has risen since 1980 is genuinely disputed and turns on how untaxed income is assigned.
- Causes include technology, trade, superstar firms, weakened institutions, and capital returns. Mobility is lower in the United States than in Canada or Scandinavia and varies sharply by neighborhood.
