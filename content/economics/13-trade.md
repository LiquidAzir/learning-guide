## Recap

Chapter 3 introduced Ricardo's comparative advantage as the most counterintuitive true idea in the subject. This chapter works it through, adds what two centuries of theory and evidence have found about its limits, and turns to the questions that dominate the news: tariffs, deficits, exchange rates, and whether globalization was worth it.

## Comparative advantage

Suppose England can make a bolt of cloth with 100 hours of labor and a barrel of wine with 120, while Portugal needs 90 for cloth and 80 for wine. Portugal is better at both. Common sense says Portugal should make both and England nothing.

Ricardo's point is that what matters is not who is better but who is *relatively* better.[^1] In England a barrel of wine costs 1.2 bolts of cloth (120 hours against 100). In Portugal it costs 0.89 bolts (80 against 90). Wine is relatively cheap in Portugal and cloth relatively cheap in England. If Portugal makes wine and England cloth and they trade at, say, one for one, both end up with more of both than if each made everything itself.

:::math The arithmetic of gains from trade
Each country has 3,600 hours. Without trade, splitting hours evenly: England makes 18 bolts and 15 barrels; Portugal makes 20 bolts and 22.5 barrels. World total: 38 bolts, 37.5 barrels.

With specialization: England puts all 3,600 hours into cloth and makes 36 bolts. Portugal puts all 3,600 into wine and makes 45 barrels. World total: 36 bolts, 45 barrels. Trading 18 bolts for 18 barrels, England ends with 18 bolts and 18 barrels (up from 18 and 15); Portugal ends with 18 bolts and 27 barrels (down 2 bolts, up 4.5 barrels, a net gain at any reasonable valuation). The world has more wine and nearly as much cloth from the same hours. Nothing was invented; the gain came purely from each doing what it does relatively best.
:::

The principle applies to people as well as countries: a surgeon who is also the world's fastest typist should still hire a typist, because an hour of her typing costs an hour of surgery. It is why specialization exists at all.

## Who loses

Comparative advantage says a country gains *on average*. It does not say everyone gains. When England specializes in cloth, English winemakers lose their jobs. Wolfgang Stolper and Paul Samuelson showed in 1941 that trade raises the return to the factor a country has in abundance and lowers the return to the scarce one: in a rich country with abundant capital and skilled labor and scarce unskilled labor, trade with poor countries raises profits and skilled wages and lowers unskilled wages.[^2] The gains exceed the losses, so the winners could in principle compensate the losers and leave everyone better off. In practice they mostly do not, and the losers know it.

The clearest evidence is the **China shock**. After China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, its exports to the United States tripled in a decade. David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson compared American regions by how exposed their industries were to Chinese competition and found that exposed regions lost manufacturing jobs that were not replaced, saw wages fall, disability rolls rise, and marriage rates decline, with effects still visible fifteen years later.[^3] The national gains from cheaper goods were real and large; the losses were concentrated, durable, and geographically visible in a way the gains were not. Economists had known this would happen in principle. What they had underestimated was how slowly labor markets adjust and how much geography matters.

## Why rich countries trade with each other

Ricardo explains why Portugal trades wine for English cloth. It does not explain why Germany sells cars to France and France sells cars to Germany, which is most of world trade. Paul Krugman answered in 1979: **economies of scale** and a taste for **variety**.[^4] Building cars is cheaper per car in large factories, so each country cannot efficiently make every model, and consumers want choice. Countries specialize in variants and swap, and the gains come from scale and choice rather than from differences in cost. Krugman received the 2008 Nobel Prize for this **new trade theory**, which also explained why industries cluster: once Detroit or Silicon Valley exists, suppliers and workers gather there and the advantage compounds.

The volume of trade between two countries is predicted with startling accuracy by the **gravity model**: it rises with the size of both economies and falls with the distance between them, just as Newton's gravity does with mass and distance.[^5] Distance still matters enormously despite container ships and the internet; a border reduces trade by more than the tariffs at it can explain, which is one reason trade agreements that harmonize regulations do more than ones that only cut tariffs.

## Tariffs

A **tariff** is a tax on imports. Chapter 4's diagram applies directly: it raises the domestic price, helps domestic producers, hurts domestic consumers by more, raises revenue, and leaves a deadweight loss. Economists' agreement that tariffs make a country poorer on average is about as strong as any consensus in the field.[^6] The serious case for protection has always been narrower than the popular one: that a young industry may need shelter until it reaches scale (the **infant industry** argument, made by Alexander Hamilton in 1791), that some industries matter for national security whatever they cost, and that a large country can sometimes shift the terms of trade in its favor. Each is valid in principle and each has been used to justify far more protection than it can support.

History offers two natural experiments in each direction. Britain repealed its Corn Laws, tariffs on grain, in 1846, and food prices fell while industry boomed. The United States passed the Smoot–Hawley tariff in 1930, raising duties on more than 20,000 goods. Trading partners retaliated, and the value of world trade fell by two-thirds between 1929 and 1934, though most of that was collapsing prices and demand; the volume fell by about a quarter. Douglas Irwin's careful accounting finds the tariff made the Depression somewhat worse but did not cause it; its lasting damage was retaliation and a generation of discrimination against American exports.[^7] After 1945 the rich countries built a system, first the GATT and then the WTO, for negotiating tariffs down together, and average tariffs on manufactured goods fell from about 40 percent to under 4. World exports grew from about a tenth of world output to nearly a third.

The 2018 to 2025 tariffs are the newest experiment. Studies of the 2018 tariffs on Chinese goods found that nearly the entire cost was passed through to American buyers, with foreign exporters absorbing almost none, and that retaliation cost American farmers export markets that required \$28 billion in compensation.[^8] The much larger 2025 tariffs, which raised the average US rate from about 2.4 percent to around 10, have been tracked in real time: importers paid almost the whole tariff (about 90 percent passed through to import prices), but by late 2025 only about a fifth of it had reached shop shelves, with importers and retailers absorbing the rest for now; the estimated net effect on national income was small because consumer losses were roughly offset by revenue.[^9] Whether they achieved their stated goals, reducing the trade deficit and reviving manufacturing employment, has no clear evidence yet, and the Latest Research section tracks the results as they arrive.

## Trade deficits

A country runs a **trade deficit** when it imports more than it exports. The United States has run one every year since 1976, and it is widely read as losing. It is not, or not simply.

The accounting is unforgiving. A country that buys more from the world than it sells must pay the difference, and it pays by selling assets: bonds, stocks, real estate, companies. The trade deficit is exactly matched by a capital inflow. The United States runs a trade deficit because foreigners want to hold dollar assets, which lets Americans consume more than they produce, borrowing the difference. Whether that is good depends on what the borrowing funds: productive investment, which pays for itself, or consumption, which does not. National accounts also make the deficit equal to the gap between national saving and investment, so a country that saves little will run a deficit regardless of its tariffs. Tariffs shift which goods are imported; they do not change the saving gap, which is why tariff changes have historically had little visible effect on the US trade balance.

## Exchange rates

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. Under a **floating** rate the market sets it; under a **fixed** rate the central bank holds it by buying or selling its own currency. Two forces anchor it in the long run. **Purchasing power parity** says currencies should adjust so that the same basket of goods costs the same everywhere; it holds loosely over decades and badly over months. **Interest parity** says money flows toward higher interest rates, pushing that currency up. In the short run exchange rates move on expectations and are close to unpredictable.

Countries cannot have everything. Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming showed in the 1960s that a country can pick at most two of three: a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital, and an independent monetary policy.[^10] The **impossible trinity** explains the great choices. The eurozone chose fixed rates (a single currency) and free capital, and gave up national monetary policy, which is why Greece could not devalue its way out of its 2010 crisis. China long chose a fixed rate and independent policy and restricted capital flows. The United States floats and keeps both policy and open capital.

:::story When the trinity breaks
The choice has been made, and unmade, in three great episodes. Under the **gold standard** before 1914 every major currency was fixed to gold, capital moved freely, and central banks gave up control of their economies to defend the peg. When the Depression came, the countries that abandoned gold early, Britain in 1931, recovered first, and those that clung to it, France until 1936, suffered longest; Barry Eichengreen's phrase for the standard was "golden fetters."[^13] The Bretton Woods system of 1944 fixed currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold, and lasted until 1971, when the United States, unwilling to accept the constraint any longer, closed the gold window and the world floated.

The third episode is the **Asian crisis** of 1997. Thailand, Korea, and Indonesia had fixed their currencies to the dollar, opened to capital, and borrowed heavily in dollars at short maturities. When investors lost confidence, the pegs broke, the currencies halved, and every dollar debt doubled overnight in local terms; output fell by 6 to 13 percent in a year, in economies that had grown 7 percent a year for a generation.[^14] Argentina repeated the pattern in 2001, when its peso-dollar peg collapsed along with its banking system. The lesson written into policy afterward: a fixed rate with open capital and foreign-currency debt is the trinity's most dangerous corner, and most emerging economies now float, hold large dollar reserves, and borrow in their own currencies where they can.
:::

## Globalization's balance sheet

Between 1990 and 2020 world trade roughly tripled, and the effects were among the largest in economic history. Extreme poverty, measured on the World Bank's \$2.15-a-day line, fell from 38 percent of humanity to under 9, overwhelmingly through export-led growth in Asia (chapter 14). Prices of traded goods in rich countries fell relative to incomes; the poorest households, who spend most on such goods, gained most in that respect.[^11] Global inequality *between* countries fell for the first time in two centuries.

At the same time, the China shock and its equivalents hollowed out specific regions in rich countries, inequality *within* those countries rose, and the political consequences have reshaped the politics of trade in the United States and Europe. Economists' view of the profession's earlier consensus has shifted: the gains were real and larger than the losses, but the losses were more concentrated, more persistent, and more politically consequential than the standard models predicted, and the compensation the theory called for never arrived.

:::frontier
Three questions are open. Whether **reshoring** and "friend-shoring" of supply chains after the pandemic and amid US–China tension raise resilience enough to justify their cost. Whether the return of **industrial policy**, from American semiconductor subsidies to European battery plants, will repeat East Asia's success or Latin America's failure; the newest evidence is more favorable to well-designed policy than the older consensus was.[^12] And whether the world is dividing into trading blocs, reversing the integration of the last seventy years. The tariff experiments of 2025 and 2026 will supply the data.
:::

## Summary

- Comparative advantage: trade benefits both sides even when one is better at everything, because what matters is relative cost. The gains are real and can be shown arithmetically.
- Trade creates losers as well as winners; the China shock showed the losses are concentrated, durable, and local.
- Most trade is between similar countries and driven by scale and variety (Krugman); its volume follows a gravity law.
- Tariffs make a country poorer on average, are paid mostly by domestic buyers, and have historically invited retaliation. The 2025 tariffs are being measured in real time.
- A trade deficit is a capital inflow and equals the saving-investment gap; tariffs do not change it.
- The impossible trinity forces a choice between fixed rates, open capital, and independent monetary policy; the gold standard, Bretton Woods, and the 1997 Asian crisis show what happens when the choice fails. Globalization cut poverty and between-country inequality while raising within-country inequality.
