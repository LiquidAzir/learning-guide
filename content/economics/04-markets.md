## Recap

Smith claimed that self-interest under competition serves the public; the marginalists located value in the buyer's mind; Marshall drew the supply-and-demand diagram. This chapter takes the diagram seriously and asks what markets actually accomplish, how to measure it, and what happens when you interfere.

## Prices are information

Start with a puzzle. A pencil requires cedar from Oregon, graphite from Sri Lanka, rubber from Malaysia, brass from a dozen mines, and lacquer from a chemical plant. No one person knows how to make all of it. No one directs the loggers, the miners, and the chemists to coordinate. Yet pencils cost a few cents and are never in short supply.

Friedrich Hayek explained why in 1945.[^1] The knowledge needed to run an economy, who needs what, where things are scarce, which machine just broke, is scattered across millions of minds and changes constantly. No central office could collect it. But nobody needs to. When tin becomes scarce, its price rises, and every user of tin, without knowing why, economizes; every potential supplier, without being told, looks harder for more. The price carries the one bit of information each person needs, "tin is scarcer than before," to everyone who needs it, and nothing else. A **price system** is a machine for compressing and transmitting knowledge, and it is the reason planned economies, which tried to replace it with reports and quotas, chronically produced the wrong things in the wrong amounts.

:::key
The case for markets is not that people are selfish or that outcomes are fair. It is that prices aggregate knowledge no planner can have. This is an argument about information, and it is why the strongest critiques of markets (chapter 5) are also about information: the cases where prices fail to carry the truth.
:::

## Measuring the gains from trade

When you buy a coffee for \$3 that you would have paid \$5 for, you are \$2 better off. Economists call that **consumer surplus**: the gap between what buyers would have paid and what they did pay, summed across all buyers. It is the area under the demand curve and above the price. Symmetrically, **producer surplus** is the gap between the price sellers receive and the least they would have accepted: the area above the supply curve and below the price. The total, **social surplus**, is the value the market creates. In a competitive market at equilibrium, it is as large as it can be. Every trade that makes both sides better off happens; no trade that would make either worse off does.

That last sentence is a strong claim, and the rest of the chapter tests it.

## Taxes and the triangle of loss

Put a tax of \$1 on each unit sold. Buyers now pay more, sellers receive less, and the quantity traded falls. Some of the lost surplus becomes government revenue, which is a transfer rather than a loss: the money still exists. But the trades that no longer happen, the ones that were worth doing at the old price and are not worth doing with the tax, are simply gone. Their surplus is destroyed, not moved. This is **deadweight loss**, and on the diagram it is a triangle.

{{fig:deadweight-loss|A tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive. Quantity falls from Q* to Q₁. The rectangle is tax revenue, a transfer. The triangle is deadweight loss: trades that would have created value and now do not happen.}}

Two facts about that triangle drive tax policy. First, it grows with the *square* of the tax: doubling a tax quadruples the deadweight loss, because a tax twice as large kills twice as many trades, each of which was on average worth twice as much. That is why economists prefer many small taxes to one big one. Second, it is smaller when demand or supply is inelastic, because fewer trades are discouraged. That is why economists like taxes on land (perfectly inelastic; nobody makes less land) and on harms like carbon, where discouraging the activity is the point, and why they dislike high taxes on things people can easily stop buying.

Who actually pays a tax has nothing to do with who hands over the money. The burden falls on whichever side is less able to walk away: the less elastic side. A payroll tax nominally split between employer and employee is, in the long run, borne mostly by the employee, because workers are less able to leave the labor market than firms are to automate or relocate; the classic evidence is a Chilean reform that cut employer payroll taxes and saw wages rise almost one for one.[^2] Recent studies complicate the picture: a Swedish cut in payroll taxes on young workers went largely into firms' profits and hiring rather than into young workers' pay, at least for several years.[^8]

## Price controls

Governments often try to override the equilibrium price directly. The diagram predicts what happens, and the predictions have been tested.

A **price ceiling** below the equilibrium, such as rent control, creates a shortage: more people want apartments at the controlled rent than landlords will supply. The apartments go to those who got there first, who have connections, or who pay under the table; landlords stop maintaining units they cannot raise rent on; and over time new housing is not built. Studies of San Francisco's 1994 expansion of rent control found that it did protect existing tenants, cutting their displacement by about 20 percent, and that landlords responded by converting or redeveloping: buildings newly covered by rent control cut their supply of rental housing by about 15 percent, which pushed up rents citywide by about 5 percent for everyone not protected.[^3] The policy helped the people it covered and hurt the people it did not, which is what the diagram predicts.

:::story Why rents are high in the first place
Rent control is a response to high rents, and the dominant modern explanation for high rents is not landlords but land-use rules. In the most productive cities of the rich world, zoning, height limits, and permitting make it slow and expensive to build, so supply cannot follow demand and prices rise instead. Where cities have loosened those rules, as Auckland did in 2016 by allowing denser housing across most of its residential land, construction rose sharply and stayed high.[^9] The economics traces back to Ricardo's theory of rent (chapter 3): when the supply of a location is fixed, its owners collect the gains from everything that makes the location valuable. That is also why the land tax Henry George proposed in 1879 keeps reappearing in economists' recommendations: it falls on something that cannot be moved or reduced, so it has no deadweight loss.
:::

A **price floor** above the equilibrium, such as a minimum wage, creates a surplus: more people want to work at that wage than firms want to hire. The textbook prediction is unemployment. Here the evidence is more interesting, and it produced one of the great empirical fights in the field. In 1994 David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food employment in New Jersey, which had just raised its minimum wage, with neighboring Pennsylvania, which had not, and found no job loss.[^4] Three decades of follow-up work have found that moderate minimum wages have small or zero employment effects, for a reason that overturns the diagram's key assumption: employers are not powerless price-takers in the labor market.[^5] Chapter 8 explains the mechanism and the current fight over how high is too high.

:::warning
"The diagram predicts a shortage, so rent control is bad" is too quick. The diagram tells you *who* gains and loses: protected tenants gain, would-be tenants and future renters lose. Whether that trade is worth making is a value judgment. What economics contributes is refusing to let anyone pretend the losers do not exist.
:::

## What it costs to make things

The supply curve comes from somewhere: from what it costs firms to produce. Every firm's costs split into **fixed costs**, which do not change with output (the factory lease, the software license), and **variable costs**, which do (materials, hourly labor). The cost of producing one more unit is the **marginal cost**, and it is the number that matters for decisions, because a firm that can sell a unit for more than it costs to make should make it.

:::math Where the supply curve comes from
A firm that takes the market price as given produces up to the point where
$$P = MC$$
price equals marginal cost. Below that output, the next unit brings in more than it costs, so make it; above it, the next unit costs more than it brings in, so stop. As price rises, that crossing point moves out along the marginal cost curve, so the firm supplies more. The market supply curve is every firm's marginal cost curve added together. Marshall's upward-sloping blade is cost, made visible.

**Average cost** is total cost divided by units. When a firm has large fixed costs, average cost falls as output rises, because the fixed cost is spread over more units. These **economies of scale** are why big factories beat small ones, why software with a billion users is nearly free per user, and why some industries end up with one firm (the natural monopoly below). When average cost eventually rises, because the plant is congested or management is stretched, the firm has hit **diseconomies of scale**.
:::

One word needs care. When economists say competition drives profit to zero, they mean **economic profit**: revenue minus *all* costs, including the opportunity cost of the owner's time and money. A firm earning exactly enough to keep its owners from doing something else is earning what accountants call a healthy return and economists call zero economic profit, or **normal profit**. Anything above that attracts entrants; anything below drives exit.

## Market structures

The supply-and-demand diagram assumes many small sellers, none of whom can affect the price. Real markets vary.

**Perfect competition** is that ideal: many sellers of an identical product, free entry, full information. Wheat comes close. Each farmer is a **price-taker**: it accepts the market price and cannot move it, and if the price exceeds the cost of growing wheat, more farmers grow it until it does not. Economic profit is competed down to zero.

**Monopoly** is one seller. A monopolist does not take the price as given; it chooses the price, and it chooses a higher one than competition would produce, selling less. The units it declines to sell, which some buyers valued above their cost, are deadweight loss again, with the same triangle. Monopolies arise from control of a resource, from legal grants (patents, licenses), from **natural monopoly** where one firm can serve the market more cheaply than two (water pipes, power lines), or from **network effects** where a product is worth more the more people use it (telephone networks, social platforms).

A seller with market power will often charge different buyers different prices for the same thing. Airlines charge business travelers who book late far more than tourists who book early; software has student editions; cinemas have matinee prices. This **price discrimination** sorts buyers by how much they are willing to pay and charges each group accordingly. It can raise a seller's profit while also serving customers who would be priced out at a single price, which is why economists are ambivalent about it and why it is legal in most forms.

**Oligopoly** is a few sellers, each large enough to affect the price and each watching the others. Airlines, mobile carriers, and the makers of breakfast cereal live here. Their behavior depends on strategy and is the subject of game theory (chapter 7); it ranges from fierce competition to **tacit collusion**, keeping prices high without ever agreeing to.

**Monopolistic competition** is many sellers of *slightly different* products: restaurants, clothing brands, phone apps. Each has a little pricing power from its differences and faces constant entry from rivals. Most consumer markets look like this.

## The theorem behind the invisible hand

Smith's claim that competitive markets produce a good outcome was made precise in the early 1950s by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu. Working separately in 1951, each proved the **First Welfare Theorem**: any competitive equilibrium is **Pareto efficient**, meaning no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. (The term honors Vilfredo Pareto, who defined that standard of efficiency in 1906.)[^10] Then together in 1954 they proved that such an equilibrium *exists*: an economy of many markets can have a **general equilibrium**, a set of prices at which every market clears simultaneously, answering Walras's question from eighty years earlier.[^6] The invisible hand is a theorem.

The theorem's importance lies as much in its assumptions as its conclusion. It requires that everyone be a price-taker (no monopoly), that there be no **externalities** (my actions do not affect you except through prices), that goods be private (excludable and used up when consumed), and that everyone have full information. Each of those is violated in important markets, and each violation is a chapter-5 topic. The theorem is best read as a list of the conditions under which markets can be left alone, and therefore as a map of where they cannot.

Two further cautions. Pareto efficiency says nothing about fairness: an economy in which one person owns everything and everyone else starves can be Pareto efficient, since redistributing would make the owner worse off. A companion result, the **Second Welfare Theorem**, says that any efficient outcome you prefer can in principle be reached by redistributing wealth first and then letting markets run; the phrase "in principle" hides the entire problem, since redistribution in practice changes behavior and creates its own deadweight losses. And the theorems describe a resting point, not how an economy reaches it or how fast. Whether real markets converge to equilibrium, and what happens along the way, is the subject of the macroeconomics chapters.

## What planning tried and learned

The twentieth century ran the experiment. From 1928 the Soviet Union replaced prices with plans, setting output targets for every factory. It industrialized fast, at enormous human cost, and then stagnated. Managers met quotas by weight (heavy nails), by count (tiny nails), or by falsifying reports; shortages of some goods coexisted with warehouses of others; innovation had no channel to spread. By the 1980s Soviet income per person was perhaps a third of the American level and falling behind (Western intelligence estimates at the time put it nearer half; they were later judged too generous).[^7] China's turn toward markets after 1978 produced the fastest sustained growth in history (chapter 10). The point is not that planning cannot work at all; wartime economies and large corporations plan extensively. It is that nobody has found a substitute for prices as a way of discovering what things are worth.

## Summary

- Prices transmit dispersed knowledge that no planner can collect. That, not selfishness or fairness, is the core argument for markets.
- Consumer and producer surplus measure the gains from trade; competitive equilibrium maximizes their sum.
- Taxes create deadweight loss that grows with the square of the tax and shrinks with inelasticity; the burden falls on whoever cannot walk away.
- Price ceilings cause shortages and price floors cause surpluses, as predicted, except where the underlying market is not competitive: moderate minimum wages have had little employment effect. High rents mostly reflect restricted supply.
- Firms produce where price equals marginal cost; the supply curve is marginal cost added up. Economies of scale come from spreading fixed costs, and economic profit counts opportunity cost.
- Market structures range from perfect competition to monopoly; the deadweight-loss triangle reappears whenever a seller can set price. Price discrimination sorts buyers by willingness to pay.
- The First Welfare Theorem proves competitive markets are efficient and, by its assumptions, lists exactly when they are not. Efficiency is not fairness.
