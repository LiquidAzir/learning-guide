## What does a country's headline growth number leave out?

Everything so far has been about individual markets and the people in them. **Macroeconomics** asks about the whole: how much a country produces, how many people are working, how fast prices rise. Before any of that can be explained it has to be measured, and the measurements are less obvious and more consequential than they look.

## GDP

**Gross domestic product** is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period, usually a year or a quarter. It is the single most-quoted number in economics, and it is younger than the airplane.

Before the 1930s no government knew how large its economy was. When the Great Depression struck, policymakers were arguing about a collapse they could not measure. The US Congress commissioned Simon Kuznets to build the first national accounts, delivered in 1934; the same report contains his warning that "the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income."[^1] The framework was standardized internationally after the Second World War and is now maintained by the United Nations.

GDP can be counted three ways, which must agree. **Production**: add up each firm's **value added**, its sales minus what it bought from other firms, so that the flour is not counted twice. **Income**: add up everyone's wages, profits, rents, and interest, since every dollar of output is someone's income. **Expenditure**: add up what everyone spent on final goods. The expenditure version gives the identity that organizes all of macroeconomics:

:::math The national income identity
$$Y = C + I + G + NX$$
$Y$ is GDP. $C$ is **consumption**, household spending on goods and services, about two-thirds of the US total. $I$ is **investment**: business spending on factories, equipment, and software, plus new housing. In economics "investment" means building productive capacity, not buying stocks. $G$ is **government purchases** of goods and services (a soldier's salary counts; a pension payment does not, since it is a transfer, not a purchase of output). $NX$ is **net exports**: exports minus imports, negative for countries that import more than they sell. The equation is an accounting identity, true by construction. Its power is that any theory of the economy has to say which term is moving and why.
:::

**Nominal GDP** is measured in the year's own prices. **Real GDP** strips out price changes by valuing every year's output at the prices of one base year, so that a rise means more stuff, not just higher prices. The ratio of nominal to real is the **GDP deflator**, a broad price index. Growth rates on the news are always real.

Comparing countries adds a further step. Converting at market exchange rates understates poor countries, where a haircut or a meal costs a fraction of its rich-country price. **Purchasing power parity** (PPP) conversion uses the prices of a common basket instead, and more than triples the measured size of India's economy relative to market rates.[^2]

## What GDP misses

Kuznets's warning was apt. GDP counts what is sold. It omits housework and childcare (unless paid for), volunteer work, and leisure. It counts a hurricane's rebuilding as output and the destroyed houses as nothing. It counts the sale of a forest's timber and not the loss of the forest. It records the average and says nothing about the distribution: a country in which all the growth goes to one percent of the population shows the same GDP growth as one where it is shared. And it treats a dollar spent on prison the same as a dollar spent on schooling.

None of this is a secret; the people who build GDP say so on the first page. The measure exists because it is consistent, comparable across countries and years, and closely tied to things people care about: countries with higher GDP per person have longer lives, more schooling, less child mortality, and report more life satisfaction, with a relationship that flattens but does not vanish at high incomes.[^3] Alternatives such as the United Nations' Human Development Index, which combines income with life expectancy and education, track GDP per person closely for exactly that reason. The honest use of GDP is as a measure of production, not wellbeing, and the mistake is not in the number but in the sentence it gets put into.

## Unemployment

The **unemployment rate** is the share of the labor force that is out of work and actively looking. Both halves of that definition matter. Someone who has given up searching is not unemployed; they are **out of the labor force**, and so are students, retirees, and full-time parents. The **labor force participation rate**, the share of adults working or looking, therefore carries information the unemployment rate misses: US participation fell from 67 percent in 2000 to about 62 percent by the 2020s, partly from aging and partly from prime-age men leaving work, without any of that showing up as unemployment.[^4]

Statistical agencies publish several definitions. The headline US rate (called U-3) counts only active searchers; a broader one (U-6) adds discouraged workers and part-timers who want full-time work, and runs about twice as high. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Unemployment is never zero, because matching people to jobs takes time and some skills fall out of demand: economists call the rate consistent with a healthy, stable economy the **natural rate**. The Congressional Budget Office currently puts it for the United States at about 4.4 percent, though the estimate moves and is itself a live controversy (chapter 11).[^11]

Unemployment and output move together. **Okun's law**, an empirical regularity first noted by Arthur Okun in 1962, holds that each percentage point of unemployment above the natural rate costs roughly two percent of GDP.[^5] It is one of the more reliable rules of thumb in macroeconomics.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is the rate of increase in the general level of prices, and it is measured by a **price index**: the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services, tracked over time. The US **Consumer Price Index** prices about 80,000 items a month in stores and online, weighted by how much households actually buy of each.[^6] The percentage change in the index is the inflation rate.

Three problems make this harder than it sounds. **Substitution**: when beef gets expensive, people buy chicken, so a fixed basket overstates how much worse off they are. **Quality change**: a 2024 phone costs about what a 2010 phone did and does vastly more, so a price index that treats them as the same good records inflation where there was improvement. **New goods**: streaming services did not exist in the basket of 2005. A 1996 commission estimated that these biases together made the CPI overstate inflation by about 1.1 percentage points a year; methodological changes since have reduced but not eliminated the problem.[^7] Central banks generally watch a **core** measure that strips out volatile food and energy prices, not because those do not matter but because they swing for reasons that say little about the underlying trend.

Inflation matters for a reason that goes beyond rising prices: it silently rewrites every contract written in money. A borrower who owes \$100,000 at 3 percent interest gains from unexpected inflation of 8 percent, because the dollars repaid are worth less; the lender loses the same amount. Wages set in January buy less by December. Savers in cash lose purchasing power. Hyperinflation, at rates of hundreds or thousands of percent a year, destroys the usefulness of money altogether, and chapter 11 has the cases.

## Interest rates

An **interest rate** is the price of borrowing: the percentage of a loan paid per year for the use of the money. The rate you see is the **nominal** rate. The rate that matters for decisions is the **real** rate, the nominal rate minus expected inflation, because that is what the loan costs in actual goods. The distinction is Irving Fisher's, from 1896, and the **Fisher equation** that states it (real rate ≈ nominal rate − expected inflation) is used every day by every central bank.[^12] A 6 percent loan when inflation is 5 percent costs about 1 percent in real terms; a 2 percent loan when prices are falling 2 percent costs 4. Central banks set a short-term nominal rate (chapter 11) and everything else adjusts around it.

## Business cycles

Economies do not grow smoothly. Output rises for years, then falls for a few quarters, then resumes. Economists call this the **business cycle** and, in the United States, a committee at the National Bureau of Economic Research dates its turning points, declaring **recessions** (periods of broad, significant decline in economic activity) after the fact, usually months later.[^8] The popular rule of two consecutive quarters of falling GDP is a shorthand, not the definition.

Since the end of the Second World War the United States has had twelve recessions, averaging about ten months each, with expansions between them averaging about five years and lengthening over time.[^9] The 2007 to 2009 recession cut output by more than 4 percent from peak to trough and took years to recover;[^13] the 2020 pandemic recession cut it 10 percent in two months and was, by the committee's dating, the shortest on record. Why cycles happen, and what to do about them, is the subject of chapter 12.

:::key
Every macroeconomic number is a construction with choices inside it: which basket, which base year, who counts as looking for work. The choices are made openly by statistical agencies whose independence is one of the quiet foundations of good policy. When a government starts interfering with its statistics, as Argentina did with its inflation numbers from 2007 to 2015 and Greece did with its deficit before 2010, markets stop believing anything it says, and the cost of that is measured in crises.[^10]
:::

:::try Put the idea to work
A country's GDP rises after an expensive rebuilding effort following a disaster. Does that show the disaster made its residents better off?

:::answer Show the reasoning
No. GDP measures production over a period, not the value of everything people own or their overall welfare. Rebuilding adds to measured activity while replacing lost assets and responding to suffering. A flow of spending cannot by itself reveal the change in wealth or well-being.
:::
:::

## Summary

- GDP is the market value of final output, built three ways that must agree; the expenditure identity $Y = C + I + G + NX$ organizes macroeconomics. Real GDP corrects for prices; PPP corrects for cross-country price differences.
- GDP measures production, not wellbeing, and says nothing about distribution or the environment. It correlates strongly with what people care about, which is why it persists.
- Unemployment counts active searchers only; participation carries the rest. Okun's law links the unemployment gap to lost output.
- Inflation is measured by a price index with known biases from substitution, quality change, and new goods. It silently redistributes between borrowers and lenders.
- Real interest rates, nominal minus expected inflation, are what decisions depend on.
- Business cycles are dated after the fact; twelve US recessions since the Second World War, of shrinking frequency and varied depth.
