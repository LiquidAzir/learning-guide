## Recap

The First Welfare Theorem says competitive markets are efficient, provided there is no market power, no externalities, no public goods, and no missing information. This chapter goes through the exceptions. Each is a place where the price fails to carry the truth, and each has a body of evidence about what fixes work.

## Externalities: when the price leaves something out

A factory dumps waste into a river. The factory's costs include labor, materials, and power, and its price reflects them. It does not include the fishery downstream that dies, because the factory never pays for that. The harm is real, but it is **external** to the transaction, so the price is wrong: too low, and too much of the product is made. This is a **negative externality**. There are positive ones too: a beekeeper's bees pollinate the neighboring orchard, which pays nothing, so there are fewer bees than would be ideal.

Arthur Pigou proposed the fix in 1920: tax the harm by the size of the damage, so the price tells the truth.[^1] A **Pigouvian tax** on carbon, set equal to the damage a ton does, makes every emitter economize exactly as if they cared about the climate, whether or not they do. Economists across the political spectrum favor carbon pricing for this reason; a 2019 statement to that effect was signed by more than 3,500 economists, including 28 Nobel laureates.[^2]

Ronald Coase complicated the picture in 1960.[^3] If the fishery and the factory could bargain cheaply, he observed, they would reach the efficient outcome on their own, regardless of who legally held the right to the river: the fishery would pay the factory to pollute less if that was cheaper than losing fish, or the factory would pay the fishery for the right to pollute if its product was worth more than the fish. The **Coase theorem** says externalities are a problem of **transaction costs**, the costs of finding, negotiating with, and enforcing agreements with the affected parties. When those are low, markets fix themselves. When they are high, as with millions of drivers and a warming atmosphere, they do not, and the choice of fix matters. Coase's real contribution was to make transaction costs the central question.

The most successful application combines both ideas. In 1990 the United States capped total sulfur dioxide emissions from power plants and let plants trade the permits. Plants that could cut cheaply sold permits to those that could not, so the cuts happened where they cost least. Emissions fell by half within two decades and by more than 90 percent within three, at less than half the projected cost, and acid deposition in the Northeast fell with them.[^4] **Cap and trade** now covers carbon in the European Union, California, and China; the evidence to date is that carbon prices do reduce emissions, by amounts that depend on the price.[^5]

## Public goods: the free-rider problem

A lighthouse guides every ship whether or not the ship pays. National defense protects the taxpayer and the tax evader alike. A discovery, once made, can be used by everyone at once. Paul Samuelson formalized such goods in 1954.[^6] A **public good** has two properties: it is **non-excludable** (you cannot keep non-payers from using it) and **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce another's). Private markets undersupply public goods, because each person's best move is to let others pay and enjoy the result anyway. Economists call this **free riding**, and it is why lighthouses, basic research, and armies are mostly funded by taxes.

The category is broader than it sounds. Knowledge is the most important public good in the economy. Once a vaccine formula or a better algorithm exists, giving it to one more person costs nothing and excludes no one, so the efficient price is zero, but at a price of zero nobody would fund the research. Patents resolve this by granting a temporary monopoly: inefficiently high prices for twenty years in exchange for the invention existing at all. Whether the trade is well struck, and for which industries, is a live debate; some economists argue the system now does more to block innovation than to fund it, while studies of drug patents find that shorter protection does reduce the research firms undertake.[^16] The same tension shapes the economics of ideas in chapter 10.

## The commons

A pasture open to everyone will be overgrazed, because each herder gets the whole benefit of an extra cow and bears only a fraction of the cost of the worn-out grass. Garrett Hardin named this the **tragedy of the commons** in 1968, and it describes fisheries, aquifers, the atmosphere, and antibiotics (each use hastens resistance for everyone).[^7] A **common-pool resource** is rival but not excludable: my use depletes yours, but nobody can be fenced out.

The standard fixes are privatization (assign the pasture to one owner who bears all the costs) or regulation (set a quota). Elinor Ostrom spent a career documenting a third: communities that manage their commons themselves, through local rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions, often for centuries.[^8] Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese forests, Spanish irrigation systems, and Maine lobster fisheries all work this way. She won the 2009 Nobel Prize for showing that the tragedy is not inevitable and that the choice is not only between market and state. The world's fisheries illustrate both outcomes: those under **individual transferable quotas** (a fixed share of the total allowed catch that each fisher owns and can sell) or strong community management have largely recovered; the open-access ones continue to collapse.[^9]

## Information: the market for lemons

The First Welfare Theorem assumes everyone knows what they are buying. In 1970 George Akerlof asked what happens when they do not, using the used-car market.[^10] Sellers know whether their car is a good one or a "lemon"; buyers cannot tell. So buyers offer a price reflecting the average quality. At that price, owners of good cars will not sell (their cars are worth more than the average), so only lemons come to market, buyers learn this and lower their offers, and the market can unravel entirely. Good used cars go unsold even though there are buyers who would pay their true worth.

This is **asymmetric information**, and its two children run through every insurance and credit market. **Adverse selection** is the lemons problem: the people most eager to buy health insurance are the sick, so insurers charge more, so the healthy drop out, so insurers charge more still. **Moral hazard** is the change in behavior once insured: a fully insured driver drives less carefully, a bank with a government guarantee takes bigger bets. Both explain why insurance markets so often need mandates (to keep the healthy in the pool) and deductibles (to keep the insured careful), and why left alone they can fail to exist at all. Kenneth Arrow founded health economics in 1963 by pointing out that medical care violates nearly every assumption of the welfare theorems at once.[^17] The two great experiments in the field confirmed the theory: the RAND experiment of the 1970s, which randomly assigned families to plans with different levels of cost-sharing, found that people who paid more out of pocket used about a third less care with little measurable effect on their health; and Oregon's 2008 Medicaid lottery found that coverage raised use of care, virtually eliminated catastrophic medical bills, and improved mental health, while its effect on measured physical health over two years was small.[^18]

Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Prize with Akerlof for working out the responses. Spence showed how the informed side can **signal**: a university degree tells employers something about persistence and ability even if nothing learned in class is ever used, which is one reason education pays even when its content does not.[^11] Stiglitz showed how the uninformed side can **screen**: an insurer offering a menu of policies with different deductibles lets customers sort themselves by risk. Information economics has since become one of the largest fields in the subject; the design of health-insurance markets, credit scoring, and online reputation systems all descend from it.

## Market power

Chapter 4 described monopoly. The policy response is **antitrust** (in Europe, competition policy), which dates from the Sherman Act of 1890, passed to break up the trusts that controlled American oil, sugar, and railways. Standard Oil was split into thirty-four companies in 1911.[^19] For most of the twentieth century antitrust was aggressive; from the late 1970s a school of thought centered at the University of Chicago argued that the only proper test was whether a practice raised prices to consumers, and enforcement narrowed.[^12]

The last decade reopened the argument. Measured **markups**, the gap between prices and costs, appear to have risen across the American economy since 1980, though how much depends on method.[^13] The largest technology firms sit on network effects that make them natural near-monopolies in search, social media, and app distribution, and in 2024 a US federal court found that Google had illegally maintained its search monopoly.[^14] Whether the rise in concentration reflects abuse or simply the efficiency of very good firms remains contested, and so does the size of the rise itself: the leading estimates come from the accounts of publicly listed firms, and other economists using different data find a much smaller increase.[^13]

Natural monopolies, where one network is cheaper than two, are handled differently: they are regulated or publicly owned, and the regulator's problem is to set a price that covers costs without allowing monopoly profit, using information the firm has every incentive to distort. It is an information problem again.

## When the fix fails too

Every market failure invites a government response, and governments fail as well. Regulators get captured by the industries they oversee; politicians serve concentrated interests that lobby over diffuse voters who do not; programs outlive their purpose because their beneficiaries defend them. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock founded **public choice** theory in 1962 to analyze government with the same tools used on markets, assuming officials pursue their own interests too.[^15] Its central concept is **rent-seeking**: spending resources to capture existing wealth (a tariff, a license, a subsidy) rather than to create new wealth. The sugar tariff that costs American consumers a few dollars each and earns a few thousand producers millions is the standard example.[^20]

The honest conclusion is that the choice is never between a failing market and a perfect government. It is between imperfect institutions, and the question is which failure is smaller in a given case. The evidence in this chapter is what that comparison looks like when done carefully: carbon pricing works; well-designed commons management works; insurance mandates work; and each has failed when designed badly.

## Summary

- Externalities make prices lie about costs; Pigouvian taxes and cap-and-trade make them tell the truth, and the acid-rain program proved it at scale.
- Public goods are undersupplied because of free riding; knowledge is the most important one, and patents are an imperfect fix.
- Common-pool resources are overused unless privatized, regulated, or, as Ostrom showed, managed by their users.
- Asymmetric information produces adverse selection and moral hazard, can unravel markets entirely, and is answered by signaling, screening, and mandates.
- Market power is met with antitrust, whose reach expanded, narrowed, and is now expanding again.
- Governments fail too. The choice is between imperfect institutions, decided by evidence.
