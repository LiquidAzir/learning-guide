## Recap

Chapter 9 defined GDP per person. This chapter asks the question that Robert Lucas said makes it hard to think about anything else: why is it roughly \$80,000 in the United States and under \$2,000 in Malawi, at purchasing-power parity, and what would it take to change that?[^1] The differences are not small. They are the difference between a child dying of diarrhea and a child in school, and they are the largest fact about the modern world.

## The hockey stick

For almost all of human history, income per person barely moved. The best estimates put it at roughly the equivalent of \$1,000 to \$2,000 a year in today's money in 1000 CE, and about the same in 1700, everywhere.[^2] Some societies were richer than others, and living standards rose and fell with plagues and harvests, but there was no trend. Malthus's trap (chapter 3) held: any gain in productivity was absorbed by more people.

Then, around 1800 in Britain and spreading outward, growth started and did not stop. British income per person more than doubled in the nineteenth century, then quadrupled in the twentieth. The United States, Western Europe, and Japan followed; more recently South Korea, Taiwan, and China. World GDP per person is now about fifteen times what it was in 1800.[^2] Plotted over the long run, the graph is flat for millennia and then turns sharply up: the hockey stick. Explaining that turn is the central task of growth economics, and there are two layers to it, a mechanical one and a deep one.

## The mechanics: Solow

Robert Solow's 1956 model asks what determines how much an economy produces from what it has.[^3] Output depends on **capital** (machines, buildings, roads), **labor** (people and their hours), and a factor for how productively they are combined, which Solow called **technology** and which is better read as "everything else."

:::math The production function
The standard form is
$$Y = A\, K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}$$
$Y$ is output. $K$ is the capital stock and $L$ the labor force. $A$ is **total factor productivity**, the efficiency with which the two are used; it captures technology, organization, institutions, and anything else that makes the same inputs yield more. The exponent $\alpha$ (alpha), about one-third in most economies, is capital's share of income, the fraction of national income paid to the owners of capital as profit, rent, and interest; $1-\alpha$, about two-thirds, is labor's share, paid as wages. The exponents sum to one, which means doubling both inputs doubles output.

Because $\alpha < 1$, capital has **diminishing returns**: each extra machine adds less than the last. That is the key. An economy that saves and builds capital grows, but each addition yields less, so growth from capital accumulation alone slows and eventually stops at a **steady state** where new investment just replaces worn-out machines. Long-run growth in income per person can come only from growth in $A$.
:::

{{fig:solow|The Solow model. Output rises with capital per worker but flattens, because each machine adds less than the last. Saving funds new capital; wear and tear destroys some each year. Where the two meet is the steady state: a poor economy below it grows by accumulating capital, a rich one at it can grow only if the whole output curve lifts, which is what better ideas do.}}

Solow's model made two predictions that held up well. Poor countries with little capital should grow faster than rich ones when they start investing, because their returns are higher; this **convergence** describes postwar Europe and Japan, and later Korea and China, catching up to the American frontier. And the rich countries' continued growth must be mostly productivity. When Solow did the accounting for the United States, about seven-eighths of growth in output per worker over the first half of the century came from $A$, not from more capital per worker.[^4] Since $A$ was defined as whatever the model could not explain, this was a **residual**, and economists called it, only half joking, "the measure of our ignorance." The next forty years were spent trying to open it.

## Ideas

Paul Romer's answer, in 1990, was that the residual is ideas, and ideas are different from other goods.[^5] A machine can be used by one worker at a time. A blueprint can be used by every factory in the world at once without being used up: it is **non-rival**, in the language of chapter 5. This changes the arithmetic of growth. A larger economy has more people to think of ideas and a larger market to spread each idea across, so the return to producing ideas does not diminish the way the return to building machines does. Growth can sustain itself, and a policy that raises the rate of idea production (research funding, patents, education, openness to trade) raises the growth rate permanently, not just the level. Romer received the 2018 Nobel Prize.

Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt supplied the mechanism in 1992: **creative destruction**, a phrase borrowed from Joseph Schumpeter.[^6] Each new idea makes some existing product or firm obsolete. The innovator gains a temporary monopoly and profit; that profit is what motivates the innovation; the next innovator destroys it. Growth is a churn of entry and exit, and anything that protects incumbents from that churn, whether by regulation, subsidy, or monopoly, slows it. Aghion and Howitt shared the 2025 Nobel Prize with the economic historian Joel Mokyr, whose work asked the deep question the models leave open: why did the churn begin when and where it did?[^7]

## The deep question: institutions

Machines, education, and ideas are the **proximate** causes of growth: they are what growth is made of. But they are also choices, and the deep question is why some societies make them and others do not. Why did Britain industrialize and not China, which had gunpowder, paper, and the compass centuries earlier? Why has Botswana grown steadily since independence while its neighbors stagnated?

The most influential answer is **institutions**: the rules, formal and informal, that structure economic life. Douglass North argued in 1990 that societies grow when their rules make it profitable to be productive rather than to grab, and that such rules, above all secure property rights and impartial courts, are historically rare.[^8] Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson gave the argument its evidence in 2001 by exploiting a natural experiment: European colonization.[^9] Where colonists could settle, they built **inclusive** institutions, broad property rights and constraints on rulers, for their own benefit. Where disease made settlement deadly, they built **extractive** institutions designed to take resources out, with a small elite and no constraints. The mortality that European settlers faced two to three centuries ago predicts income per person today with remarkable strength, and the effect runs through institutions rather than geography, since the same tropical climates hosted both kinds. The mortality data have been challenged as patchy and partly imputed from neighboring countries, and the size of the effect is disputed, but the direction has survived two decades of re-examination.[^9] The three received the 2024 Nobel Prize.

Institutions are not the only deep candidate. **Geography** matters: landlocked countries and tropical ones face real disadvantages in trade and disease. **Culture**, meaning trust, attitudes to work, and beliefs about the future, is hard to measure but shows up in careful studies. And Mokyr's answer to the British question is intellectual: a culture of useful knowledge that valued tinkering and let scientists and craftsmen talk to each other, which Britain had unusually much of in 1750.[^7] The honest state of the field is that institutions carry the most evidential weight, that the others matter too, and that all of them are entangled with history in ways the models struggle to separate.

:::warning
Growth economics has a long record of confident theories overturned by the next decade's data. In the 1960s, the Soviet Union's investment-led growth was expected to overtake the West; it stalled. In the 1980s, Japan was expected to dominate; it stagnated. In the 2000s, the "BRICs" were expected to converge rapidly; Brazil and Russia did not. The lesson is not that the theories are worthless but that growth depends on political choices no model predicts. Treat every growth forecast, including the ones in the next section, as conditional.
:::

## Who grew, and how

The postwar record offers natural experiments of its own. Korea and Taiwan went from Malawi-level incomes in 1960 to rich-country status by 2000, through high investment, universal education, export-oriented manufacturing, and a state that pushed firms into world markets while protecting them at home. Whether that **industrial policy** caused the growth or merely accompanied it was long disputed; a wave of careful studies since about 2015 has moved the profession toward crediting it more, while emphasizing that the same tools failed in Latin America and Africa without the export discipline.[^10]

China is the largest case in history. After 1978 it moved from collective farms and planned industry to markets, in stages and without a blueprint, and its income per person rose about twenty-five-fold in forty years by the World Bank's count, lifting about 800 million people out of extreme poverty.[^11] The ingredients were the ones the models name: enormous investment, a fast-growing and increasingly educated workforce, openness to trade after 2001, and a shift of hundreds of millions of workers from low-productivity agriculture to factories. Its growth has slowed since 2010, as the models predict for an economy approaching the frontier with an aging population and a very large debt load.

India, Vietnam, Bangladesh, and much of Southeast Asia followed similar paths at varying speeds. Sub-Saharan Africa mostly did not until the 2000s, and its growth since then has been real but uneven. The world as a whole has converged since 1990, with the gap between rich and poor countries narrowing for the first time in two centuries, almost entirely because of Asia.[^12]

## The slowdown

Since about 1970, productivity growth in the rich countries has been slower than in the postwar decades, and since 2005 slower still.[^13] Robert Gordon argues the great inventions, electricity, the internal combustion engine, indoor plumbing, are behind us and the digital revolution is smaller than it looks. Others point to measurement (free digital services are not in GDP), to the exhaustion of the education boom, to the difficulty of finding new ideas as the easy ones are used up, or to institutional sclerosis. The debate matters because, at 1 percent growth, income doubles every 70 years; at 2 percent, every 35. Which world we are in determines what a young person today can expect.

A second drag is slower and more certain: demography. Birth rates have fallen below the replacement level in every rich country and in China, so working-age populations are shrinking in Japan, Korea, Italy, and Germany and have begun to shrink in China.[^15] In the Solow model that is a falling $L$, and it also means fewer workers supporting each retiree, which pulls saving and investment down. Whether it also slows the production of ideas, because fewer people are thinking, is the question Romer's framework raises and nobody has yet answered.

:::frontier
Whether artificial intelligence breaks the slowdown is the growth question of the decade, and the profession is divided along familiar lines. Acemoglu's task-based estimate finds AI raising productivity by less than 1 percent over ten years, because most tasks are not yet automatable; optimists building on Romer argue that AI could accelerate the production of ideas themselves, which would be a change in the growth rate, not the level.[^14] The early evidence from actual workplaces shows large gains for specific tasks and no visible effect on aggregate productivity or wages so far. Chapter 16 and the Latest Research section track it.
:::

## Summary

- For all of history until about 1800, income per person was flat. Then it began doubling every generation in a widening circle of countries.
- Solow: output comes from capital, labor, and productivity. Capital has diminishing returns, so long-run growth must come from productivity, which turned out to be most of it.
- Romer: productivity growth is ideas, which are non-rival and so do not diminish. Aghion and Howitt: the mechanism is creative destruction.
- The deep cause is contested but institutions carry the most evidence: inclusive rules produce growth, extractive ones do not, as colonization's natural experiment shows.
- East Asia converged through investment, education, and export-oriented industrial policy; China's forty-year run is the largest escape from poverty in history.
- Rich-country productivity growth has slowed since 1970; whether AI reverses that is open.
