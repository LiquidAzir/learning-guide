## How to use this

Terms are grouped by the chapter that introduces them, so the glossary doubles as a summary of the course. Within each group they are alphabetical. Related terms are folded into the entry they belong with, so if you cannot find a word on its own, look under the idea it belongs to.

## Foundations (chapters 1 and 2)

**Administrative data.** Records governments collect for other purposes (tax, payroll, benefits), now the main raw material of empirical economics.

**Compounding.** Growth on growth: a quantity growing at rate $g$ multiplies by $(1+g)$ each year. The **rule of 70** gives the doubling time as $70/g$ for $g$ in percent.

**Counterfactual.** What would have happened otherwise. The standard against which any causal claim is judged, and never directly observable.

**Discounting and present value.** Converting future money into today's terms by dividing by $(1+r)^t$, where $r$ is the interest rate and $t$ the years ahead. The choice of $r$ decides how much the future counts.

**Elasticity.** The percentage change in one thing per percentage change in another. **Inelastic** demand (below 1 in size) barely responds to price; **elastic** demand responds more than proportionally.

**Equilibrium.** The price at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. Above it a **surplus** pushes the price down; below it a **shortage** pushes it up.

**Expected value.** The average outcome, weighting each possibility by its probability. **Risk aversion** is preferring a sure thing to a gamble of equal expected value.

**Fallacy of composition.** Assuming what is true for one is true for all. If everyone saves more at once, total saving may not rise.

**Gross domestic product (GDP).** The market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period. **Real** GDP corrects for inflation; **nominal** does not; the ratio of the two is the **GDP deflator**, a broad price index.

**Income and substitution effects.** The two ways a price change alters what you buy: it changes how much your money is worth (income effect) and how one good compares with another (substitution effect). **Substitutes** replace each other (tea for coffee); **complements** go together (cars and fuel). **Normal goods** are bought more as income rises; **inferior goods** less.

**Inflation.** The rate at which the general price level rises, measured by the change in a **price index** such as the CPI. **Core** inflation strips out volatile food and energy prices to show the trend.

**Macroeconomics and microeconomics.** The study of the whole economy (growth, unemployment, inflation) and the study of individuals, firms, and markets.

**Marginal.** Pertaining to one more unit. **Marginal benefit** and **marginal cost** are the extra gain and extra cost of one more; decisions are made where they meet. **Diminishing returns**: each additional unit adds less.

**Model.** A deliberate simplification that keeps what matters and drops what does not. A map, not the territory.

**Natural experiment and randomized controlled trial.** A situation in which policy or chance assigns a treatment as if at random; and an experiment in which the researcher does so by lottery. The two main routes to a credible counterfactual.

**Opportunity cost.** The value of the best alternative given up. The true cost of any choice.

**Real versus nominal.** Corrected for inflation, versus measured in the money of the day.

**Scarcity.** The condition that there is never enough of everything, so every choice gives something up. The subject's starting point.

**Sunk cost.** Money already spent and unrecoverable. Irrelevant to any decision going forward, though people rarely treat it so.

**Supply and demand.** Curves showing how much sellers offer and buyers want at each price. Price changes move along a curve; anything else shifts it.

## History and markets (chapters 3 to 5)

**Adverse selection and moral hazard.** Under asymmetric information: the tendency of the riskiest to buy insurance (adverse selection) and of the insured to take more risk (moral hazard).

**Antitrust.** Laws against monopoly and collusion; in Europe, competition policy.

**Asymmetric information.** One party to a transaction knows more than the other. The used-car market is the classic case.

**Cap and trade.** Capping total emissions and letting firms trade permits, so cuts happen where they cost least.

**Coase theorem.** With low transaction costs, parties bargain to the efficient outcome whatever the legal starting point. Externalities are therefore a transaction-cost problem.

**Comparative advantage.** Being *relatively* better at something. Both parties gain from trade even if one is absolutely better at everything.

**Consumer surplus and producer surplus.** What buyers would have paid minus what they did; what sellers received minus the least they would have accepted. Their sum is the gain from trade.

**Costs.** **Fixed** costs do not change with output; **variable** costs do; **marginal** cost is the cost of one more unit; **average** cost is total cost per unit. **Economies of scale**: average cost falling as output rises, because fixed costs are spread. A price-taking firm produces where price equals marginal cost.

**Deadweight loss.** Value destroyed, not transferred, when a tax, price control, or monopoly prevents trades that would have benefited both sides. A triangle on the diagram.

**Division of labor.** Specialization, which raises output and is limited by the size of the market. Smith's engine of growth.

**Economic profit.** Revenue minus all costs including the opportunity cost of the owners' time and money. Competition drives it to zero; **normal profit** is the return that just keeps owners in the business.

**Externality.** A cost or benefit that falls on someone outside a transaction, so the price omits it. Pollution is negative; a beekeeper's pollination is positive.

**First and Second Welfare Theorems.** A competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient, given no market power, no externalities, no public goods, and full information; its assumptions list where markets fail. And any efficient outcome can in principle be reached by redistributing first and letting markets run.

**Free rider.** Someone who benefits from a public good without paying for it.

**General equilibrium.** All markets clearing simultaneously. Walras asked whether it exists; Arrow and Debreu proved it does.

**Invisible hand.** Smith's phrase, used once, for the way competition can turn self-interest to public benefit.

**Jevons paradox.** Using a resource more efficiently can raise total use of it, because efficiency makes it cheaper.

**Labor theory of value.** The classical view that a thing's value is the labor that made it. Replaced by marginal utility.

**Laissez faire.** "Let it be": the doctrine that the state should not interfere with prices.

**Malthusian trap.** Population growing until living standards fall back to subsistence. Described most of history before 1800; escaped since.

**Marginal utility.** The value of one more unit to the buyer. Explains why water is cheap and diamonds dear.

**Market power.** The ability of a seller (monopoly) or buyer (monopsony) to affect the price rather than take it as given.

**Market structures.** Perfect competition (many identical **price-taking** sellers, none able to move the price), monopoly (one), oligopoly (few, who may sustain **tacit collusion**, high prices without an agreement), monopolistic competition (many, slightly different).

**Mercantilism.** The doctrine that wealth is gold and trade a contest. Suited its age; wrong about wealth.

**Natural monopoly.** A market one firm can serve more cheaply than two, such as water pipes. Usually regulated.

**Network effects.** A product worth more the more people use it.

**Pareto efficiency.** A state in which no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. Says nothing about fairness.

**Pigouvian tax.** A tax on a harm equal to the damage it does, so the price tells the truth.

**Price ceiling and price floor.** A legal maximum (rent control) or minimum (minimum wage) price. The first causes shortages, the second surpluses, in competitive markets.

**Price discrimination.** Charging different buyers different prices for the same thing, sorted by willingness to pay: student discounts, late airfares.

**Price system.** Prices as a mechanism for transmitting dispersed knowledge. Hayek's argument for markets.

**Public choice.** The analysis of government using the same self-interest assumptions applied to markets.

**Public good.** Non-excludable and non-rival: nobody can be kept from using it and one person's use does not reduce another's. Undersupplied by markets.

**Rent and rent-seeking.** Income from owning something scarce rather than producing; and spending resources to capture such income rather than create wealth.

**Signaling and screening.** Ways the informed party reveals information (a degree) and the uninformed party sorts (a menu of insurance policies).

**Tragedy of the commons.** Overuse of a resource everyone can access and no one owns. Fixed by property rights (such as **individual transferable quotas**, ownable and sellable shares of a total catch), regulation, or community management.

**Transaction costs.** The costs of finding, negotiating with, and enforcing agreements. Central to why firms exist and when externalities matter.

## People and strategy (chapters 6 to 8)

**Anchoring, framing, and mental accounting.** Estimates pulled toward a recent number; the same choice answered differently depending on how it is described; money treated differently depending on which mental pot it sits in.

**Audit study.** A field experiment sending otherwise identical applications that differ in one signal, to measure discrimination.

**Behavioral economics.** The study of how actual psychology changes economic predictions.

**Bounded rationality.** Limited attention and computation, leading people to use **heuristics**, rules of thumb that work most of the time.

**Child penalty.** The fall in a mother's earnings after a first child, relative to the father's. The main source of the remaining gender gap in rich countries.

**Conspicuous consumption.** Veblen's term for spending whose point is to be seen. Part of the broader finding that people care about relative position.

**Dominant strategy.** A choice that is best whatever the other player does.

**Folk theorem.** In a repeated game with patient players, cooperation can be sustained by the threat of retaliation.

**Expected utility.** The average of the utility of each outcome, weighted by probability. The standard model of risky choice.

**Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve.** A summary of inequality from 0 (equal) to 1 (one person has all), derived from the curve plotting cumulative income share against cumulative population share.

**Human capital.** The skills and knowledge a worker carries, which drive most wage differences.

**Hyperbolic discounting and present bias.** Discounting the near future much more steeply than the far future; the source of procrastination and under-saving.

**Intergenerational mobility.** The extent to which children's incomes are independent of their parents'.

**Level-k reasoning.** Models in which players think only a limited number of steps ahead. Fits laboratory play better than Nash equilibrium.

**Loss aversion and the endowment effect.** Losses feel about twice as bad as equal gains; people demand more to give something up than they would pay to get it.

**Marginal product.** What one more worker adds to output. In a competitive market, the wage.

**Mechanism design.** Constructing the rules of a game to produce a desired outcome given private information. Auctions and matching are examples.

**Monopsony.** A buyer with power over the price, such as a dominant employer over wages.

**Nash equilibrium.** A set of choices from which no player can gain by changing alone. Every finite game has one; it is neither a prediction nor an endorsement.

**Nudge.** A change in how a choice is presented, without changing options or incentives. Defaults are the strongest kind.

**Principal–agent problem.** Getting someone with their own interests and private information to act in yours.

**Prisoner's dilemma.** A game in which each player's dominant strategy produces a worse outcome for both than cooperation would.

**Prospect theory.** Kahneman and Tversky's model: outcomes judged as gains and losses from a reference point, losses weighted more, probabilities misweighted.

**Reference point.** The baseline against which gains and losses are felt, usually the status quo.

**Skill-biased technical change.** Technology that raises the productivity of educated workers while replacing routine work, widening the wage gap between them.

**Stable matching.** A pairing in which no two participants would both prefer each other to their assigned partners. Found by the deferred acceptance algorithm.

**Superstar firms.** Firms that, in markets where the best product can serve everyone, capture a share once split among many. A leading explanation for labor's falling share of income.

**Statistical discrimination.** Using group membership as a proxy for unobserved individual traits.

**Tit-for-tat.** Cooperate first, then copy the other player's last move. The winner of Axelrod's tournaments.

**Ultimatum game.** One player proposes a split, the other accepts or rejects. People reject unfair offers at a cost to themselves.

**Utility function.** A number assigned to each option so that better options get bigger numbers. Only the ranking matters.

**Vickrey auction.** A sealed-bid auction in which the winner pays the second-highest bid, so honest bidding is optimal. The **generalized second-price auction** used for search ads is a close relative that is not quite truthful.

## The economy as a whole (chapters 9 to 12)

**Aggregate demand.** Total spending in the economy. Keynes's explanation for depressions.

**Anchored expectations.** The state in which people expect inflation near the target and set wages and prices accordingly, so it largely stays there.

**Austerity.** Cutting government spending or raising taxes during a downturn to reduce deficits. Europe's 2010–12 version is widely judged to have been too fast.

**Automatic stabilizers and discretionary stimulus.** Taxes and transfers that cushion downturns without anyone deciding: receipts fall and benefits rise in a recession. Versus spending or tax cuts deliberately passed in response to one.

**Bank run.** Depositors withdrawing at once because they fear others will. Self-fulfilling; answered by deposit insurance and a lender of last resort.

**Business cycle and recession.** The recurring rise and fall of output; a period of broad, significant decline, dated after the fact.

**Central bank and monetary policy.** The institution that issues money and sets interest rates; the management of rates and the money supply. In the United States the policy rate is the **federal funds rate**, at which banks lend reserves to each other overnight.

**Creative destruction.** New products and firms constantly replacing old ones. The engine of growth in Aghion and Howitt's theory.

**Demographic transition.** The shift from high birth and death rates to low ones, which opens a window of many workers and few dependents and, later, an aging population.

**Development economics.** The study of why poor countries stay poor and what works to change it.

**Digital currencies.** Central-bank issued electronic money, under pilot in several countries. Distinct from stablecoins (private) and Bitcoin (an asset, not money).

**Fiat money.** Money with no backing but the issuer's promise and general acceptance.

**Fiscal policy.** Government spending and taxation used to influence the economy.

**Global financial crisis.** The 2007–09 crisis that began in US mortgages and became a run on institutions that had borrowed short to hold long, risky assets.

**Inclusive and extractive institutions.** Rules that spread rights and opportunities broadly, versus rules designed to take resources from the population for an elite.

**Inflation targeting.** A central bank's public commitment to a specific inflation rate, usually 2 percent, to anchor expectations.

**Institutions.** The rules, formal and informal, that structure economic life. The leading deep explanation of growth.

**Liquidity trap.** Interest rates at zero, so monetary policy can do no more.

**Lucas critique.** Any relationship that policy exploits will change once people understand the policy.

**Monetarism.** Friedman's doctrine that inflation comes from money growth and that central banks should target the money supply. Right about the long run; abandoned as a rule when velocity proved unstable.

**Money.** Whatever is generally accepted in payment. Defined by three jobs: **medium of exchange**, **unit of account**, and **store of value**. The **monetary base** is currency plus bank reserves; broad money adds deposits.

**Multiplier.** The total change in output per dollar of government spending. Textbook $1/(1-c)$, where $c$ is the **marginal propensity to consume**, the fraction of an extra dollar that gets spent; empirically 0.6 to 1 in normal times, larger in deep recessions.

**National income identity.** $Y = C + I + G + NX$: output equals consumption plus investment plus government purchases plus net exports.

**Natural rate of unemployment.** The unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation; never zero.

**Neutral real rate ($r^*$).** The interest rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. The starting point of the Taylor rule; it fell for forty years and may be rising.

**Non-rival.** A good one person's use of which does not reduce another's. Ideas are non-rival, which is why growth from ideas need not diminish.

**Okun's law.** Each point of unemployment above the natural rate costs about two percent of GDP.

**Output gap.** How far GDP is above or below its sustainable level, in percent.

**Proximate and deep causes.** What growth is made of (capital, education, ideas) versus why some societies accumulate them (institutions, geography, culture).

**Public debt and $r$ versus $g$.** What a government owes. If the interest rate $r$ is below the growth rate $g$, the debt ratio falls on its own; a government borrowing in its own currency risks inflation rather than default.

**Phillips curve.** The apparent trade-off between inflation and unemployment, which vanishes once inflation is expected.

**Purchasing power parity (PPP).** Converting currencies by what they buy rather than at market rates.

**Quantity equation.** $MV = PY$: money times velocity equals nominal GDP. Sustained inflation requires money growth.

**Quantitative easing.** Central bank purchases of long-term bonds to lower long-term rates when short rates are at zero.

**Rational expectations.** The assumption that people use all available information and understand how policy works.

**Real business cycle theory.** Models in which recessions are efficient responses to technology shocks and policy can only make things worse. Influential in the 1980s; absorbed and bounded since.

**Real interest rate.** The nominal rate minus expected inflation, a relation called the **Fisher equation**. What decisions depend on.

**Solow model and total factor productivity.** Output from capital, labor, and a productivity factor $A$; capital has diminishing returns, so growth from capital alone stops at a **steady state** where investment just replaces worn-out machines, and long-run growth is all $A$. Because $A$ is measured as whatever the inputs cannot explain, it is called the **Solow residual**. **Convergence**: poor countries growing faster as they catch up.

**Stagflation.** Rising inflation and rising unemployment together, as in the 1970s. Impossible on the original Phillips curve.

**State capacity.** A government's ability to collect taxes, enforce contracts, and deliver services. A form of wealth poor countries lack.

**Sticky prices.** Prices and wages that adjust slowly, letting demand shortfalls persist. The core of New Keynesian economics.

**Taylor rule.** A formula summarizing how central banks set rates in response to inflation and output gaps.

**Unemployment rate and labor force participation.** The share of the labor force out of work and looking; and the share of adults working or looking. The headline US rate is **U-3**; **U-6** adds discouraged workers and involuntary part-timers.

**Value added.** A firm's sales minus what it bought from other firms. Summed across all firms, it is GDP.

**Velocity of money.** How many times a dollar changes hands in a year; $V$ in $MV = PY$. Its instability is why money growth and inflation are loosely linked in the short run.

## The world (chapters 13 to 15)

**Beta and the capital asset pricing model.** How much an asset moves with the market; in the CAPM, the only risk that should be rewarded, because all other risk can be diversified away.

**Big push.** The postwar idea that a coordinated wave of aid-financed investment could lift a poor country out of its trap at once. Revived by Sachs; mixed record.

**Black–Scholes.** The 1973 formula for pricing options from the stock price, the option's terms, the interest rate, and volatility. Fails in crashes, which it treats as nearly impossible.

**Bond, stock, derivative, option, swap.** A loan; a share of ownership; a contract whose value depends on something else; the right to buy or sell at a set price; an agreement to exchange streams of payments.

**Bubble and irrational exuberance.** Prices rising on the expectation of further rises rather than on what the asset will produce. Becomes a crash through leverage. Shiller's phrase for the mood that drives it.

**Capital requirements.** Rules requiring banks to fund assets with their own money so they can absorb losses.

**China shock.** The concentrated, persistent local job losses in rich countries after China's entry into world trade.

**Diversification.** Holding many assets that do not move together, reducing risk without reducing expected return.

**Economies of scale and new trade theory.** Cheaper production per unit at larger scale; Krugman's explanation of why similar countries trade similar goods, for scale and variety.

**Efficient markets hypothesis.** Prices reflect all available information, so no analysis of public information reliably beats the market, and prices follow a **random walk**: moving only on news, which is unpredictable.

**Equity premium.** The extra return stocks have earned over safe bonds, about 5 percent a year, as compensation for risk.

**Exchange rate.** The price of one currency in another. **Floating** rates are set by the market; **fixed** rates by the central bank. **Interest parity**: money flows toward higher interest rates, pushing that currency up.

**Gravity model.** Trade between two countries rises with their sizes and falls with distance.

**Impossible trinity.** A country can have at most two of: fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, independent monetary policy.

**Index fund.** A fund holding every stock in a market at minimal cost. The practical conclusion of efficient markets.

**Industrial policy.** Government efforts to steer the economy toward particular sectors.

**Leverage.** Investing with borrowed money, which magnifies both gains and losses.

**Microfinance.** Small loans to poor entrepreneurs. Modest effects in trials.

**Private credit.** Loans made directly to companies by investment funds, outside banking regulation.

**Reshoring and friend-shoring.** Moving supply chains back home, or to allied countries, for resilience or security at some cost in efficiency.

**Resource curse.** The tendency of resource-rich countries to grow slowly, because resource revenue frees rulers from accountability.

**Stablecoin.** A digital token pegged to a currency and backed by safe assets. Subject to run risk.

**Tariff.** A tax on imports. Raises domestic prices, helps producers, hurts consumers by more, and invites retaliation.

**Trade deficit.** Importing more than exporting; exactly matched by a capital inflow and equal to the gap between saving and investment.

## The frontier (chapter 16)

**Credibility revolution.** The shift since 1990 to research designs that mimic experiments.

**Difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables.** Comparing a treated group with a similar untreated one before and after; comparing just above and just below a cutoff; using something that affects the cause but not the outcome except through the cause.

**External validity.** Whether a finding in one setting holds in another.

**Social cost of carbon.** The dollar damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide. Sets the right carbon price; estimates range from about \$50 to over \$1,000.
