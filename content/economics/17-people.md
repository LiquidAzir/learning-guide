## How to use this chapter

Economics is unusual among sciences in how much of it bears individual names: Smith's invisible hand, Nash equilibrium, the Phillips curve, the Coase theorem. This roster is organized by era; each entry gives the central contribution and the chapter where it appears. Where credit was shared or contested, the entry says so. The Nobel Memorial Prize, awarded since 1969, is noted because it marks what the profession judged to matter; its omissions, above all Joan Robinson, are noted too.

## Before economics (to 1770)

**Aristotle** (384–322 BCE). Distinguished use value from exchange value and disapproved of trade for profit; framed the questions for two thousand years. Chapter 3.

**Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274). The just price and the prohibition of usury: the medieval ethics of exchange. Chapter 3.

**Thomas Mun** (1571–1641). The clearest statement of mercantilism: wealth is bullion, trade is a contest. Chapter 3.

**François Quesnay** (1694–1774). The *Tableau économique*, the first model of an economy as a circular flow; his circle made *laissez faire* a slogan. Chapter 3.

**David Hume** (1711–1776). Showed that a country hoarding gold would suffer rising prices until the gold flowed out again, demolishing mercantilism's logic before Smith. Chapter 3.

## The classical era (1770–1870)

**Adam Smith** (1723–1790). *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): wealth is consumption, growth comes from the division of labor, and competition harnesses self-interest. Also a moral philosopher who distrusted merchants. Chapters 1, 3, 4.

**Thomas Malthus** (1766–1834). Population grows faster than food, so living standards return to subsistence. Right for all history before 1800, wrong after. Chapters 3, 10.

**David Ricardo** (1772–1823). Comparative advantage, the theory of rent, and the model of a rigorous economic argument. Chapters 3, 13.

**Jean-Baptiste Say** (1767–1832). Say's law: supply creates its own demand, so general gluts are impossible. Keynes wrote the *General Theory* against it. Chapter 12.

**John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873). Systematized classical economics and added the theory of international prices; also a philosopher of liberty and an early feminist. Chapter 3.

**Karl Marx** (1818–1883). Pushed the labor theory of value to a theory of exploitation and a prediction of collapse. The price theory failed; the analysis of capitalism's dynamism endures. Chapter 3.

## The Marginal Revolution and its heirs (1870–1930)

**William Stanley Jevons** (1835–1882), **Carl Menger** (1840–1921), **Léon Walras** (1834–1910). Independently located value in marginal utility, solving the water-diamond paradox and turning economics mathematical. Walras posed the general equilibrium question. Chapters 3, 4.

**Alfred Marshall** (1842–1924). *Principles of Economics* (1890): the supply-and-demand diagram, elasticity, consumer surplus, and the vocabulary of the subject. Chapters 2, 3.

**Vilfredo Pareto** (1848–1923). Pareto efficiency: a state from which no one can gain without another losing. The standard of efficiency in welfare economics. Chapter 4.

**Irving Fisher** (1867–1947). The quantity equation of money, the distinction between real and nominal interest, and debt deflation. Chapters 9, 11.

**Arthur Pigou** (1877–1959). Externalities and the tax that corrects them. Chapter 5.

**Thorstein Veblen** (1857–1929). Conspicuous consumption and the critique of the rational consumer, half a century before behavioral economics. Chapters 3, 6.

## The Keynesian era (1930–1970)

**John Maynard Keynes** (1883–1946). *The General Theory* (1936): aggregate demand, the multiplier, the liquidity trap, and the case for fiscal policy. Also co-negotiated the postwar monetary system at Bretton Woods, where the American plan largely prevailed over his. Chapters 3, 12.

**Joan Robinson** (1903–1983). Imperfect competition (1933), independently of Chamberlin; monopsony, a term she coined; and a leading Keynesian. Never awarded the Nobel Prize, an omission the profession still discusses. Chapters 3, 8.

**Friedrich Hayek** (1899–1992). Prices as a system for transmitting dispersed knowledge; the case against planning. Nobel 1974. Chapter 4.

**Simon Kuznets** (1901–1985). Built the first national income accounts and warned against reading welfare into them. Nobel 1971. Chapter 9.

**John von Neumann** (1903–1957) and **Oskar Morgenstern** (1902–1977). *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1944): game theory and expected utility. Chapters 6, 7.

**Paul Samuelson** (1915–2009). Formalized much of modern economics: public goods, the Stolper–Samuelson theorem, and the textbook that taught the postwar world. First American Nobel laureate in economics, 1970. Chapters 5, 13.

**Kenneth Arrow** (1921–2017) and **Gérard Debreu** (1921–2004). Proved separately (1951) that competitive equilibrium is efficient and together (1954) that it exists, making the invisible hand a theorem and listing its assumptions. Nobel 1972 and 1983. Chapter 4.

**Robert Solow** (1924–2023). The growth model (1956) and the finding that most growth is productivity, not capital. Nobel 1987. Chapter 10.

**John Nash** (1928–2015). Nash equilibrium (1950), the concept that made game theory general. Nobel 1994. Chapter 7.

**Herbert Simon** (1916–2001). Bounded rationality: people use rules of thumb because they must. Nobel 1978. Chapter 6.

**Ronald Coase** (1910–2013). Why firms exist (1937) and why externalities are a transaction-cost problem (1960). Nobel 1991. Chapters 5, 8.

**A. W. Phillips** (1914–1975). The Phillips curve (1958), the trade-off that vanished. Chapter 11.

**Thomas Schelling** (1921–2016). Commitment, credibility, and focal points in strategy. Nobel 2005. Chapter 7.

**Gary Becker** (1930–2014). Applied economic reasoning to discrimination, crime, family, and human capital. Nobel 1992. Chapter 8.

## The counterrevolution and synthesis (1960–2000)

**Milton Friedman** (1912–2006). Monetarism, the natural rate of unemployment, the monetary explanation of the Depression (with Anna Schwartz), and the most influential public advocate of free markets. Nobel 1976. Chapters 11, 12.

**Anna Schwartz** (1915–2012). Co-author of *A Monetary History of the United States*; her archival work underpinned monetarism. Chapter 12.

**Robert Lucas** (1937–2023). Rational expectations and the Lucas critique: any exploited relationship breaks. Nobel 1995. Chapters 11, 12.

**Edmund Phelps** (b. 1933). Independently of Friedman, the expectations-augmented Phillips curve. Nobel 2006. Chapter 11.

**Finn Kydland** (b. 1943) and **Edward Prescott** (1940–2022). Time inconsistency, the case for central bank independence, and real business cycle theory. Nobel 2004. Chapters 11, 12.

**George Akerlof** (b. 1940), **Michael Spence** (b. 1943), **Joseph Stiglitz** (b. 1943). Information economics: lemons, signaling, screening. Nobel 2001. Chapter 5.

**James Buchanan** (1919–2013) and **Gordon Tullock** (1922–2014). Public choice: governments fail too, and rent-seeking is why. Buchanan Nobel 1986. Chapter 5.

**Elinor Ostrom** (1933–2012). Communities can govern their commons without market or state. First woman to win the economics Nobel, 2009. Chapter 5.

**Daniel Kahneman** (1934–2024) and **Amos Tversky** (1937–1996). Prospect theory and the catalogue of cognitive biases; the founding of behavioral economics. Kahneman Nobel 2002. Chapter 6.

**Richard Thaler** (b. 1945). Mental accounting, the endowment effect, and nudges. Nobel 2017. Chapter 6.

**Eugene Fama** (b. 1939) and **Robert Shiller** (b. 1946). Efficient markets and their limits; shared the 2013 Nobel, with Lars Peter Hansen, from opposite sides. Chapter 15.

**Harry Markowitz** (1927–2023), **William Sharpe** (b. 1934), **Fischer Black** (1938–1995), **Myron Scholes** (b. 1941), **Robert Merton** (b. 1944). Portfolio theory, the capital asset pricing model, and option pricing: the mathematics of modern finance. Chapter 15.

**Paul Krugman** (b. 1953). New trade theory (1979) and economic geography. Nobel 2008. Chapter 13.

**Robert Mundell** (1932–2021). The impossible trinity and the theory of optimum currency areas, which framed the euro debate. Nobel 1999. Chapter 13.

**Paul Romer** (b. 1955). Endogenous growth: ideas are non-rival and growth can sustain itself. Nobel 2018. Chapter 10.

**Douglass North** (1920–2015). Institutions as the deep determinant of growth. Nobel 1993. Chapter 10.

**Hyman Minsky** (1919–1996). Stability breeds instability; financial fragility as the engine of crisis. Ignored until 2008. Chapter 12.

**Amartya Sen** (b. 1933). Famines as failures of entitlement, not food; capabilities as the measure of development. Nobel 1998. Chapter 14.

## The credibility revolution and the present (1990–)

**David Card** (b. 1956) and **Alan Krueger** (1960–2019). The New Jersey minimum wage study (1994) and the natural-experiment method it exemplified. Card Nobel 2021. Chapters 4, 16.

**Joshua Angrist** (b. 1960) and **Guido Imbens** (b. 1963). The methodology of instrumental variables and causal inference from natural experiments. Nobel 2021. Chapter 16.

**Abhijit Banerjee** (b. 1961), **Esther Duflo** (b. 1972), **Michael Kremer** (b. 1964). Randomized controlled trials in development. Nobel 2019; Duflo the youngest laureate in the field. Chapter 14.

**Daron Acemoglu** (b. 1967), **Simon Johnson** (b. 1963), **James Robinson** (b. 1960). The colonial-origins evidence that institutions cause prosperity. Nobel 2024. Chapter 10.

**Philippe Aghion** (b. 1956), **Peter Howitt** (b. 1946), **Joel Mokyr** (b. 1946). Creative destruction as the engine of growth, and the history of why it began. Nobel 2025. Chapter 10.

**Ben Bernanke** (b. 1953), **Douglas Diamond** (b. 1953), **Philip Dybvig** (b. 1955). Bank runs, deposit insurance, and the role of bank failures in the Depression. Nobel 2022; Bernanke also chaired the Fed through 2008. Chapters 11, 12.

**Claudia Goldin** (b. 1946). Two centuries of women's work; the child penalty as the source of the remaining gender gap. Nobel 2023. Chapter 8.

**Thomas Piketty** (b. 1971), **Emmanuel Saez** (b. 1972), **Gabriel Zucman** (b. 1986). Top-income shares from tax data and the argument that capital returns drive inequality. Their US estimates are disputed. Chapter 8.

**Raj Chetty** (b. 1979). Intergenerational mobility mapped from tax records; the causal effect of neighborhoods. Chapter 8.

**David Autor** (b. 1967), **David Dorn**, **Gordon Hanson** (b. 1964). The China shock: the concentrated, persistent local costs of trade. Chapter 13.

**Alvin Roth** (b. 1951) and **Lloyd Shapley** (1923–2016). Matching markets without prices: schools, residencies, kidneys. Nobel 2012. Chapter 7.

**Paul Milgrom** (b. 1948) and **Robert Wilson** (b. 1937). Auction theory and, with Preston McAfee, the spectrum auction design. Nobel 2020. Chapter 7.

**William Nordhaus** (b. 1941). Integrated climate-economy models and the social cost of carbon. Nobel 2018. Chapter 16.

**Olivier Blanchard** (b. 1948). Public debt when interest rates are below growth; the 2021 inflation postmortem with Bernanke. Chapters 11, 12.

## The pattern

Three things stand out in this list. The first is how often the decisive idea was a correction of the previous consensus: Smith against mercantilism, the marginalists against the labor theory, Keynes against Say, Friedman against Keynes, Kahneman against the rational model, the credibility revolution against everyone's regressions. Economics advances by argument, and its history is a history of being productively wrong. The second is how recently its evidence became credible: almost every empirical finding cited in this guide is from after 1990. The third is who is missing. Joan Robinson never received the prize; only three women have; and the roster of laureates is overwhelmingly American and European, in a discipline whose most important recent facts are about Asia and Africa.
