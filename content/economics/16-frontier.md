## Recap

You have the map. This chapter walks the edge: the methods that changed the field, the failures that humbled it, and the questions it is working on now. Unlike physics, economics' frontier is less about undiscovered laws than about hard measurement, contested values, and a world that changes faster than the data.

## The credibility revolution

The single largest change in economics since 1990 is in how it establishes facts. For most of the century, empirical economics meant fitting equations to whatever data existed and hoping the controls were adequate. The results were fragile: the same data, in different hands, gave opposite answers about the minimum wage, about schooling, about almost everything. Edward Leamer's 1983 essay "Let's Take the Con Out of Econometrics" described the practice as a search for the specification that confirmed what the author already believed.[^1]

The response, now called the **credibility revolution**, was to insist on a source of variation that mimics an experiment. Joshua Angrist, David Card, and Guido Imbens received the 2021 Nobel Prize for developing it.[^2] The main designs, each of which appears somewhere in this guide:

**Randomized controlled trials** assign a treatment by lottery, so treated and untreated groups differ only by chance. The gold standard where feasible; the development chapter is built on them.

**Difference-in-differences** compares a group that experienced a change with a similar group that did not, before and after. Card and Krueger's minimum wage study (chapter 4) compared New Jersey with Pennsylvania. The assumption is that the two would have moved in parallel absent the change.

**Regression discontinuity** exploits a sharp cutoff: students just above and just below a test-score threshold for a scholarship are nearly identical except for the scholarship, so comparing them isolates its effect.

**Instrumental variables** find something that affects the cause but not the outcome except through the cause. Angrist used the Vietnam draft lottery, which affected military service but nothing else about a man, to measure what service did to later earnings.[^3] Acemoglu and colleagues used settler mortality as an instrument for institutions (chapter 10).

:::key
Every design rests on an assumption that cannot be fully tested: that the comparison group is what the treated group would have been. The revolution did not make causal claims certain. It made the assumptions explicit, so they can be argued about, and it raised the bar for what counts as evidence. A modern economics paper is judged first on whether its design is credible and only then on its result. That is a real advance, and it is why this guide could cite evidence rather than opinion on rent control, minimum wages, trade, aid, and nudges.
:::

The revolution has costs. It favors questions that have a natural experiment over questions that matter, so the field knows more about the effects of class size than about the causes of growth. And the results are local: a minimum wage study in New Jersey in 1992 may not transfer to California in 2024, as chapter 8's dispute shows. **External validity**, whether a finding holds elsewhere, is the current frontier of methods.

## What economics got wrong

Honesty requires the list.

**Forecasting.** Economists did not predict the 2008 crisis, the 2021 inflation, or most recessions, and the profession's forecasting record is poor.[^4] Some of this is inherent: an economy that responds to forecasts cannot be forecast like weather. Some was a failure of models that omitted finance. The field has become more modest about prediction and more focused on understanding mechanisms after the fact, which is less satisfying and more honest.

**Overconfidence in consensus.** The pre-2008 confidence that financial innovation had made the system safer, the 1990s confidence that liberalizing capital flows was always good, the 2000s confidence that trade adjustment would be smooth: each was a consensus among experts that turned out to be wrong in ways that hurt people. The China shock evidence (chapter 13) overturned a consensus the profession had held for decades.

**Replication.** A 2016 attempt to replicate eighteen laboratory experiments from top economics journals succeeded for eleven.[^5] That is better than psychology's record and worse than it should be. The field has since adopted pre-registration and data-sharing requirements at its major journals, and several celebrated findings have shrunk or vanished on re-examination.

**Politics.** Economics has a direct line to power and has sometimes used it badly, lending credibility to policies its evidence did not support. The austerity of 2010 to 2012 leaned on a study whose central claim collapsed when its data were re-examined (chapter 12). Economists' surveyed views on policy are more united and more moderate than the public debate suggests, but the public hears the outliers.

## The live debates

These are the questions serious economists disagree about in 2026, with the state of the evidence.

**Artificial intelligence and work.** The most-studied question in the field right now. Daron Acemoglu estimates that current AI will raise productivity by under 1 percent over a decade, because most tasks are not yet automatable; optimists argue AI accelerates the production of ideas and so raises the growth rate itself.[^6] The workplace evidence: a generative AI assistant raised the output of customer-service agents by 14 percent, with the least experienced gaining most; Danish workers who adopted chatbots saw no change in earnings after two years; and by mid-2026, employment of the youngest workers in the most AI-exposed occupations had fallen about 19 percent relative to their peers, driven by reduced hiring rather than layoffs.[^7] Whether this is the start of something large or another round of automation the labor market will absorb, as it has every previous one, is the question of the decade, and nobody's answer should be trusted yet.

**Inequality.** How much has it risen? The two leading teams using the same tax records disagree by a factor of several, because a third of national income does not appear on individual returns and must be allocated by assumption (chapter 8). What to do about it divides on values as much as evidence.

**Climate.** The **social cost of carbon**, the damage done by one more ton of emissions, is the number that sets the right carbon price, and estimates range from about \$50 to over \$1,000 per ton depending on how damages are measured and how the future is discounted.[^8] The framework for such estimates is William Nordhaus's DICE model, which since 1992 has joined a simple climate model to a growth model so that emissions, warming, damages, and the cost of cutting emissions are weighed in one place; it earned him the 2018 Nobel Prize, and his own estimates sat at the low end of the range, which is where much of the argument lies.[^13] The US government's central estimate rose to \$190 in 2023 and was withdrawn from regulatory use in 2025. A 2024 paper in *Nature* claiming that past emissions had committed the world to a 19 percent income loss by 2050 was retracted in 2025 after its results were traced to bad data for a single country.[^9] Carbon pricing itself has a clearer record: a 2024 meta-analysis of 21 real schemes found emissions cuts of 5 to 21 percent.[^10]

**Debt.** Rich-country public debt is near postwar records and interest costs are rising; the IMF projects global government debt reaching 100 percent of world GDP by 2029.[^11] Whether this is sustainable depends on interest rates relative to growth, which nobody can forecast, and on political credibility, which is not a number.

**Industrial policy.** After decades of consensus against it, subsidies for semiconductors, batteries, and clean energy are back in the United States and Europe. The newest evidence is more favorable than the old, but the record includes as many expensive failures as successes (chapter 13).

**Housing.** Rents and prices in productive cities have risen far faster than incomes across the rich world. The evidence increasingly points at supply restrictions: where cities have loosened zoning, as Auckland did in 2016, construction rose sharply and stayed high.[^12] The policy is unpopular with existing owners, which is the problem.

**Immigration.** The evidence that immigrants lower native wages is weak overall and disputed at the low-skill margin; the evidence that migration raises the incomes of migrants and world output is strong (chapter 14). The gap between what economists find and what voters believe is unusually wide here.

## How to read an economic headline

Economics headlines fail in predictable ways. A checklist, adapted from the ones in the physics and chemistry guides:

1. **Compared to what?** Every cost is relative to an alternative. A policy that "costs \$50 billion" costs it relative to something; ask what.
2. **Real or nominal?** Any dollar figure compared across years without an inflation adjustment is misleading. "Record high" spending, wages, or debt in nominal dollars is nearly always a record because prices rose.
3. **Correlation or cause?** Ask what the counterfactual is and how the study identified it. If the answer is "countries with more X have more Y," it is a question, not an answer.
4. **Whose average?** A rise in average income is consistent with most people getting poorer. Ask about the distribution.
5. **Who loses?** Every policy has losers. A report that finds none is not a report.
6. **How strong is the evidence, and who else has found it?** As in the sciences: one study is a claim, replication is a result, and the Latest Research section marks each entry as confirmed, preliminary, disputed, or retracted for exactly this reason.
7. **Is it a forecast?** If so, discount it. The profession's record on forecasting is poor, and honest economists say so.

:::frontier
The field's methods are better than they have ever been, its data are larger, and its humility, after 2008 and 2021, is greater. Its central limitation is unchanged: it studies people who read the studies. That makes economics permanently provisional, and it means the most valuable thing the subject offers is not answers but a disciplined way of asking, which this guide has tried to teach. The Latest Research section is where the asking continues.
:::

## Summary

- The credibility revolution replaced argument with designs that mimic experiments: RCTs, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables. Assumptions are now explicit and arguable.
- Economics has been wrong about forecasts, about pre-2008 finance, about trade adjustment, and in some replications; it has responded with more modesty and better methods.
- Live debates: AI and work, the size of the rise in inequality, the social cost of carbon, debt sustainability, industrial policy, housing supply, immigration. Each has real evidence and real disagreement.
- Read headlines by asking compared to what, real or nominal, correlation or cause, whose average, who loses, who else found it, and whether it is a forecast.
