## Recap

Chapter 9 defined recessions and noted that they recur. Chapter 11 gave central banks their tools. This chapter asks why an economy that was working stops working, and what the century since the Great Depression has taught about the answer. It is the most contested chapter in the guide, because it is where economics has been most publicly wrong and where it has learned the most.

## The classical view and its failure

Before 1930 most economists believed that markets clear. If workers are unemployed, wages fall until firms hire them; if goods go unsold, prices fall until they sell. A downturn corrects itself. Recessions were understood as temporary adjustments, and the government's job was to balance its budget and let the correction proceed.

Then the correction did not proceed. From 1929 to 1933 US output fell by a quarter, prices fell by a quarter, and unemployment rose to 25 percent, and it stayed high for a decade.[^1] Wages did fall. It did not help. The classical model had no room for an economy sitting, year after year, with idle factories and idle workers who wanted to work.

## Keynes

John Maynard Keynes's *General Theory* of 1936 offered an explanation and a remedy.[^2] The explanation was **aggregate demand**: the total spending in the economy on consumption, investment, and government purchases. If spending falls, firms sell less, cut production and jobs, workers earn less and spend less, and the fall feeds on itself. Falling wages do not fix this, because lower wages mean lower spending. The economy can settle into an equilibrium with mass unemployment, and there is no automatic force to lift it out.

The remedy followed. If private spending has collapsed, public spending can replace it, and the effect is larger than the spending itself.

:::math The multiplier
When the government spends an extra dollar, the people who receive it spend part of it, the people who receive that spend part again, and so on. If each round spends a fraction $c$ (the **marginal propensity to consume**) and saves the rest, the total increase in spending is
$$\Delta Y = \frac{1}{1 - c}\,\Delta G$$
With $c = 0.8$, the **multiplier** $1/(1-c)$ is 5: a dollar of spending raises output by five. That is the textbook version, and it is too large, because it ignores taxes, imports, and the central bank's response. Empirical estimates of the multiplier for US government spending mostly run between 0.6 and 1 in normal times, with some estimates near 2 in deep recessions when resources are idle and interest rates are stuck at zero; in booms the extra spending mostly bids up prices.[^3] The disagreement over that range is the disagreement over fiscal policy.
:::

Keynes also identified the **liquidity trap**: when interest rates have fallen to zero, the central bank can do no more, and only fiscal policy is left. The idea seemed antique for fifty years. Then Japan hit zero in 1999 and the rest of the rich world in 2008.

:::story Japan's lost decades
Japan in 1989 was the economy everyone expected to dominate the century. Its stock market and land prices had roughly tripled in five years; at the peak, the grounds of the Imperial Palace were said to be worth more than all the real estate in California. Then the Bank of Japan raised rates, the bubble burst, and the economy did not recover. Banks carrying loans secured on collapsed land values stopped lending; firms spent a decade paying down debt rather than investing; prices began to fall, and falling prices made debts heavier in real terms and gave everyone a reason to wait before spending. The Bank of Japan cut its rate to zero in 1999, invented quantitative easing in 2001, and still could not lift inflation to its target for twenty years.[^15] Japan is the case that revived Keynes's liquidity trap, showed that a country borrowing in its own currency can carry debt above 200 percent of GDP without a crisis, and taught the rest of the world what to expect after 2008. It was also less of a catastrophe than the headlines: output per working-age person kept growing at nearly the American pace; what shrank was the number of working-age people.
:::

## The counterrevolution

Keynesian economics dominated policy from the 1940s to the 1970s and then met the same fate as the Phillips curve (chapter 11). Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued in 1963 that the Depression was not a failure of private demand but of the Federal Reserve, which let the money supply fall by a third as banks collapsed; had the Fed acted, they said, the downturn would have been ordinary.[^4] The **monetarist** conclusion was that monetary policy was powerful and fiscal policy unnecessary, and the 1970s stagflation, which Keynesian models had not predicted, made the case for them.

Robert Lucas went further. If people have **rational expectations**, meaning they use all available information and understand how policy works, then systematic policy cannot fool them, and Keynesian demand management is at best useless and at worst destabilizing. Edward Prescott and Finn Kydland built **real business cycle** models in which recessions were not failures at all but efficient responses to shocks in technology, and government intervention could only make things worse.[^5] For a time in the 1980s the leading research program treated recessions as efficient responses to shocks, and at several departments Keynesian macroeconomics was no longer taught as live theory.

## The synthesis

The extremes did not survive contact with data. Prices and wages demonstrably do not adjust instantly; contracts, menus, and social norms make them **sticky**, and sticky prices are all it takes for demand to matter in the short run. By the 1990s a **New Keynesian** synthesis had formed: Lucas's insistence on expectations and microfoundations, combined with Keynes's insight that sticky prices let demand shortfalls persist.[^6] In this framework monetary policy is the first line of defense, fiscal policy matters when rates are at zero, and the central bank's job is to manage expectations. It is the view most central banks hold today, embodied in large models called DSGE (dynamic stochastic general equilibrium: models that trace how forward-looking households and firms respond to random shocks, with every market clearing) that they use for forecasting. Those models had one feature that would prove costly: most of them had no banks in them.

## 2008

The **global financial crisis** began in the American housing market. From 2000 to 2006 house prices rose 90 percent, financed by mortgages of declining quality bundled into securities that rating agencies certified as safe and banks worldwide bought with borrowed money.[^7] When prices turned in 2006, the securities lost value, nobody knew who held how much, and the banks that had funded themselves with overnight loans faced the Diamond–Dybvig run of chapter 11, this time not by depositors but by other institutions. Lehman Brothers failed on 15 September 2008; credit markets froze; world trade fell 15 percent in six months.

The response combined every school. Central banks cut rates to zero and, in the largest lender-of-last-resort operation in history, lent trillions against collateral no one else would take. Governments recapitalized banks (the US Troubled Asset Relief Program) and passed fiscal stimulus of about 5 percent of GDP in the United States and comparable amounts elsewhere.[^8] The Depression did not recur: US unemployment peaked at 10 percent rather than 25, and output regained its previous peak in three years rather than eight. Bernanke, the Fed chair, was a scholar of the 1930s and said afterward that the lesson of Friedman and Schwartz had been applied.

The recovery was slow, and the slowness produced the decade's argument. Keynesians argued the stimulus had been too small and withdrawn too soon; the evidence on multipliers at the zero bound, mostly from careful comparisons of spending across regions, came in near the high end of the range.[^9] Critics of stimulus pointed to rising debt and invoked a 2010 study finding that growth slowed sharply once public debt passed 90 percent of GDP; in 2013 a graduate student, Thomas Herndon, working with two professors, found a spreadsheet error, excluded data, and an unusual weighting scheme; corrected, growth above the 90 percent line averaged 2.2 percent rather than the reported −0.1, and the threshold disappeared.[^10] The **austerity** adopted by several European governments in 2010 to 2012, cutting spending and raising taxes during a downturn, was followed by a second recession there. Most macroeconomists now judge that consolidation to have been too fast, though defenders note that some countries had lost access to markets and had little choice, and the evidence that multipliers are much larger at the zero bound, mostly from cross-regional studies, is stronger than the evidence from aggregate data.

:::story The eurozone crisis
The euro's crisis of 2010 to 2012 ties three chapters together. Nineteen countries shared one currency and one central bank but kept nineteen separate budgets, which meant a government in trouble could neither devalue (chapter 13) nor count on its central bank to buy its debt. When Greece revealed in 2009 that its deficit was far larger than reported, investors began to ask which other countries might default, and the interest rates on Irish, Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian debt rose to levels that made default more likely, a self-fulfilling run on governments rather than banks. The rescue packages came with austerity, and the austerity deepened the recessions: Greek output fell by a quarter, as much as America's in the Depression. The run ended in July 2012 when Mario Draghi, the ECB's president, said the bank would do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro; the promise alone brought rates down, because a lender of last resort that is believed rarely has to lend.[^16] The lesson economists drew was the one Bagehot had drawn in 1873: a currency needs a backstop, and a currency union without a fiscal partner is fragile.
:::

The deeper lesson was about finance. Hyman Minsky had argued in the 1980s that stability breeds instability: long calm periods encourage more leverage until the system becomes fragile, and the models that ignored this had ignored the mechanism of the crisis.[^11] Banks, leverage, and financial fragility have since been built into the standard models, and bank capital requirements were roughly tripled.

## Fiscal policy and debt

Governments have two levers. **Automatic stabilizers** work without anyone deciding: in a recession tax receipts fall and unemployment payments rise, so the deficit widens and cushions the drop, and the reverse in a boom. **Discretionary stimulus** is deliberate: tax cuts, transfers, or spending passed in response to a downturn. The first is fast and reliable; the second is slower and its size is the multiplier fight above.

Both add to **public debt**, and how much debt is too much is the other permanent argument. The arithmetic turns on two rates. If the interest rate the government pays, $r$, is below the economy's growth rate, $g$, then the debt-to-GDP ratio falls on its own even with a modest deficit, because the economy outgrows the debt. Olivier Blanchard pointed out in 2019 that $r < g$ had been the normal condition in the United States for decades, so debt was cheaper than the conventional wisdom assumed.[^12] Since 2022, interest rates have risen and the condition is no longer comfortably met; the US Congressional Budget Office projects debt rising from 100 percent of GDP to about 175 percent by 2056 under current law, with interest costs exceeding defense spending.[^13] Nobody knows the level at which markets balk, because it depends on a country's credibility, and countries that borrow in their own currency (the United States, Japan) have far more room than those that do not (Greece, Argentina). A government that borrows in its own currency can always create the money to pay; the risk it runs is inflation, not default. A government that borrows in someone else's currency, or in a shared one, can run out, and when investors suspect it might, they demand higher rates, which makes the running out more likely: the bank run of chapter 11 with a country as the bank. Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's eight-century record of such episodes finds defaults clustering after credit booms and capital inflows, and finds, to the embarrassment of every generation, that each was told "this time is different."[^17] Japan has carried debt above 200 percent of GDP for years without crisis; Greece defaulted at 170.

## The pandemic

The 2020 recession was unlike any other: a deliberate shutdown, with output falling 10 percent in two months and recovering nearly as fast. The policy response was also unprecedented in size, about 25 percent of GDP in the United States across three fiscal packages, plus rates at zero and asset purchases.[^14] It worked in the sense that mattered most: no wave of bankruptcies, no banking crisis, and employment back to its previous level within three years, against more than six after 2008. It also contributed to the inflation of 2021 to 2023, by how much being the subject of chapter 11's postmortems. The current judgment is that the response was right in direction and probably too large in the final round, which is the kind of judgment economics can now make with evidence rather than ideology.

:::key
The century's arc runs from "recessions cure themselves" to "spending cures recessions" to "nothing cures recessions" to the present position. Recessions have causes that can be identified. Monetary policy is the first response and works unless rates are at zero; fiscal policy works too, and especially then. Financial fragility can turn a downturn into a catastrophe and must be watched. And all of it depends on expectations, which depend on credibility. Each step was learned from a disaster.
:::

## Summary

- Classical economics said markets clear; the Depression showed they can stay stuck for a decade.
- Keynes: aggregate demand can fall short and stay short; spending has a multiplier; at zero interest rates only fiscal policy is left.
- Monetarists blamed the Fed for the Depression; rational-expectations theorists argued policy cannot systematically help. Both were partly right and both overreached.
- The New Keynesian synthesis, sticky prices plus rational expectations, is the working framework. It omitted finance and 2008 exposed that.
- 2008 was a bank run in new clothes; the combined response prevented a depression; austerity afterward is widely judged too fast; multipliers at the zero bound are probably larger than in normal times. Japan's lost decades and the eurozone crisis are the two great case studies.
- Debt sustainability depends on $r$ versus $g$ and on credibility; the pandemic response was unprecedented, effective, and probably oversized.
