## Power and institutions

**State.** Durable organization of public authority over a territory and population.

**Government.** People and organizations exercising governing authority at a particular time.

**Regime.** Fundamental rules governing how political power is obtained and exercised.

**Institution.** Durable formal or informal rules and practices that structure behavior.

**State capacity.** Ability to implement decisions through resources, information, and organization.

**Legitimacy.** The perceived rightfulness of authority, distinct from popularity or coercive ability.

**Veto player.** An actor whose agreement is required to change a policy or arrangement.

**Federalism.** A constitutional distribution of authority across levels of government.

## Representation and cooperation

**Plurality.** Selecting the option with the most votes, which may be fewer than half.

**Condorcet winner.** An option that defeats every other option in pairwise majority comparisons; one need not exist.

**Proportional representation.** Electoral arrangements seeking a relationship between vote shares and seats, subject to particular formulas and thresholds.

**Principal–agent problem.** A difficulty in delegation arising from differing goals and information.

**Public good.** A good that is non-rival and difficult to exclude others from in the relevant setting.

**Common-pool resource.** A resource that is rival but difficult to exclude users from.

**Commitment problem.** Difficulty making a promise credible when future incentives may favor breaking it.

**Sortition.** Selection by lot; fairness and representation depend on recruitment and selection rules.

## Administration and evidence

**Administrative burden.** Learning, compliance, and psychological costs of interacting with public programs.

**Accountability.** Arrangements for demanding explanations, assigning responsibility, and applying consequences or correction.

**Counterfactual.** The outcome that would have occurred under an alternative condition.

**Identification.** The reasoning and assumptions connecting observed evidence to an estimated quantity, especially a causal effect.

**External validity.** Applicability of a finding beyond the studied setting and population.

**Spillover.** An effect of an intervention on people or units outside its directly assigned group.

**Normative claim.** A claim about what ought to be, requiring values as well as relevant factual information.

## Read a study in seven moves

1. State the institutional question without naming a favored outcome.
2. Identify the population, setting, and period.
3. Describe the intervention or comparison.
4. Identify the outcome and how it was measured.
5. State the result, its magnitude where available, and uncertainty.
6. List the strongest alternative explanation or limitation.
7. Explain what transfers to another setting and what still needs testing.

A study can inform a debate without resolving every value judgment in it. Preserve that distinction when reading and when sharing an entry from the feed.
