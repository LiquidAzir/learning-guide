## A rule nobody can implement

A government announces universal access to a service. It lacks trained staff, reliable records, and transport to remote communities. The rule exists, but the promised outcome does not. **State capacity** is the ability to carry out decisions, not simply the authority to announce them.

A **state** is a durable organization of public authority over a territory and population. A **government** is the set of people and organizations exercising governing power at a particular time. A **regime** comprises the fundamental rules determining how power is obtained and exercised. An election can change a government without replacing the state or regime.

## Power takes several forms

Power can involve coercion, control over resources, agenda-setting, or influence over what people consider possible. The ability to keep a proposal off the agenda can matter as much as the ability to win a visible vote.

**Legitimacy** concerns whether authority is regarded as rightful. It differs from popularity, effectiveness, and the ability to compel obedience. People may dislike a decision while accepting the procedure that produced it; they may also obey an authority they do not regard as legitimate because refusal is costly.

These distinctions prevent circular explanations. “The state succeeded because it was strong” explains little unless strength is measured through capacities that could have been assessed independently of that success.

## Capacity and constraint

Tax collection, record-keeping, infrastructure, staffing, and territorial reach enable government action. Oversight, rights, elections, and legal procedures constrain how those capacities are used. Capacity and accountability are separate dimensions: an effective administration may be poorly constrained, and a formally accountable administration may be unable to deliver.

Information creates a recurring problem. Central officials may know the law but not local conditions. Local officials may know the conditions but have incentives to conceal failure. Institutional design must address both knowledge and incentives.

## Formation and persistence

States developed through varied combinations of conflict, bargaining, commerce, colonization, administration, and collective provision. No single sequence describes every region. Historical explanations should distinguish how an institution originally arose from why it persists now. A rule can survive because current groups benefit from it even after its original purpose has vanished.[^1]

:::deeper A rule on paper needs an organization behind it

### Follow a routine public service

Imagine a state promises to register every birth. Doing so requires more than passing a law. Someone must reach families, verify information, maintain records, correct errors, protect access, and make the document useful to other institutions. Funding, trained staff, record systems, and public cooperation all contribute.

This example separates formal authority from practical capacity. A government can have legal power to require registration while lacking staff or reliable records. Another administration may be effective at registration but weak at collecting a different kind of tax. Capacity is often uneven across functions and territories.

### Compliance can have several sources

People may comply because a service is useful, because they regard the institution as legitimate, because others expect it, or because noncompliance carries a penalty. Observing compliance does not tell you which mechanism dominates. A process that works only under constant costly enforcement may operate differently when that enforcement is interrupted.

Legitimacy concerns beliefs about rightful authority. It is not identical to approval of every current policy or fear of punishment. A person can accept an institution's authority while criticizing its decisions, just as a highly popular decision can occur within an institution whose authority is contested.

### Separate the enduring organization from its current leadership

A government consists of the people and arrangements directing public power at a particular time. State institutions can persist across changes of government. A regime concerns the broader rules governing access to and exercise of power. These categories overlap in practice, but separating them prevents a leadership change from being mistaken automatically for an entirely new state.

When comparing cases, ask which function, territory, population, and period the evidence describes. A single national score can be a useful summary while concealing major differences in what the institution actually does.
:::

:::try Authority without capacity
A national agency has legal authority to inspect every water system but only enough trained staff to inspect one in twenty each year. Which distinction is most useful for explaining the gap?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Legal authority and administrative capacity. Expanding the agency's formal powers alone does not supply staff, information, equipment, or operating funds. Also ask how the scarce inspection capacity is allocated and monitored.
:::
