## A cheaper program that reaches nobody

A new application system reduces administrative spending. Participation also collapses among people with limited internet access. Whether the reform succeeded depends on the objective, the affected population, and which costs the evaluation counted.

A **theory of change** specifies the chain from intervention to outcome: information reaches people, they understand it, they act, services are delivered, and a relevant condition improves. Each link can fail. Measuring only the final outcome hides the mechanism; measuring only the first step can exaggerate success.

## Separate effectiveness from value judgments

An evaluation might estimate that a program increases completed applications by five percentage points. Deciding whether that is worthwhile requires costs, downstream effects, distribution, and alternatives. A causal estimate does not itself choose society's priorities.

**Cost-effectiveness analysis** compares costs per specified outcome. **Cost–benefit analysis** attempts to express diverse consequences in comparable value terms. Choices about valuation, discounting, and distribution need to be visible. A benefit concentrated among already advantaged people may raise a different normative question from the same total benefit spread more broadly.

## Implementation and scale

A pilot may rely on exceptional staff, volunteers, or intensive supervision. Scaling changes workload, labor markets, supply chains, and the population served. An intervention that works locally can have different effects when it becomes widespread.

**Spillovers** occur when an intervention affects people outside the assigned treatment group. They can be beneficial, such as shared information, or adverse, such as displacement of scarce services. A study design must consider the level at which treatment is assigned and outcomes are measured.

An Indonesian experiment on electronic benefit delivery examined whether expanding banking-agent access also increased use of other financial services. It found no detectable broader household financial-service effects in that setting. That is a useful result: delivering the intended transfer and transforming financial inclusion are distinct claims.[^1]

### Specify the outcome and the comparison

Suppose a tutoring pilot increases a defined assessment score relative to a randomized comparison group. That is evidence about the tested program, participants, implementation, and outcome. It does not yet establish the effect of offering the program to every school under a different staffing arrangement.

Participants in a pilot may volunteer, organizers may supply unusual support, and the number of experienced tutors may be limited. Expansion can change who participates, how consistently the program is delivered, and what happens to other services competing for the same staff.

### Cost-effectiveness requires a common unit

Imagine program A costs 100,000 units and produces 100 additional successful outcomes, while program B costs 120,000 and produces 160. Average cost per additional outcome is 1,000 for A and 750 for B. If the outcomes are genuinely comparable, B looks more cost-effective on that measure.

But moving from A to B costs an additional 20,000 for 60 additional outcomes: about 333 per additional outcome at that margin. Average and incremental comparisons answer different questions. Neither calculation establishes affordability, distributional fairness, or the value of effects omitted from the outcome measure.

### Look beyond the participant average

A program can improve an average while benefiting groups differently. It can also affect nonparticipants: a placement service might help its clients partly by displacing other applicants. Whether that is a material concern depends on the policy's objective and the wider labor market, not only the participant result.

Track implementation, uncertainty, side effects, and distribution alongside the central estimate. A good evaluation should make it easier to decide what to continue, modify, or stop. It should not require treating one successful pilot as proof of a universal policy solution.
:::
