## Nobody has half the seats

In a 100-seat legislature, parties hold 45, 35, and 20 seats. A majority requires 51. Every pair could form a majority, but that arithmetic does not predict which pair will govern. Policy differences, leadership rules, credibility, and the value of remaining in opposition affect negotiations.

**Political parties** organize candidates, coordinate legislative action, and present policy packages to voters. They reduce some information costs while bundling choices people might prefer to make separately. Supporting a party does not imply agreeing with every item in its platform.

## Representation has several meanings

**Descriptive representation** concerns resemblance between representatives and represented groups. **Substantive representation** concerns action on their interests. **Procedural representation** concerns authorization and accountability through rules. These can support one another but are not interchangeable.

A representative may act as a **delegate**, following constituents' stated preferences, or as a **trustee**, exercising judgment. In practice, both models face a question: whose preferences count when constituents disagree or when effects fall on people outside the constituency?

## Delegation creates an information problem

In a **principal–agent relationship**, one party delegates a task to another. Voters cannot continuously observe officials; officials cannot continuously observe every administrator. Agents may have different information and goals. Monitoring, reporting, incentives, and removal procedures attempt to address the resulting gap.

An election offers periodic accountability, but voters must infer responsibility for outcomes affected by many institutions and outside events. Coalition governments can share responsibility; divided institutions can blame one another. Institutional clarity matters for whether a sanction rewards or punishes the actor actually responsible.

## Collective organization is uneven

A small group with concentrated stakes may organize more easily than a large group with small individual stakes. That does not mean small groups always prevail. Organization costs, public attention, resources, institutions, and counter-mobilization shape influence.[^1]

Treat “the public wants” as a claim requiring measurement. Question wording, issue salience, knowledge, and trade-offs affect responses. A preference expressed without a cost may change when the cost becomes explicit.
