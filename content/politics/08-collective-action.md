## Everybody wants the flood barrier

A barrier protects the neighborhood whether or not each household contributes. That creates a **free-rider problem**: a person may prefer to benefit from others' payments. If everyone reasons that way, a project they jointly value may never be built.

A **public good** is non-rival and non-excludable in the relevant setting: one person's use does not significantly reduce another's, and excluding non-payers is difficult. A **common-pool resource**, such as a fishery, is difficult to exclude people from but rival: one catch reduces what remains available.

These are properties of a good under particular technologies and rules, not labels for everything government provides. Congestion can change rivalry, and technology can change exclusion.

## A small contribution game

Four people can each contribute 10 units. Every contribution generates 16 units of benefit spread equally among the four. A contributor pays 10 and receives 4 from their own contribution, so the individual immediate net return is −6. Yet if all contribute, everyone pays 10 and receives 16: a net gain of 6.

The conflict is between individual incentives and collective returns under this setup. Change monitoring, repetition, group norms, sanctions, or the distribution of benefits, and behavior may change. The game describes a mechanism; it does not prove that everyone always free-rides.

## Institutions can change the game

Taxes, contracts, associations, negotiated rules, and community governance are different ways to organize contribution and use. Elinor Ostrom's research challenged the assumption that common resources must always be either centrally controlled or left unmanaged. Durable arrangements can depend on clear boundaries, locally appropriate rules, monitoring, conflict resolution, and the ability to adapt.[^1]

There is no guarantee that a local institution is inclusive or effective. Ask who can participate, who monitors the monitors, which users are excluded, and whether ecological or economic conditions have changed.

## Costs crossing a boundary

An **externality** is an effect on others that is not fully reflected in the decision-maker's incentives. Upstream pollution affects downstream residents. Coordination becomes harder when the people bearing costs have no vote in the institution making the decision. Moving a decision to a larger jurisdiction may internalize some effects while sacrificing local knowledge.

Why is an unregulated fish stock not the same type of collective-action problem as a broadcast warning signal?

:::answer Show the reasoning
Fish are rival: catching one reduces the stock available to others. Receiving a broadcast warning generally does not prevent another person receiving it. Both can involve exclusion problems, but rivalry changes the incentives and relevant rules.
