## Who gets the bridge?

Three towns share a river. Each wants a new bridge nearby, but the region can afford only one. Someone must decide where it goes, who pays, how the contract is awarded, and what happens if the builder fails.

The engineering question concerns a bridge that works. The economic question concerns costs and benefits. The political questions concern authority, representation, conflict, and accountability. Who gets a voice? Which interests count? Who can challenge the decision? Those questions remain even if everyone agrees on the engineering calculations.

**Political science** studies power, collective decisions, and the institutions that structure them. **Institutions** are durable rules and practices, formal or informal, that shape behavior. A written constitution is an institution; so are expectations about how officials actually use their powers.[^1]

## What this guide is for

The aim is to understand mechanisms. You will compare constitutions, electoral systems, parties, bureaucracies, courts, public services, and international arrangements. Numerical examples show how changing a rule changes outcomes. Research examples ask what evidence could distinguish one explanation from another.

Three kinds of question stay separate. A **descriptive** question asks what happens. A **causal** question asks why it happens or what an intervention changes. A **normative** question asks what ought to happen. Data can inform a choice between values; they cannot silently make that choice for us.

## A route through the subject

Start with methods and state capacity. Move through constitutional rules, voting, representation, and administration. Then examine collective-action problems, accountability, international relations, and policy evaluation. The reference chapters collect influential ideas and the terms needed to assess a study.

The [economics guide](#/economics) supplies tools for incentives and resource allocation. The [history guide](#/history) provides context for how institutions emerge and change. Neither removes the need to inspect who can exercise authority and how they can be held responsible.

:::key A useful institutional question
If the same people faced different rules, which actions would become easier, harder, more rewarding, or more costly?
:::
