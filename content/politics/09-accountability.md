## The report exists, but nothing happens

An audit documents overcharging on a public contract. The report is published, yet repayment never occurs and the practice continues. Transparency succeeded at revealing information. Accountability requires additional links: responsibility, authority to act, and a consequence that can actually be applied.

**Rule of law** concerns constraints on public and private power through publicly knowable legal rules and institutions. It is not simply the existence of many laws. **Rule by law** can describe the use of legal instruments as tools of power without comparable constraints on those who wield them.

## Several directions of oversight

**Vertical accountability** includes mechanisms by which the public can sanction officeholders, such as elections. **Horizontal accountability** involves institutions checking other institutions: courts, auditors, legislatures, and oversight bodies. Public-interest organizations and independent reporting can supply information needed by both.

No single mechanism is sufficient in every setting. A court may be independent but inaccessible. An auditor may be skilled but lack access to records. Elections may occur without giving voters meaningful alternatives or reliable information. Examine the chain rather than checking that an institution exists on paper.[^1]

## Independence and responsiveness

Judicial independence can protect decisions from immediate political pressure. Judges nevertheless interpret rules through appointment systems, procedures, professional norms, and review mechanisms. The design problem is how to protect impartial adjudication while maintaining lawful accountability and effective access, not how to place every actor beyond scrutiny.

**Due process** concerns fair procedures for decisions affecting people. Specific legal requirements vary by jurisdiction. As an institutional principle, consider notice, an opportunity to respond, reasons for decisions, and review. This chapter explains those functions rather than advising on any particular case.

## Detection changes the data

Suppose detected procurement violations rise from 20 to 60 after the audit budget triples. Corruption may have increased, detection may have improved, or both. A cleaner indicator might estimate violations in comparable randomly selected contracts using a stable procedure. Administrative totals are shaped by enforcement effort.

Oversight also has costs. Extensive review can slow ordinary decisions. The relevant comparison asks whether the design reduces serious errors and abuse relative to its delays, costs, and effects on discretion—not whether oversight is always beneficial or always obstructive.

### Information has to reach someone able to act

Suppose an audit reveals that a public project paid for materials that were not delivered. Publishing the report supplies information. Accountability additionally requires an institution capable of investigating, establishing responsibility, correcting the loss where possible, and applying appropriate consequences through a credible process.

If a report is unreadable, difficult to find, or released after a decision can no longer be changed, formal disclosure may have limited practical value. If an oversight body lacks authority or resources, excellent information may still lead to little action.

### More detected problems can mean better detection

Imagine a new inspection system reports twice as many violations as the old one. That might reflect more misconduct, more thorough detection, changed definitions, or a combination. A raw violation count cannot distinguish those explanations.

Measure inspection intensity, case selection, definitions, and follow-up alongside detected violations. An institution that reports few problems may be performing well, or it may be looking less effectively. Comparing organizations without accounting for these differences can reward weak monitoring.

### Independence and responsibility both matter

Oversight requires enough independence to investigate powerful actors. The oversight body also needs procedures governing its own conduct, evidence standards, and opportunities to challenge mistakes. Independence is not the absence of all accountability; it is protection from inappropriate interference within a defined role.

Rule-of-law institutions aim to make the exercise of authority governed by public rules and procedures rather than arbitrary personal decisions. Actual performance must be assessed through access, consistency, enforcement, and remedies, not simply the presence of a formal provision.

The transferable lesson is to follow the complete chain: discover information, interpret it, assign responsibility, act through a legitimate process, and check whether the underlying problem changes.
