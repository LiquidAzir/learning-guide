## Who gets invited into the room?

A citizens' assembly recruits volunteers and selects a smaller panel to deliberate. The organizers want demographic representation and a fair chance for each volunteer. Those goals can conflict when groups volunteer at different rates.

**Sortition** is selection by lot. It does not automatically make every stage of recruitment representative. A carefully designed selection algorithm can improve fairness within the volunteer pool while leaving the initial decision to volunteer, the agenda, the evidence presented, and the implementation of recommendations as separate problems.

Research on fair assembly selection formalizes that trade-off and tests algorithms on real recruitment datasets.[^1] The mathematical guarantee concerns the selection objective and assumptions. It does not establish that every assembly produces good policy.

## Participation needs an outcome

Participatory budgeting gives residents a role in allocating some public funds. Evaluate who participates, what authority they possess, how proposals are screened, and whether the allocation is implemented. Participation in a consultation and control over a budget are different interventions.

A field experiment in China found changes in civic engagement after communities were invited into participatory budgeting. Its specific institutional setting and measured outcomes belong beside the finding; it is not a general ranking of political systems.[^2]

## A feed for learning

Every entry in this subject's [research feed](#/politics/research) must identify a lasting institutional question, a research method, a result or clearly labeled proposal, and a limitation. Primary studies, transparent datasets, and substantive research reports can qualify. A preprint remains labeled preliminary.

Campaign claims, daily polls, scandals, personality coverage, party press releases, and opinion columns are outside its purpose. Selection follows research quality and learning value, not whether a conclusion flatters a particular side. A null result, failed replication, or correction can be especially useful if it changes what we should believe about a mechanism.

## Compare before generalizing

Ask which people, rules, resources, and time period generated the result. Look for studies that vary a mechanism in another setting. When two studies disagree, check their treatment, comparison, outcome, and implementation before concluding that one must be dishonest.

### Who had a chance to enter the pool?

Suppose organizers invite a sample of residents to a citizens' assembly. Some cannot attend because of work, care responsibilities, transport, language, or distrust. A panel-selection algorithm can distribute chances fairly among volunteers while the volunteer pool remains unrepresentative of the wider population.

Compensation, accessible scheduling, translation, and recruitment design can change who is able to participate. Those choices occur before the algorithm draws a panel. A guarantee about selection probabilities should therefore state the population to which it applies.

### Formal fairness and useful deliberation differ

An algorithm might satisfy demographic quotas while maximizing a defined measure of equality in selection chances. That is a mathematical achievement under its assumptions. It does not establish that participants receive balanced information, deliberate effectively, or influence decisions afterward.

To assess those outcomes, study the briefing materials, facilitation, opportunities to speak, handling of disagreement, and institutional response to recommendations. A well-selected panel can still be ignored, and a widely discussed recommendation can still lack a clear route to implementation.

### Decide what would count as an effect

If participation changes attitudes after six months, that is a different result from changing a budget, improving a service, or increasing trust across an entire population. A study should identify whose attitudes or actions were measured and what comparison supports a causal interpretation.

Rotating assemblies add a time dimension. Equal chances in one selection round do not automatically imply fair participation across many rounds, especially if membership and eligibility change. Temporal selection models study those rules; field evaluation must still examine how the resulting institutions operate.

This is the standard for the feed: explain what the research actually establishes, identify the next missing link, and preserve the distinction between an interesting institutional proposal and a demonstrated improvement in public outcomes.

:::try Representative volunteers?
An algorithm gives every volunteer an equal chance of selection. Does that guarantee the resulting assembly represents the entire population?

:::answer Show the reasoning
No. Volunteers may differ from non-volunteers, and random samples still vary. Equal selection within a pool is one fairness property. Population representation also depends on recruitment, participation, sampling design, and the dimensions being represented.
