## The proposal needs three signatures

A housing reform has public support but requires agreement from a legislature, an executive, and regional governments. Failure may reflect deliberate checks, conflicting preferences, unclear responsibility, or an institution designed for a different problem. “The system is slow” identifies a symptom; the next step is to map its decision points.

A **constitution** establishes fundamental rules about authority, selection, procedures, and rights. Some rules are entrenched in a single text; others depend on statutes, precedents, and conventions. Written rules and actual practice must be studied separately.

## Separate offices, different incentives

In a **parliamentary system**, the executive normally depends on legislative confidence. In a **presidential system**, an independently selected executive typically serves a fixed term subject to specified removal procedures. Actual systems vary, and labels alone do not explain how parties, courts, and administrative organizations operate.

**Separation of powers** allocates responsibilities among institutions. **Checks and balances** give institutions ways to restrain one another. They can reduce unilateral action while also making responsibility harder to identify.

A **veto player** is an actor whose agreement is required for a change. Adding veto players can protect against changes some people regard as harmful. It can also preserve harmful arrangements. Whether stability is desirable depends partly on what is being stabilized and whose interests are represented.[^1]

## Local knowledge and shared problems

**Federalism** constitutionally distributes authority across levels of government. **Decentralization** more broadly transfers responsibilities or resources downward. They are not synonyms, and the written allocation may differ from who controls the money.

Local decisions can use local knowledge and accommodate different preferences. But a river, epidemic, or transport network crosses boundaries. A locality may underinvest in benefits enjoyed elsewhere or shift costs onto neighbors. Shared funding and coordination can address spillovers while introducing additional negotiation and monitoring problems.

## Trace a decision

For the bridge example, draw who proposes, funds, authorizes, implements, reviews, and can block the project. Then ask whether voters or affected residents can identify responsibility. This often reveals more than ranking systems by a single label.

A reform adds a second chamber's approval to every infrastructure decision. What are one plausible benefit and one plausible cost?

The extra approval may protect represented regions or catch poor proposals. It may also delay beneficial projects or preserve the status quo. Which effect dominates depends on representation, preferences, procedures, and the quality of existing decisions.
